# Basketball

Jumping, landing and cutting on a hard floor, in a population that is tall and
frequently young. The landing is the question.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A basketball history tests how the knee was loaded during jumping and landing,
which is the sport's characteristic demand. 3.15.5 covers the mechanics.

Landing from a jump with the knee collapsing inwards is a classic anterior
cruciate mechanism, and it happens without contact.

Repeated jumping also produces the commonest overuse presentation in the
sport, which is patellar tendinopathy. 4.16.3 covers it.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether the injury happened on landing, on a cut, or on contact.

Ask whether they landed on one leg or two, because single leg landing is the
higher risk pattern.

Ask about jumping volume and whether it has recently increased, for the
overuse presentations.

Ask about the floor and the footwear, and about how old the shoes are.

Ask about growth in an adolescent player, since a recent growth spurt changes
both the differential and the loading. 4.19.1 covers growth plate assessment.

### What a Positive Means

A non-contact landing with valgus collapse, a pop and immediate swelling is an
anterior cruciate rupture, and this is the mechanism most studied in
prevention work. 3.7.12 covers loading during landing.

Pain below the patella, worse with jumping and after sitting, is patellar
tendinopathy.

Anterior pain in an adolescent with tenderness over the tibial tubercle is an
apophysitis. 4.16.4 covers it.

A twisting cut with slower swelling fits a meniscal tear. 4.2.18 covers
twisting injuries.

An ankle injury with knee pain is common in this sport and the knee may be
compensating rather than injured.

### Key Learning Points

- Landing with the knee collapsing inwards is a classic non-contact cruciate mechanism.
- Single leg landing is the higher risk pattern.
- Repeated jumping produces patellar tendinopathy, the commonest overuse presentation here.
- In an adolescent, a recent growth spurt changes the differential.
- An ankle injury with knee pain may mean the knee is compensating.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is from the landing mechanics to the loaded structures.

Valgus collapse on landing loads the anterior cruciate and the medial
structures together, which is why the mechanism produces combined injuries.

Repeated eccentric extensor loading produces tendon pathology rather than
ligament rupture, which is the overuse half of the differential.

The prevention literature on landing mechanics is worth knowing and worth
reading carefully: screening for frontal plane landing has not predicted
injury consistently while the training it selects for does reduce injury
rates. 3.14.8 sets out that pair.

That distinction matters when counselling an athlete, because the honest
message is that everybody benefits from the training rather than that the
screening identifies who needs it.

### How Good It Is

The basketball history performs well for the mechanism, because landing and
cutting are distinct and well recalled.

It performs well for separating the acute from the overuse presentation, which
is the main division.

It performs poorly for predicting who will be injured, which is the screening
problem above.

The jumping volume question is the most useful for the overuse presentations
and is rarely asked.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus collapse on landing loads the cruciate and the medial structures together.
- Repeated eccentric extensor loading gives tendon pathology rather than rupture.
- Frontal plane landing screening has not consistently predicted injury.
- The training it selects for does reduce injury rates, which is a different claim.
- The honest message is that everybody benefits from the training.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the basketball history matters for return risk and for the
contralateral knee.

A young athlete returning to jumping and cutting carries a reinjury risk and a
risk to the other knee, and both should be stated. 4.21.7 covers return to
sport testing.

Height and age matter in the conversation: a tall, young, high level player
has a long exposure ahead of them.

A patellar tendinopathy in a competitive player is a load and rehabilitation
problem and surgery is a late and uncertain option, which should be said
early.

The return target is set by the specific demand: single leg landing and
deceleration, rather than by a general fitness measure. 4.21.2 covers the
single leg hop.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating patellar tendinopathy as a structural problem
and imaging it repeatedly, when the intervention is a progressive loading
programme.

The second is clearing an athlete to return on time rather than on criteria,
which is a decision the history frames by establishing what they intend to do.
4.21.8 covers return to play decisions.

The third is missing the ankle and hip contribution to a landing problem.

And in an adolescent, attributing anterior pain to the patellofemoral joint
when it is an apophysitis changes the management and the prognosis. 4.16.4
covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- A young athlete returning carries a risk to both knees, and both should be stated.
- Patellar tendinopathy is a load and rehabilitation problem; surgery is late and uncertain.
- Set the return target by the specific demand rather than by general fitness.
- Clearing on time rather than on criteria is a recognisable error.
- In an adolescent, an apophysitis is not a patellofemoral problem.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the basketball player presents after a reconstruction
with persistent difficulty landing or cutting, and the history separates
confidence from structure.

A player with a stable knee who cannot land confidently has a rehabilitation
and psychological problem, and that group is larger than the structural
failures. 4.22.6 covers the relevant instrument.

A player with giving way on cutting raises an unaddressed rotational
component. 4.14.3 covers the pattern.

A player with anterior pain after a patellar tendon graft reconstruction
raises the harvest site, and the history is pain at the harvest site with
kneeling rather than joint pain. 3.7.18 covers the graft.

A chronic patellar tendinopathy that has had a procedure and is unchanged
usually had a loading problem that the procedure did not address.

### Key Learning Points

- A stable knee that cannot land confidently is a rehabilitation and psychological problem.
- That group is larger than the structural failures.
- Giving way on cutting raises an unaddressed rotational component.
- Anterior pain after a patellar tendon graft is often the harvest site, worse with kneeling.
