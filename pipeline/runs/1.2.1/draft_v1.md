# Bones of the Knee

Four bones meet at or beside the knee: the femur, the tibia, the patella and
the fibula.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

Four bones are involved. The femur is the thigh bone, the longest bone in your
body. It ends in two rounded knuckles called condyles.

The tibia is the shin bone. Its top is broad and almost flat, like a table
top, and the rounded end of the femur sits on it.

The patella is the kneecap. It is a small bone that sits inside the big tendon
at the front, and it slides in a groove on the femur as you bend and
straighten.

The fibula is the thin bone on the outside of your shin. It does not take much
weight. It gives things somewhere to attach.

### Function

A round thing on a flat thing is not a stable arrangement, and that is exactly
what the femur on the tibia is. The bones alone would slide apart. Everything
else in the knee exists to stop that happening.

The kneecap has a cleverer job. By sitting in front of the joint it holds the
thigh tendon further away from the centre of the knee, which makes the muscle
more effective. It works like moving your hand further down a spanner.

### Key Learning Points

- Four bones: femur, tibia, patella and fibula.
- The femur ends in two rounded condyles; the top of the tibia is almost flat.
- The patella sits inside the tendon at the front and slides in a groove.
- Bones alone would slide apart, so the soft tissues provide the stability.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

The femur is the thigh bone. Its lower end widens into two rounded
prominences, the medial and lateral condyles, separated at the back by a notch
and joined at the front by a groove called the trochlea.

The tibia is the shin bone and takes nearly all the weight passing through the
leg. Its upper surface, the tibial plateau, is broad and only gently dished.

The patella is the kneecap, the largest sesamoid bone in the body, meaning a
bone formed within a tendon. Its back surface is covered in thick cartilage
and glides in the trochlear groove.

The fibula lies on the outer side of the shin. It carries little weight but
anchors ligaments and tendons on that side.

### Function

The bones set the geometry and little else. Because the femoral condyles are
rounded and the tibial plateau is nearly flat, the surfaces do not lock
together, which is what allows the wide range of movement.

The patella increases the mechanical advantage of the thigh muscle. Without
it, straightening the knee against resistance would take considerably more
force.

### Clinical Relevance

The shape of these bones explains several everyday findings. A shallow
trochlear groove allows the kneecap to track poorly or slip out of place.
Differences in condyle shape and in the slope of the tibial plateau affect how
the joint is loaded and how it behaves after injury.

The ends of these bones are also where pain is often felt and where swelling
collects, which is why clinicians press along specific bony landmarks during
an examination.

Fractures of these bones are covered in the conditions section rather than
here.

### Key Learning Points

- The femur ends in two condyles with a trochlear groove at the front.
- The tibial plateau is broad and only gently dished, so it does not grip the femur.
- The patella is the largest sesamoid bone and increases quadriceps mechanical advantage.
- The fibula carries little weight but anchors structures on the outer side.
- Bony shape explains kneecap tracking and how load is distributed.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is the kneecap really a separate bone?
Yes. It forms within the quadriceps tendon and is the largest bone of that
type in the body. It is held in place by the tendon above and below it.

**Q.** Which bone takes the weight?
The tibia. The fibula carries only a small proportion, which is why it can be
used as a graft elsewhere in the body.

**Q.** Why can I feel bony lumps around my knee?
Several landmarks sit close to the skin: the condyles at the sides, the tibial
tubercle at the front below the kneecap, and the head of the fibula on the
outer side. Feeling them is normal.

**Q.** Can you be born with an unusual kneecap?
Yes. Variations in kneecap shape and in the depth of the groove are common and
often cause no trouble at all.

Seek assessment if you cannot bear weight after an injury, if the knee is
deformed, or if there is severe pain over a bony point after a fall.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The distal femur expands into medial and lateral condyles, separated
posteriorly by the intercondylar notch and continuous anteriorly with the
trochlear groove. The medial condyle extends further distally, which
compensates for the oblique femoral shaft and keeps the joint line roughly
horizontal in stance.

The proximal tibia presents medial and lateral plateaus separated by the
intercondylar eminence. The medial plateau is larger and concave; the lateral
is smaller and convex in the sagittal plane, which has implications for
translation during flexion. The plateau slopes posteriorly.

The patella is triangular with a proximal base and distal apex. Its posterior
surface bears the thickest articular cartilage in the body, divided by a
vertical ridge into medial and lateral facets.

The fibular head articulates with the posterolateral tibia at the proximal
tibiofibular joint and provides attachment for the lateral collateral ligament
and the biceps femoris tendon.

### Function

Articular incongruity between convex femoral condyles and a relatively flat
tibial plateau permits rolling, gliding and rotation, at the cost of bony
stability. The intercondylar notch houses the cruciate ligaments, and its
dimensions have been studied in relation to cruciate injury.

The patella acts as an anatomical pulley, displacing the quadriceps tendon
anteriorly and increasing its moment arm about the flexion axis, with the gain
greatest in terminal extension.

### Clinical Relevance

Trochlear morphology governs patellar tracking, and a shallow or dysplastic
groove predisposes to maltracking and instability. Posterior tibial slope
influences anteroposterior translation and is of interest in cruciate injury
and in surgical planning.

Palpable landmarks structure the clinical examination: the joint line, the
femoral epicondyles, the tibial tubercle and the fibular head. Each
corresponds to attachments examined in the following pages.

Fracture patterns and their management belong to the conditions and surgery
sections.

### Key Learning Points

- The medial femoral condyle extends further distally, offsetting the oblique femoral shaft.
- The medial tibial plateau is concave and larger; the lateral is convex in the sagittal plane.
- The patella bears the thickest articular cartilage in the body, on two facets.
- The patella acts as a pulley, increasing the quadriceps moment arm, most in extension.
- Trochlear morphology governs patellar tracking; posterior slope influences translation.
