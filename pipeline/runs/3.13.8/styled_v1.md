# Alignment and OA

Alignment drives progression and is also changed by it. The direction of
causation is the hard part, and it runs both ways.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Two things can each cause the other. A knee that is bow legged wears on the
inside, and a knee worn on the inside becomes more bow legged.

Once that happens, the problem accelerates, because each step makes the other
worse.

Breaking the loop early is easier than breaking it late, which is why timing
matters more than it first appears.

### At the Knee

Varus loads the inner compartment, and the inner compartment is where wear
usually starts. 3.13.3 covers varus.

As cartilage and then bone are lost on that side, the leg becomes more bow
legged. 1.2.2 covers cartilage.

The increased deformity increases the load, and the loop closes. 6.73 covers
medial compartment disease.

The same applies on the other side, with valgus and the outer compartment, and
it is less common. 3.13.4 covers valgus.

### What Changes It

The alignment the leg started with. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

Body weight, which scales the load. 3.5.7 covers obesity.

Previous meniscal loss or injury, which removes a load spreader. 3.5.5 covers
it.

Activity, which decides how many loading cycles the joint sees.

### Clinical Relevance

It explains why osteoarthritis in the knee is usually one sided rather than
even. 6.71 covers the introduction.

It is the argument for intervening while one compartment is still intact.

It is also why a severe deformity is less correctable: much of it is now
inside the joint. 3.13.10 covers the principles.

5.3.4 covers how osteoarthritis looks on imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- Varus causes medial wear and medial wear causes more varus.
- Once the loop closes the problem accelerates.
- Cartilage then bone loss on one side increases the deformity.
- It explains why knee osteoarthritis is usually one sided.
- A severe deformity is less correctable because much of it is inside the joint.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Alignment is both a risk factor for progression and an outcome of it, which
makes the epidemiology harder than it looks.

A cross sectional association between varus and medial disease cannot separate
the two directions. Only longitudinal data can.

Where longitudinal data exists, it supports alignment as a predictor of
progression rather than of incidence, which is a meaningful distinction.

### At the Knee

Baseline varus predicts medial compartment progression in longitudinal
cohorts, with the effect size rising with the degree of deformity.

The adduction moment predicts more strongly than the static angle, which
points at load as the mechanism. 3.13.7 covers it.

Evidence for alignment predicting the first appearance of disease is weaker
than for predicting its progression.

Deformity acquired through joint space loss adds an intra-articular component
that no extra-articular surgery addresses. 3.13.6 covers it.

Meniscal extrusion and subchondral change accompany the process and contribute
to the measured deformity. 3.5.5 covers meniscal loss.

### What Changes It

Baseline alignment, which is the exposure in question. 3.13.5 covers its
origin.

Body mass, which is the strongest modifiable risk factor and acts through
load. 3.5.7 covers obesity.

Meniscal status, which changes load distribution independently of alignment.
3.5.5 covers it.

Previous injury, which starts the process at a site load alone would not have
chosen. 6.77 covers post-traumatic disease.

Muscle strength and gait, which change the moment without changing the angle.
3.13.7 covers them.

### How It Is Measured

Standing long leg radiography at baseline and follow up, which is how cohort
studies measure it. 5.3.2 covers the technique.

Radiographic grading of joint space, which is the usual progression endpoint
and is insensitive early. 5.3.4 covers it.

Magnetic resonance imaging of cartilage and bone marrow, which detects change
earlier and has no agreed threshold. 5.11 covers it.

Gait analysis for the adduction moment, which gives the mechanism and is
confined to research. 3.16.7 covers it.

Separating cause from consequence requires a baseline measurement taken before
disease, which most cohorts do not have.

### Clinical Relevance

Distinguish alignment as a progression risk from alignment as a cause of
incident disease, since the evidence differs.

Apportion the measured deformity between bone and joint, because only the
first part is correctable. 3.13.6 covers it.

Address body mass alongside alignment, since it is the stronger modifiable
factor. 3.5.7 covers it.

Avoid reading a cross sectional association as a causal direction. 3.13.7
covers the mechanism.

### Key Learning Points

- Alignment is both a risk factor for progression and an outcome of it.
- Cross sectional data cannot separate the two directions.
- Baseline varus predicts progression more clearly than it predicts incidence.
- The adduction moment predicts more strongly, which points at load as the mechanism.
- Acquired deformity includes an intra-articular component no osteotomy addresses.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The careful statement is that alignment predicts progression of established
disease, that its role in incident disease is weaker, and that the loop makes
both hard to measure.

The practical consequence is that alignment correction has its best rationale
in the knee with a deformity and a preserved opposite compartment, which is a
narrow window. 3.13.10 covers the targets.

A severe varus knee has most of its deformity inside the joint, so an
extra-articular correction can restore the axis without restoring the joint.
3.13.6 covers obliquity.

Correcting alignment after that point redistributes load onto a compartment
that may also be worn, which is the reason for assessing the opposite side
before correcting. 6.74 covers lateral disease.

Conventional teaching has kept alignment as a central factor while accepting
that the mechanical and the biological accounts of progression are both
incomplete. 6.154 covers the controversies.

### What Changes It

How much of the deformity is bony against intra-articular, which decides what
correction achieves.

The state of the opposite compartment, which decides whether load can be moved
there. 5.11 covers cartilage imaging.

Body mass and gait, which act on the moment rather than the angle. 3.13.7
covers them.

Meniscal and ligament status, which change the compartment's tolerance. 3.5.5
covers meniscal loss.

The patient's age and expected remaining demand, which decide whether a joint
preserving strategy makes sense at all. 6.76 covers the young patient.

### How It Is Measured

Longitudinal cohort data with baseline alignment, which is the only design
that can address causation and is scarce.

Joint space measurement, which is the regulatory endpoint and a poor proxy for
cartilage health. 5.3.4 covers it.

Compositional magnetic resonance imaging, which detects earlier change and
lacks a threshold. 5.11 covers it.

Gait analysis, which measures the mechanism and does not appear in the large
cohorts.

The mechanical and the biological literatures use different endpoints, which
is part of why they have not converged.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess the opposite compartment before correcting alignment, because the
correction moves load onto it. 5.11 covers the imaging.

Apportion the deformity and state the apportionment, since an extra-articular
correction cannot address an intra-articular loss.

Offer weight reduction and gait modification as part of the mechanical plan
rather than instead of it. 3.13.7 covers them.

Describe alignment as a predictor of progression rather than as a cause of the
disease, which is what the evidence supports.

Take the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations into the decision,
because the window in which correction is rational is narrow and the operation
is large.

### Key Learning Points

- Alignment predicts progression of established disease; its role in incidence is weaker.
- Correction has its best rationale with a deformity and a preserved opposite compartment.
- A severe varus knee carries most of its deformity inside the joint.
- Correcting late moves load onto a compartment that may also be worn.
- The mechanical and biological literatures use different endpoints and have not converged.
