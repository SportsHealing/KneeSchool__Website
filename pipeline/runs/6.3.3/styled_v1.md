# Tibial Avulsion

A bone injury wearing a ligament's name, and the one pattern in this chapter a
radiograph can diagnose.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

Sometimes the ACL does not tear at all. Instead it pulls off a piece of the shin
bone where it attaches. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

The ligament is intact. What has failed is the bone it was anchored to. 6.3.2
covers the mid-substance tear.

This happens more in children than in adults, because growing bone is weaker
than the ligament attached to it. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

### Investigations and Imaging

Because a piece of bone has moved, this is the one ACL pattern that can show up
on an ordinary x-ray. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

### Key Learning Points

- Sometimes the ligament pulls off a piece of shin bone instead of tearing.
- The ligament itself is intact.
- The bone it was anchored to is what failed.
- This is commoner in children than in adults.
- It is the one pattern an ordinary x-ray can show.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

A tibial spine avulsion means the ligament has pulled its bony attachment off
the top of the shin bone. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

The ligament is intact and attached to that fragment, so the problem is a
displaced piece of bone rather than a torn ligament. 6.3.2 covers the
mid-substance tear.

It is commoner in children, where the growing bone fails before the ligament
does. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

### How It Presents

The knee swells rapidly, as with any ACL injury. 6.2.5 covers acute
haemarthrosis.

A displaced fragment can block the knee from straightening fully, which is a
mechanical problem rather than a painful one. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Management Overview

Because the ligament is intact, the treatment is to put the bone back and hold
it rather than to replace the ligament. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

An undisplaced fragment may be treated without an operation. A displaced one is
usually fixed. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

The outlook once the bone has healed is generally better than for a torn
ligament, because the patient keeps their own ACL. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament pulls its bony attachment off the shin bone.
- The ligament itself is intact and attached to the fragment.
- It is commoner in children.
- The treatment is to put the bone back rather than replace the ligament.
- The outlook is generally better, because the patient keeps their own ACL.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is this the same as a torn ACL?
No. The ligament is intact. What has happened is that its anchor point has come
away with a piece of bone, so the repair is a bone operation rather than a
ligament one.

**Q.** Why does it happen to children and not adults?
Because in a growing skeleton the bone at the attachment is weaker than the
ligament. In an adult the ligament usually fails first. It is the same force
finding a different weakest link.

**Q.** Will the knee be normal afterwards?
Often, yes, once the bone has united. Some knees are left with slight looseness
or with difficulty straightening fully, and those are the two things followed up
afterwards.

**Q.** Does the knee need an operation?
It depends on whether the fragment has moved. An undisplaced one can often be
treated in a cast or brace. A displaced one is usually fixed, partly for the
ligament's tension and partly because it can block full extension.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

A tibial eminence avulsion detaches the ACL's tibial footprint with a fragment
of bone from the intercondylar eminence. 2.7.7 covers the tibial footprint.

It is characteristic of the skeletally immature knee, where the physis and the
cancellous eminence fail before the ligamentous substance does. 6.7.2 covers
physeal injuries.

Classification is by displacement, from undisplaced through hinged to completely
displaced, and displacement drives treatment. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature
patients.

### Investigations and Imaging

Radiographs identify the fragment and are the reason this pattern is diagnosed
without cross sectional imaging. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Magnetic resonance imaging adds the associated injuries and the ligament's own
condition, which displacement alone does not report. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL
injury.

An entrapped intermeniscal ligament or meniscus can block reduction, which is a
finding the scan contributes and the radiograph cannot. 6.4.3 covers meniscal
root tears.

### Management Overview

Undisplaced fragments are immobilised; displaced fragments are reduced and
fixed. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Fixation technique is chosen with the physis in mind, which is what distinguishes
this from an adult fracture of the same shape. 6.7.3 covers reconstruction
strategies.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL's tibial footprint detaches with a fragment of eminence.
- The immature skeleton fails at the bone before the ligament.
- Classification is by displacement and displacement drives treatment.
- Radiographs diagnose it; the scan adds the company and the block.
- An entrapped meniscus can prevent reduction.

## MRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer separates this from a cruciate rupture in the
first sentence.

The expected points are the intact ligament, the bony failure, the paediatric
predominance, the displacement based classification, and the possibility of a
block to reduction. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Say that a radiograph makes the diagnosis and the scan changes the plan. 5.7.4
covers acute ACL injury.

Say that the ligament is often lax even after anatomical union, so a good
radiograph does not promise a stable knee. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the
ACL.

### Management Overview

Reduce and fix a displaced fragment, and look for the soft tissue that may be
preventing reduction before attributing it to the fragment's shape. 6.4.3 covers
meniscal root tears.

Preserve the physis, which constrains implant choice and position. 6.7.3 covers
reconstruction strategies.

### Complications and Prognosis

Loss of extension from a malreduced or prominent fragment is the characteristic
complication and is mechanical. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Residual laxity despite union is the second, and it is explained by the
ligament having been stretched before the bone failed. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Arthrofibrosis follows immobilisation and is the third. 6.7.1 covers skeletally
immature patients.

### Key Learning Points

- Separate this from a cruciate rupture in the first sentence.
- A radiograph makes the diagnosis and the scan changes the plan.
- Look for a soft tissue block before blaming the fragment's shape.
- Union does not promise a stable knee.
- Loss of extension, residual laxity and arthrofibrosis are the three
  complications.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to treat this as a paediatric
fracture with a ligament attached, and to say so.

The physis governs implant choice and position, so an adult fixation strategy
applied unchanged is the error the question is testing for. 6.7.3 covers
reconstruction strategies.

Anatomical reduction matters for extension as much as for stability, because a
prominent fragment blocks the notch. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Early motion after secure fixation is what prevents the stiffness that
immobilisation produces, which makes fixation stability a functional decision
rather than a technical preference. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Complications and Prognosis

Residual anterior laxity after union is common and is usually asymptomatic,
which is the finding most likely to be over-treated. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Extension loss is the complication that reliably troubles patients and is the
one worth operating to prevent. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is which fixation construct best balances stability
against physeal risk. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The second is whether residual laxity after union needs any treatment in an
asymptomatic adolescent. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The third is whether arthroscopic and open reduction differ in outcome once
reduction quality is matched. 6.7.3 covers reconstruction strategies.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- This is a paediatric fracture with a ligament attached.
- An adult fixation strategy applied unchanged is the error.
- Anatomical reduction matters for extension as much as for stability.
- Early motion after secure fixation prevents the characteristic stiffness.
- Residual laxity is usually asymptomatic and most likely to be over-treated.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this injury is instructive because the radiographic result
and the clinical result come apart, and the radiograph is the one everybody
looks at.

A united, anatomically reduced eminence with a lax knee is a common outcome, and
it happens because the ligament was elongated before the bone gave way. 6.1.2
covers the functions of the ACL.

That laxity is frequently asymptomatic in an adolescent who does not pivot, and
becomes symptomatic years later when they do, which puts the presentation
outside the usual follow up window. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The useful practice is to record the laxity at discharge rather than the
radiograph alone, so that a later presentation can be interpreted. 6.1.4 covers
the natural history.

The fellow's error to avoid is reassuring from the film, because the film
reports the part of this injury that healed. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature
patients.

### Key Learning Points

- The radiographic and clinical results come apart.
- A united eminence with a lax knee is a common outcome.
- The ligament was elongated before the bone gave way.
- The laxity often declares years later, outside the follow up window.
- Record the laxity at discharge, not the radiograph alone.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant judgement is about what to tell a family when the operation has
gone well and the knee is not entirely normal.

Explaining residual laxity at the outset is harder than explaining it later and
is considerably better, because a family told afterwards hears a complication
where there was a known possibility. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Whether to act on asymptomatic laxity in a child is the live decision, and
acting commits a growing knee to a reconstruction it may never have needed.
6.7.3 covers reconstruction strategies.

The five things decide it. The patient's age and physiology, which here means
growth remaining and therefore what any reconstruction would have to respect.
Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which in an
adolescent are a prediction rather than a fact and change quickly. Their
psychological state, and in a child that of the family as well. And their own
ideas, concerns and expectations, which in paediatric practice are usually the
parents' until they are not.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and record growth remaining
because it dates every later option. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Explain residual laxity at the outset rather than afterwards.
- Told late, a known possibility is heard as a complication.
- Acting on asymptomatic laxity commits a growing knee to an operation.
- Demand in an adolescent is a prediction rather than a fact.
- Record growth remaining, because it dates every later option.
