# Hamstring Tendinopathy

Pain at the back of the knee that is not the joint, and the tendons that are
hardest to reach in the examination.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The hamstring tendons cross the back of the knee: the medial group inserting
below the medial joint line and the biceps femoris laterally to the fibular
head. 2.10.5 covers the biceps.

Examining them tests for tendinopathy at or near those insertions, which
presents as posterior or posteromedial knee pain that is frequently attributed
to the joint. 4.6.9 covers posterior palpation.

They matter in a knee examination because the back of the knee is the part most
often not examined, and these are among the findings that live there.
4.6.9 covers that argument.

### How It Is Performed

With the patient prone or with the knee flexed, palpate the medial hamstring
tendons as they pass behind the medial side of the knee to their insertions.
4.6.9 covers the technique.

Palpate the biceps femoris laterally and follow it to the fibular head, noting
that the iliotibial band lies adjacent and is a separate structure. 4.6.8
covers the fibular head.

Ask the patient to flex the knee against resistance and note whether that
reproduces the pain, which localises it to the hamstrings.

Repeat with the knee in different flexion angles, since a tendon's tenderness
changes with tension. 4.6.6 covers tendon tenderness.

Assess the joint line and the posterior joint line separately, because a
posterior horn tear produces pain in the same region. 4.6.4 covers joint line
tenderness.

### What a Positive Means

Tenderness along a hamstring tendon with painful resisted flexion indicates a
hamstring problem rather than a joint one.

Tenderness at the medial insertion below the joint line, with a normal joint
line, points at the medial hamstrings or the pes insertion. 4.16.7 covers the
pes.

Tenderness at the fibular head insertion raises the biceps and, with an injury
history, a corner problem, and the nerve is examined. 4.6.8 covers that
argument.

Posterior joint line tenderness with a normal hamstring examination points at
the meniscus instead. 4.6.9 covers that separation.

Tenderness at the ischial origin rather than near the knee is a proximal
hamstring problem and belongs to a different examination. 4.2.15 covers
referred pain.

### Key Learning Points

- The medial group inserts below the medial joint line and the biceps to the fibular head.
- These are among the findings that live in the part of the knee least often examined.
- Painful resisted flexion localises the pain to the hamstrings.
- Tenderness at the fibular head insertion raises the biceps and prompts a nerve examination.
- Posterior joint line tenderness with a normal hamstring examination points at the meniscus.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is a good answer to posterior knee pain with a normal joint, and
the resisted flexion is the localising step.

The expected points are the prone or flexed position, the palpation of the
medial group and the biceps separately, the resisted flexion, the different
flexion angles, and the separate assessment of the joint line.

Say that you would examine the posterior joint line separately, since a
posterior horn tear is the main alternative. 4.6.4 covers it.

Say that you would examine the popliteal fossa, since a cyst and a mass both
present as posterior knee pain. 4.6.10 covers it.

Say that you would assess the hamstring flexibility and the lumbar spine, since
both contribute to posterior thigh and knee symptoms. 4.1.4 covers the
structured approach.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a tendon's pain is reproduced by loading it, so
resisted flexion separates a hamstring source from a joint source in the same
region. 4.6.6 covers tendon tenderness.

The medial hamstrings and the pes anserinus insert close together, which is why
their tenderness overlaps and why the pes is a separate page. 4.16.7 covers it.

The biceps femoris inserts into the fibular head alongside the lateral
collateral ligament, which is why tenderness there has a wider differential and
why the nerve is examined. 2.10.6 covers the nerve.

The hamstrings also act as a dynamic restraint to anterior tibial translation,
which is why they are relevant in a cruciate deficient knee beyond their own
pathology. 4.11.3 covers a test built on muscle action.

### How Good It Is

The examination localises a hamstring problem reasonably well when the resisted
flexion reproduces the pain, and poorly when it does not.

The tendons are harder to reach than the anterior structures, so the palpation is
less precise than at the patella. 4.6.5 covers that contrast.

It cannot separate tendinopathy from a partial tear, which imaging does, and in
the hamstrings that distinction matters for return to sport. 4.1.6 covers that
relationship.

Its main value is excluding the joint as the source in a patient with posterior
pain, which is a real contribution. 4.6.9 covers the examination it belongs to.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Loading a tendon reproduces its pain, which separates a hamstring from a joint source.
- The medial hamstrings and the pes insert close together, so their tenderness overlaps.
- The biceps inserts alongside the lateral collateral ligament, which widens the differential.
- The hamstrings are a dynamic restraint to anterior translation beyond their own pathology.
- Its main value is excluding the joint as the source of posterior pain.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this diagnosis is a non surgical one, and the examination's
contribution is keeping the knee out of an arthroscopy.

A patient with posterior pain, a normal joint and a painful resisted flexion has
a hamstring problem managed by load, and investigating the joint adds nothing.
4.6.9 covers the examination.

A patient with biceps tenderness at the fibular head and an injury history needs
the corner assessed, which is a different and more consequential pathway.
4.13.4 covers it.

An athlete with a hamstring problem has a return to sport decision that rests on
strength and function rather than on the knee, and the functional tests are the
assessment. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

In a knee with a previous hamstring graft harvest the tendons are altered, and
posterior or medial pain after a reconstruction may be donor site related.
4.20.1 covers that assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not examining the back of the knee at all.

The second is attributing posterior pain to the meniscus without a resisted
flexion test.

The third is confusing the biceps with the iliotibial band laterally.

The fourth is missing a corner problem in a patient tender at the fibular head
after an injury. 4.6.8 covers it.

The fifth is attributing medial pain below the joint line to the joint when the
pes is tender. 4.16.7 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- The examination's contribution is keeping the knee out of an arthroscopy.
- Biceps tenderness with an injury history needs the corner assessed.
- An athlete's return to sport decision rests on strength and function rather than on the knee.
- After a hamstring graft harvest, posterior or medial pain may be donor site related.
- Not examining the back of the knee is the commonest error.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice hamstring problems around the knee are usually the
explanation for posterior pain that has been investigated as meniscal.

A patient with a normal arthroscopy and persisting posteromedial pain, tender
along the medial hamstrings with painful resisted flexion, had an
extra-articular problem. 4.6.9 covers the examination.

A patient with pain after a hamstring graft harvest has donor site symptoms,
which are common, usually self limiting and not a failure of the
reconstruction. 4.20.1 covers that assessment.

A patient with biceps tenderness and a partially recovered nerve palsy after
corner surgery has two findings in the same place, and separating them requires
the operation note. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

An athlete with recurrent hamstring problems and a cruciate deficient knee may
be loading the hamstrings as a substitute restraint, which is a mechanical
explanation rather than a coincidence. 4.11.3 covers that mechanism.

In medicolegal work a hamstring tendinopathy is an overload condition and is
difficult to attribute to a single event. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- Hamstring problems usually explain posterior pain investigated as meniscal.
- Donor site symptoms after a graft harvest are common and not a reconstruction failure.
- Biceps tenderness with a nerve palsy after corner surgery needs the operation note to separate.
- Recurrent hamstring problems in a cruciate deficient knee may be substitution rather than coincidence.
- As an overload condition it is hard to attribute to a single event.
