# Chronic ACL Injury

The cruciate deficient knee months or years on, where the ligament is the least
interesting thing in the study.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A chronic cruciate injury is imaged long after the event, when the oedema has
resolved and the secondary changes have had time to develop. 5.7.4 covers the
acute injury.

The ligament is often absent without any surrounding signal change, which makes
it quieter and easier to overlook than an acute tear. 5.7.3 covers the complete
tears.

It matters because the study's value has moved from the ligament to the
compartments, and what has happened to the menisci and cartilage decides what
can be offered. 6.5.1 covers chronic instability.

### How It Is Acquired

Confirm the ligament's absence on all three planes, since there is no oedema to
draw the eye to it. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Look for the deepened lateral femoral notch, which persists when the marrow
signal has gone. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Assess the menisci specifically, because the cruciate deficient knee tears its
medial meniscus over time. 5.6.4 covers vertical tears.

Measure meniscal extrusion and assess the compartments, which is the prognostic
information in this study. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Assess alignment on a long leg radiograph rather than on the magnetic resonance
study, because the mechanical question is a standing one. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

An absent cruciate ligament with no surrounding oedema, sometimes with a stump
at one attachment.

A persistently deepened lateral femoral notch, the bony record of the original
impaction. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Medial meniscal tears, particularly posterior horn and vertical patterns. 5.6.4
covers vertical tears.

Cartilage loss and subchondral change in the medial compartment, with meniscal
extrusion alongside it. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

### Key Learning Points

- The chronic study has no oedema to draw the eye to the ligament.
- A deepened lateral femoral notch persists when the marrow signal has gone.
- The cruciate deficient knee tears its medial meniscus over time.
- The compartments carry the prognostic information.
- Alignment is a standing question and belongs on a radiograph.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer moves from the ligament to the compartments within
a sentence.

The expected points are the absent ligament on three planes, the notch, the
menisci, the extrusion and the compartments, and the alignment radiograph.

Say that the medial meniscus is the structure to examine most carefully. 5.6.4
covers vertical tears.

Say that the compartment's state decides whether a reconstruction is offered.
5.3.16 covers Kellgren-Lawrence grading.

Say that the quiet absent ligament is easier to miss than a torn one. 5.7.1
covers the normal cruciate.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that without the cruciate the tibia translates and
rotates abnormally with every step, and the medial meniscus is the structure that
resists what is left. 3.7.8 covers anterior stability.

That repeated loading produces the posterior horn tears that characterise the
chronically deficient knee, which is a mechanical consequence rather than a
coincidence. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Losing the meniscus removes the secondary restraint as well as the load sharing,
so the instability and the degeneration accelerate each other. 3.7.3 covers the
secondary restraints.

The deepened notch is the one finding that dates the injury, which matters when
the history is unclear. 3.7.14 covers the injury mechanisms.

### How Good It Is

The chronic study is better at describing the consequences of the injury than at
describing the injury.

Its best contribution is the compartment assessment, because that is what
determines whether a reconstruction is reasonable. 5.11.2 covers chondral
defects.

It is limited by the absence of oedema making the ligament quiet, by the supine
position understating extrusion, and by alignment being invisible on it. 5.18.5
covers weight bearing imaging.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and the
comparison is the claim: this study describes consequence better than injury,
which is a comparison rather than a number.

### Key Learning Points

- Abnormal translation and rotation load the medial meniscus with every step.
- Posterior horn tears are a mechanical consequence rather than a coincidence.
- Meniscal loss removes a secondary restraint as well as load sharing.
- The deepened notch is the finding that dates the injury.
- The compartment assessment determines whether reconstruction is reasonable.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the chronic study answers whether a reconstruction still has
something to protect.

Preserved cartilage with a repairable meniscal tear is the knee where
reconstruction and repair together have the clearest argument. 5.6.13 covers
meniscal repair imaging.

Established medial compartment loss with extrusion is a compartment problem, and
reconstruction alone addresses the wrong thing. 5.3.17 covers joint space
narrowing.

Varus alignment with medial compartment loss makes the alignment the primary
decision, which is why the radiograph is part of this assessment. 5.3.7 covers
the mechanical axis.

A patient functioning well with a deficient cruciate is managed on function, and
the study changes nothing. 4.10.3 covers chronic cruciate deficiency.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the absent ligament and stopping, so the
compartment information goes unstated. 5.20.3 covers reporting cruciate
injuries.

A quiet absent ligament is missed because there is no oedema to draw attention.
5.7.3 covers the complete tears.

Meniscal extrusion goes unmeasured, so the mechanically informative finding is
absent from the report. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Cartilage loss is understated on a protocol built for ligaments. 5.5.7 covers the
cartilage.

Alignment is assumed from the coronal images rather than measured standing.
5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

### Key Learning Points

- Reporting the absent ligament and stopping omits the useful information.
- A quiet absent ligament is missed for want of oedema.
- Extrusion goes unmeasured and the report loses its mechanical content.
- A ligament protocol understates cartilage loss.
- Alignment assumed from coronal images is not a measurement.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the chronic cruciate deficient knee is where imaging is used
to answer a question it was not designed for.

The decision is whether the compartment can still be protected, and the
surrogates for that are cartilage thickness and extrusion, both measured supine
and both poorly standardised. 5.18.5 covers weight bearing imaging.

Cartilage loss on a standard ligament protocol is understated, so a knee judged
suitable on imaging can be found otherwise at arthroscopy. 5.5.7 covers the
cartilage.

Whether reconstruction alters the degenerative course is unsettled, and the
imaging is being read as though that relationship were established. 5.21.2
covers the cruciate imaging literature.

Alignment and meniscal state interact, and no single study carries both: the
standing radiograph has the alignment and the magnetic resonance study has the
meniscus. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Quantitative cartilage sequences would support this decision better than a
morphological read and are not in routine use. 5.18.1 covers quantitative MRI.

### Key Learning Points

- The surrogates for a protectable compartment are supine and poorly standardised.
- A ligament protocol understates the cartilage loss that decides the case.
- Whether reconstruction alters the degenerative course is unsettled.
- Alignment and meniscal state sit in two different studies.
- Quantitative cartilage sequences would fit this decision and are not routine.
