# POL Function

The structure that takes over near extension. Taut when the knee is straight,
slack when it bends, and the reason valgus is tested in slight flexion.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A structure running obliquely across the back of a joint is stretched when the
joint straightens and slackens when it bends.

That makes it a restraint near full extension and little use in flexion.

If a joint has one restraint for most of the range and another near extension,
testing in the first structure's range is how you isolate it.

### At the Knee

The posterior oblique ligament is a thickening of the posteromedial capsule
running from the femur obliquely downwards and forwards to the tibia and
meniscus. 2.9.3 covers the anatomy.

It is taut near full extension and slack in flexion, which is the reciprocal
of the superficial ligament's behaviour. 3.9.3 covers that ligament.

Near extension it contributes substantially to valgus and to rotational
restraint. 3.9.9 covers the division through range.

That is why valgus testing is performed at 20 to 30 degrees: flexing the knee
slackens this structure and isolates the superficial ligament.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which it responds to more sharply than the superficial
ligament.

Whether the posteromedial corner is injured as a unit. 3.9.6 covers the
corner.

Semimembranosus activity, since the muscle tensions the capsule dynamically.
2.9.5 covers it.

Chronicity, since a chronically lax medial side has stretched this structure.

### Clinical Relevance

Valgus opening in full extension implicates this structure and the corner,
which is why testing in extension is informative. Section 4 covers the tests.

It is the structure most often implicated when a medial injury behaves worse
than an isolated superficial tear should.

Reconstruction of the medial side usually includes it, because rotation and
near extension control depend on it. 3.9.8 covers the construct.

Knowing it is slack in flexion explains the whole examination sequence.

### Key Learning Points

- An oblique posterior structure is stretched by extension and slackened by flexion.
- The posterior oblique ligament is taut near extension and slack in flexion.
- That is the reciprocal of the superficial ligament's behaviour.
- Valgus testing at 20 to 30 degrees slackens it and isolates the superficial ligament.
- Opening in full extension implicates this structure and the corner.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The reciprocal behaviour is what gives the medial side continuous coverage
across the arc, in the same way the cruciate bundles do for their directions.
3.7.4 covers that comparison.

It also means a single structure's apportionment figure is uninformative
without an angle, and that the examination sequence is a sampling strategy
rather than a ritual. 3.9.9 covers the division.

Its capsular nature matters: it is a thickening rather than a discrete cord,
so its boundaries are defined by convention as much as by dissection.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching describes three arms of the posterior oblique ligament,
superficial, central and capsular, with the central arm the most substantial.
2.9.3 covers the anatomy.

Its contribution to valgus restraint is greatest at or near full extension and
falls rapidly with flexion.

It contributes substantially to restraining external rotation near extension,
which is why it appears in the anteromedial rotatory instability account.
3.9.7 covers it.

The semimembranosus inserts into and tensions the posteromedial capsule,
making this a dynamically reinforced structure. 2.9.6 covers dynamic
stabilisation.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, as the dominant variable.

Whether the corner is injured as a unit rather than in isolation.

Semimembranosus function.

Chronic stretching in a long standing medial laxity.

### How It Is Measured

Sectioning studies with valgus and rotational loads at several angles
apportion its contribution.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates the posteromedial capsule and its
injury. 5.9 covers imaging.

Valgus stress radiography in full extension measures the functional
consequence of its loss.

Its capsular nature makes selective sectioning harder than for a discrete
ligament, which is a limitation in the figures.

### Clinical Relevance

Test valgus in full extension as well as at 30 degrees.

Read opening in extension as implicating the posteromedial structures.

Expect apportionment figures here to be less precise than for a discrete
ligament.

Consider semimembranosus function as part of the dynamic restraint.

### Key Learning Points

- Reciprocal behaviour gives the medial side continuous coverage across the arc.
- An apportionment figure here is uninformative without a flexion angle.
- Conventional teaching describes three arms, with the central the most substantial.
- It restrains external rotation substantially near extension.
- Its capsular nature makes selective sectioning harder than for a discrete ligament.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the posterior oblique ligament as the structure whose involvement
converts a medial injury from non operative to operative, and treat the
examination in extension as the way that is established. 3.9.1 covers valgus
stability.

Conventional teaching includes it in anatomic medial reconstruction, with a
separate limb or a separate graft, because reconstructing the superficial
ligament alone leaves the near extension range and the rotation less
controlled. 3.9.8 covers the construct.

In a chronic medial injury it is the structure most likely to have stretched,
which is why a chronic reconstruction addresses it even where the acute injury
appeared to spare it.

### What Changes It

Acute or chronic presentation, which changes its state.

Whether the corner was injured as a unit.

Graft availability for a second limb.

Alignment, which loads the reconstruction. 3.13 covers alignment.

### How It Is Measured

Valgus stress radiography in extension measures the consequence of its loss.

Examination under anaesthetic at several angles is the practical assessment.

Robotic testing compares reconstructions that include and exclude it.

No measurement confirms a reconstructed posterior oblique limb is functioning.

### Clinical Relevance

Establish its involvement by examining in extension before deciding on
surgery.

Include it in anatomic reconstruction, as a separate limb or graft.

Address it in a chronic reconstruction even where the acute injury spared it.

Measure the result with stress radiography in extension as well as at 30
degrees.

### Key Learning Points

- Its involvement converts a medial injury from non operative to operative.
- Examination in extension is how that is established.
- Reconstructing the superficial ligament alone leaves near extension and rotation uncontrolled.
- In a chronic injury it is the structure most likely to have stretched.
- No measurement confirms a reconstructed limb is functioning.
