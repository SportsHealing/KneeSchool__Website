# Growth Plates

The growing zones near the ends of the bones, which make a child's knee behave
differently from an adult's.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

While you are still growing, your bones are not solid all the way along. Near
each end there is a disc of cartilage called a growth plate, or physis. It is
where new bone is added, which is how your bones get longer.

The knee has two of the most productive growth plates in the body: one at the
bottom of the thigh bone and one at the top of the shin bone. Between them
they produce roughly two thirds of the length of your leg.

There is also a growing area at the front of the shin, just below the knee,
where the big tendon attaches.

### Function

Growth plates make bone longer. They close and turn into solid bone when
growth finishes, which happens at different ages for different people, usually
in the mid to late teens.

Here is the part that matters. While it is open, the growth plate is softer
than the ligaments around it. In an adult, a bad twist tears a ligament. In
someone still growing, the same twist is more likely to injure the growth
plate instead.

This is why a young athlete and an adult can have the same accident and end up
with completely different injuries, and why a child's knee injury is always
assessed on its own terms.

### Key Learning Points

- Growth plates are discs of cartilage near the ends of growing bones.
- The two at the knee produce about two thirds of the length of the leg.
- They close in the mid to late teens, at different ages for different people.
- While open, they are softer than ligaments, so injury patterns differ from adults.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

The physis, or growth plate, is a layer of specialised cartilage near each end
of a long bone. At the knee there is one at the distal femur and one at the
proximal tibia. The tibial tubercle at the front has its own growing area,
called an apophysis, where the patellar tendon attaches.

They close progressively through adolescence, generally earlier in girls than
in boys, with most closure around the mid to late teens.

### Function

Growth plates are responsible for the lengthening of bone. The two at the knee
are the most productive in the lower limb, contributing roughly sixty five per
cent of leg length.

Because they contribute so much, injury or interference here has a greater
effect on final leg length than at most other sites.

### Clinical Relevance

An open growth plate is mechanically weaker than the surrounding ligaments. A
force that would tear an adult's ligament tends instead to injure the growth
plate in a child, so the same accident produces a different injury depending
on skeletal maturity.

This changes how children are assessed. Tenderness directly over a growth
plate after an injury is taken seriously even when the x-ray looks normal,
because the cartilage itself does not show up on a plain film.

It also changes surgery. Operations that would be routine in an adult may need
modifying in a growing child to avoid crossing a growth plate, since damage
there can lead to uneven growth or a limb length difference.

The apophysis at the tibial tubercle is the site of a familiar condition in
active adolescents, which is covered in the conditions and children's sections
rather than here.

### Key Learning Points

- There is a growth plate at the lower femur and the upper tibia.
- Together they contribute roughly two thirds of lower limb length.
- They close through adolescence, generally earlier in girls than in boys.
- An open growth plate is weaker than the surrounding ligaments.
- Growth plate cartilage does not show on plain x-rays, so tenderness guides assessment.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** At what age do growth plates close?
Around the knee, generally in the mid teens for girls and the late teens for
boys, with considerable individual variation. A hand x-ray is sometimes used
to estimate skeletal age.

**Q.** Can a knee injury affect how tall my child grows?
An injury to a growth plate can affect growth of that bone, which is why
children's knee injuries are assessed carefully. Most heal without any growth
problem.

**Q.** Why is a normal x-ray not reassuring in a child?
Growth plate cartilage does not appear on a plain x-ray, so a significant
injury there can look normal. Tenderness over the plate is treated as
meaningful.

**Q.** Is growing pain in the knee normal?
Aching around the knee in active adolescents is common and often related to
the growing areas under load. Persistent pain, swelling or limping should be
assessed.

See a doctor for any child or adolescent with knee pain after an injury, with
a limp, with swelling, or with tenderness over the bone near the knee.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The physis is a cartilaginous growth plate between epiphysis and metaphysis,
organised into reserve, proliferative, hypertrophic and calcification zones.
The hypertrophic zone is the mechanically weakest and is where most physeal
separations occur.

The distal femoral physis is undulating and interdigitated, which contributes
both to its stability and to its propensity for growth arrest after injury.
The proximal tibial physis is adjacent to the popliteal neurovascular
structures posteriorly.

The tibial tubercle is a traction apophysis, continuous with the proximal
tibial physis, receiving the patellar tendon insertion.

### Function

The distal femoral physis contributes approximately seventy per cent of
femoral length and nine millimetres per year in late childhood; the proximal
tibial physis contributes approximately sixty per cent of tibial length and
six millimetres per year. Together they account for roughly sixty five per
cent of lower limb length.

Physeal closure is progressive and sex dependent, occurring earlier in girls.
Skeletal rather than chronological age governs remaining growth, and is
estimated radiographically.

### Clinical Relevance

The open physis is weaker in tension and shear than the adjacent ligaments and
capsule, so loading patterns that produce ligamentous injury in adults produce
physeal separation in the skeletally immature. The same mechanism therefore
yields different injuries at different ages.

Physeal cartilage is radiolucent, so plain radiographs may appear normal
despite significant injury. Focal physeal tenderness after trauma warrants a
high index of suspicion.

Physeal injury around the knee carries a disproportionate risk of growth
disturbance because of the contribution these plates make, producing angular
deformity or limb length discrepancy. This constrains surgical technique, and
physeal sparing approaches exist for this reason.

Classification of physeal injuries and their management, and apophyseal
conditions, are covered in the children's and conditions sections.

### Key Learning Points

- The physis has reserve, proliferative, hypertrophic and calcification zones.
- Separation occurs through the mechanically weak hypertrophic zone.
- The distal femoral and proximal tibial physes give about sixty five per cent of limb length.
- Open physes are weaker than ligaments, so mechanism produces different injuries by age.
- Physeal cartilage is radiolucent, so a normal radiograph does not exclude injury.
