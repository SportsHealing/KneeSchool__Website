# Hyperextension Injury

A knee forced past straight. An uncommon mechanism with a characteristic and
frequently underestimated injury pattern.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A hyperextension injury is the knee being forced beyond full extension,
usually during a kick that missed, a stumble into a hole, or a landing with
the leg straight.

Asking about it tests whether the posterior structures and the cruciates were
loaded in a way that twisting and valgus mechanisms do not reproduce.

It matters because the injury is often more extensive than the account
suggests.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether the knee went backwards or past straight, and whether they saw or
felt it happen.

Ask whether they were kicking, landing, or stepping into something unexpected.

Ask whether the knee felt like it came apart, and whether it swelled
immediately.

Ask about any numbness, tingling or weakness in the foot afterwards, because a
nerve symptom raises the energy of the injury considerably.

Ask whether they can now fully straighten the knee and whether it feels like
it goes back too far. 4.7.3 covers hyperextension on examination.

### What a Positive Means

A hyperextension injury loads the anterior cruciate first, then the posterior
capsule and the posterior cruciate as the energy rises.

Immediate swelling with gross hyperextension raises both cruciates and the
posterior capsule, which is a higher energy injury than it may appear.

Hyperextension with a varus or valgus component raises the corresponding
corner. 4.13.7 covers the external rotation recurvatum test.

Any neurological symptom after hyperextension raises a knee dislocation that
reduced spontaneously, and the vascular assessment takes priority.

Hyperextension in a child raises a physeal injury rather than a ligamentous
one. 4.19.1 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Hyperextension loads the anterior cruciate first and the posterior structures as energy rises.
- Gross hyperextension with immediate swelling is a higher energy injury than it appears.
- Any neurological symptom raises a spontaneously reduced dislocation.
- In a child, consider the physis before the ligament.
- Ask whether the knee now feels like it goes back too far.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning is that the sequence of failure in hyperextension is
reasonably predictable and that is what makes the mechanism useful.

The anterior cruciate fails first because it is loaded in extension. 3.7.1
covers its functions.

The posterior capsule and the posterior oblique or popliteal structures fail
next.

The posterior cruciate fails with higher energy, which is counterintuitive and
worth holding, because a posterior cruciate injury in a hyperextension
mechanism indicates a serious injury.

Recurvatum with external rotation on examination indicates a posterolateral
component and is a specific finding. 4.13.7 covers the test.

### How Good It Is

Hyperextension is a well recalled mechanism because it is visually striking
and often witnessed.

It performs well for raising the suspicion of a combined injury, which is its
main value.

It performs poorly for identifying which structures, because the examination
is needed and the acute examination is limited by guarding.

The presence of pre-existing hyperextension in a hypermobile patient confounds
the assessment, which is why comparison with the other side matters here as
much as anywhere. 4.23.3 covers hypermobility.

### Key Learning Points

- The failure sequence is anterior cruciate, then posterior capsule, then posterior cruciate.
- A posterior cruciate injury from hyperextension indicates a serious injury.
- Recurvatum with external rotation indicates a posterolateral component.
- The mechanism is well recalled and raises suspicion of a combined injury.
- Pre-existing hyperextension in a hypermobile patient confounds the assessment.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a hyperextension history should widen the assessment rather
than narrow it, which is the opposite of most mechanisms in this group.

A knee with hyperextension injury and gross laxity needs a multiligament
assessment and a vascular review before any elective planning. 4.23.6 covers
the assessment.

A residual hyperextension deformity after injury changes the plan for any
subsequent reconstruction, because a graft in a hyperextending knee is loaded
differently.

The combination of hyperextension and a posterolateral injury is particularly
important, because an unaddressed posterolateral corner is a recognised cause
of cruciate reconstruction failure. 4.13.4 covers the assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating hyperextension as a minor mechanism because
the patient walked away.

The second is missing the vascular question in a knee that hyperextended
grossly and reduced itself.

The third is attributing residual hyperextension to the injury when it was
present before, which is why the other knee is examined. 4.1.5 covers the
principle.

And a hyperextension injury in a kicking sport will recur, so rehabilitation
has to address the mechanism rather than only the structure. 4.21.7 covers
return to sport.

### Key Learning Points

- A hyperextension history should widen the assessment rather than narrow it.
- Gross laxity after hyperextension needs a multiligament and vascular assessment.
- A graft in a hyperextending knee is loaded differently, which changes the plan.
- An unaddressed posterolateral corner is a recognised cause of reconstruction failure.
- Walking away does not make hyperextension a minor mechanism.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the hyperextension injury appears as a failed
reconstruction in a knee that hyperextends, or as a chronic posterolateral
problem that was never diagnosed.

A graft that stretched out in a hyperextending knee raises whether the
hyperextension was addressed at all, and frequently it was not. 4.10.12 covers
revision assessment.

A chronic posterolateral injury presenting years later with varus thrust and
lateral pain usually traces to a hyperextension or varus injury that was
assessed as a cruciate injury alone. 4.13.8 covers combined lateral injuries.

Correcting hyperextension is a different surgical problem from reconstructing
a ligament, and it may involve the bone rather than the soft tissue, which is
why the distinction matters at this level.

### Key Learning Points

- A graft that stretched out in a hyperextending knee raises whether the hyperextension was addressed.
- A late posterolateral problem often traces to a hyperextension injury assessed as cruciate alone.
- Correcting hyperextension may be a bony problem rather than a soft tissue one.
