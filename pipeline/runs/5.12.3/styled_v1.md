# Patellar Tilt

The patella rotated in the axial plane, which is measured where the joint is
stable and matters where it is not.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Patellar tilt is rotation of the patella about its long axis, so the lateral facet
faces more posteriorly. 2.4.1 covers the patellar anatomy.

It is measured on axial images as an angle between the patellar axis and a
femoral reference line. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

It matters because tilt reflects the balance between the lateral and medial
restraints, and it changes with the flexion angle at which it is measured. 2.12.4
covers the retinacula.

### How It Is Acquired

Measure on axial images at a stated flexion angle, because tilt reduces as the
patella engages. 3.12.4 covers the trochlear function.

Use a defined femoral reference line, usually the posterior condylar line, and
state which. 5.16.3 covers rotational profiles.

Measure the patellar axis consistently, since the patella's shape makes this less
obvious than it sounds. 2.4.1 covers the patellar anatomy.

Assess the lateral retinaculum, which is the structure tilt reflects. 2.12.4
covers the retinacula.

Assess the other measurements alongside, since tilt rarely occurs alone. 5.12.1
covers trochlear dysplasia.

### What Normal Looks Like

A patella whose transverse axis is roughly parallel to the posterior condylar
line at the measured angle. 5.16.3 covers rotational profiles.

Symmetrical facet joint spaces. 5.12.7 covers patellofemoral arthritis.

An unthickened lateral retinaculum. 2.12.4 covers the retinacula.

Reducing tilt as flexion increases. 3.12.4 covers the trochlear function.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

An increased angle between the patellar axis and the femoral reference line.
5.16.3 covers rotational profiles.

Lateral facet joint space narrowing with medial facet widening. 5.12.7 covers
patellofemoral arthritis.

A thickened lateral retinaculum. 2.12.4 covers the retinacula.

Tilt persisting into flexion, which indicates it is structural rather than
positional. 3.12.4 covers the trochlear function.

### Key Learning Points

- Tilt is rotation about the patella's long axis.
- It is measured against a defined femoral reference line.
- It reduces as the patella engages the trochlea.
- Measuring in flexion understates it.
- Tilt persisting into flexion is structural.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the measurement to qualify with the flexion angle before
quoting it.

The expected points are the stated flexion angle, the femoral reference line, the
patellar axis, the lateral retinaculum, and the other measurements.

Say that tilt reduces with engagement, so the angle matters. 3.12.4 covers the
trochlear function.

Say that the reference line must be named. 5.16.3 covers rotational profiles.

Say that the measurement is made where the joint is most stable. 5.3.3 covers the
skyline view.

### What Normal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that tilt is the net result of lateral and medial soft
tissue tension acting on a patella sitting in a groove, so it reflects a balance
rather than a single structure. 2.12.4 covers the retinacula.

As flexion engages the patella, the groove's geometry takes over from the soft
tissues, which is why tilt reduces and why a flexed measurement understates the
problem. 3.12.4 covers the trochlear function.

That means the measurement is made at an angle where the joint has already been
stabilised, which is the same criticism this section makes of the skyline view.
5.3.3 covers the skyline view.

Lateral facet narrowing follows persistent tilt because the lateral facet carries
more load, which links the measurement to the arthritis pattern. 3.12.3 covers the
contact pressures.

### How Good It Is

Tilt is measured reproducibly at a stated angle and is measured where it matters
least.

Its best contribution is the structural case, where tilt persists into flexion and
the lateral facet shows the consequence. 5.12.7 covers patellofemoral arthritis.

It is limited because instability occurs near extension and the measurement is
made in flexion, so the number describes the stable part of the range. 5.4.14
covers patellar tracking on ultrasound.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no tilt
angles or thresholds appear either: those are published quantities tied to
specific flexion angles and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- Tilt is the net result of lateral and medial soft tissue tension.
- Engagement lets the groove take over from the soft tissues.
- A flexed measurement understates the problem.
- The measurement is made where the joint has already been stabilised.
- Lateral facet narrowing follows persistent tilt.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level tilt contributes to the lateral tightness question rather than
to the instability decision.

Persistent tilt with lateral facet narrowing is the pattern that supports
addressing the lateral structures. 5.12.7 covers patellofemoral arthritis.

Tilt that reduces with flexion is positional and carries less weight. 3.12.4
covers the trochlear function.

Tilt alongside dysplasia, alta and a lateralised tuberosity is part of a combined
picture rather than a separate problem. 5.12.1 covers trochlear dysplasia.

The measurement needs its flexion angle and reference line stated to be usable.
5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is quoting tilt without the flexion angle. 5.20.8 covers
common reporting errors.

A different femoral reference line is used from the threshold's. 5.16.3 covers
rotational profiles.

Tilt measured in flexion is taken as the extension value. 3.12.4 covers the
trochlear function.

Tilt is treated as the instability measurement. 5.16.4 covers the tuberosity to
groove measurement.

The lateral retinaculum is not assessed. 2.12.4 covers the retinacula.

### Key Learning Points

- Tilt is quoted without the flexion angle.
- A different reference line is used from the threshold's.
- A flexed measurement is taken as the extension value.
- Tilt is treated as the instability measurement.
- The lateral retinaculum goes unassessed.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level patellar tilt is a well defined measurement of the wrong part
of the range.

Instability happens near extension, and every static measurement of tilt is made
with the knee flexed enough to fit in a coil and engaged enough to be stable, so
the measurement describes the position the patella is safe in. 5.3.3 covers the
skyline view.

That is the same objection this section raises against the skyline view, arriving
at a cross sectional measurement, which suggests the problem is the posture rather
than the modality. 5.4.14 covers patellar tracking on ultrasound.

Tilt conventions differ in both the patellar axis and the femoral reference line,
so a value needs two methods stated rather than one. 5.21.4 covers the
patellofemoral imaging literature.

Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging measures tilt through the range and is not
routine, which leaves the measurement that would answer the question unavailable.
5.18.6 covers dynamic magnetic resonance imaging.

The defensible use is to read persistent tilt with lateral facet change as a
structural finding, and to treat an isolated flexed tilt value as weak. 5.12.7
covers patellofemoral arthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- Every static tilt measurement is made where the patella is safe.
- The same objection applies to the skyline view, so the posture is the problem.
- A tilt value needs both its methods stated.
- Dynamic measurement through the range is not routine.
- An isolated flexed tilt value is weak evidence.
