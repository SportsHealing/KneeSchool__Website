# Pivot Shift

The only examination in the knee that measures what the patient complains
about, and the one the patient being awake prevents you from performing.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The pivot shift reproduces the movement a patient with an anterior cruciate
rupture describes: the tibia rotating and subluxing forwards on the femur, then
reducing.

The technique belongs to the anterior cruciate chapter, where the test is first
met. 4.10.6 covers it.

What this chapter adds is the test's place among the rotational assessments.
Rotation rather than translation is the complaint, and this is the test that
reads rotation. 3.11.4 covers pivot shift control.

### How It Is Performed

Lay the patient supine and fully relaxed, with the knee extended.

Apply a valgus force and internal rotation to the tibia, and flex the knee from
extension.

Feel for the lateral tibial plateau reducing from a subluxed position as the
knee passes about twenty to thirty degrees of flexion.

Compare with the other knee using the same grip and the same forces, and grade
the glide, the clunk and whether it is reproducible. 4.10.6 covers the grading.

Note that relaxation is the whole difficulty. A patient who guards prevents the
subluxation, so the test fails in the direction of a false negative. 4.14.6
covers the setting that removes the guarding.

### What a Positive Means

A reduction felt as the knee flexes indicates a deficient anterior cruciate.

The size of the shift reflects more than the cruciate. The anterolateral
structures, the lateral meniscus and the posterior horn all contribute to
rotational control, and their loss makes the shift larger. 3.11.3 covers the
anterolateral ligament.

A large shift in a knee with an isolated cruciate rupture suggests additional
rotational deficiency rather than a worse cruciate injury, and that distinction
changes the operation. 4.14.3 covers the pattern.

A shift in a knee with an intact Lachman test should be distrusted, since the
cruciate resists the translation the shift depends on. 4.10.4 covers the
Lachman test.

### Key Learning Points

- The test reproduces the movement the patient complains about, which no other examination does.
- Relaxation is the whole difficulty, and guarding produces a false negative.
- The anterolateral structures, the lateral meniscus and the posterior horn all affect the size of the shift.
- A large shift suggests additional rotational deficiency rather than a worse cruciate tear.
- A shift with an intact Lachman test should be distrusted.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the pivot shift is the test to name when asked what correlates with
the patient's symptoms, and knowing that is more useful than knowing the grip.

The expected points are the relaxed patient, the valgus and internal rotation,
flexion from extension, the angle of reduction, the grading, and the
comparison.

Say that the test is graded as a glide, a clunk and a gross shift, and that the
grade is recorded rather than the word positive. 4.1.8 covers the record.

Say that you would perform the Lachman test first, because a pivot shift in a
knee with no translation is not a pivot shift. 4.10.4 covers it.

Say that the test is unreliable awake and reliable under anaesthesia, and that
the difference is relaxation rather than technique. 4.14.6 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the anterior cruciate resists anterior
translation and internal rotation, and that the pivot shift loads both at once.
3.11.5 covers internal rotation control.

The iliotibial band supplies the reducing force as its line of pull crosses the
axis of rotation during flexion, which is why the reduction happens at a
particular angle rather than gradually. 3.11.1 covers its function.

The Kaplan fibres and the anterolateral ligament contribute to the restraint,
which is the anatomical basis for the larger shift in some knees and for the
lateral extra-articular procedures that address it. 3.11.2 covers the Kaplan
fibres.

The lateral meniscus posterior horn acts as a bony block to the subluxation, so
its loss increases the shift and its repair reduces it. 4.9.9 covers meniscal
root tears.

### How Good It Is

The pivot shift is the examination finding that correlates best with the
patient's complaint and worst with reproducibility, and both halves of that
matter.

It is specific rather than sensitive awake. A positive result is close to
diagnostic and a negative one excludes little, because guarding produces the
negative.

It is considerably more reliable under anaesthesia, which is why it appears in
operation notes more than in clinic letters. 4.14.6 covers that setting.

Instrumented measurement of the shift exists and has not displaced the manual
grading in practice, which is worth knowing when a number appears in a letter
from a research unit.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- It correlates best with the complaint and worst with reproducibility.
- The iliotibial band supplies the reducing force, which is why reduction happens at a particular angle.
- The Kaplan fibres and the anterolateral ligament contribute to the restraint.
- The lateral meniscus posterior horn blocks the subluxation, so its loss increases the shift.
- It is specific awake, sensitive under anaesthesia, and the difference is relaxation.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the pivot shift grade is one of the few examination findings
that changes the operation rather than the diagnosis.

A large shift in a knee having an anterior cruciate reconstruction is the main
clinical argument for adding a lateral extra-articular procedure, and that
decision rests on this test. 3.11.7 covers the procedure.

The grade also contributes to the return to sport decision after
reconstruction, where a persisting shift in an athlete is a different problem
from a stable knee with a confidence complaint. 4.22.6 covers the score built
for that question.

It contributes little to whether to reconstruct at all, which is a functional
decision, and this chapter's argument is that the test which measures the
complaint still does not make that decision. 4.10.3 covers the chronic knee.

Where the awake grade and the patient's account disagree, examination under
anaesthesia settles it, and in a revision setting that is worth arranging
deliberately. 4.14.6 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording positive or negative rather than the grade,
which discards the information the test carries.

The second is reading a negative result in a guarding patient as a normal knee.

The third is attributing a large shift to a severe cruciate tear rather than to
additional rotational deficiency.

The fourth is performing it without the Lachman test, which leaves nothing to
corroborate it.

The fifth is comparing with the other knee only when the result is abnormal,
which removes the baseline in exactly the cases that need it.

### Key Learning Points

- A large shift is the main clinical argument for adding a lateral extra-articular procedure.
- The grade contributes to the return to sport decision after reconstruction.
- It contributes little to whether to reconstruct at all, which is a functional decision.
- Recording positive or negative rather than the grade discards what the test carries.
- A large shift means additional rotational deficiency rather than a worse cruciate tear.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In revision practice the pivot shift is the finding that separates a graft that
has failed from a knee that was never rotationally controlled.

A patient with a reconstructed cruciate, a firm Lachman and a persisting pivot
shift has rotational instability over a competent graft, and that is an
anterolateral problem rather than a graft problem. 4.14.3 covers it.

That knee is the main indication for a lateral extra-articular procedure at
revision, and the examination is what identifies it rather than the imaging.
3.11.8 covers the combination.

A persisting shift with a loose Lachman is a failed graft, and the two are
distinguished by the translation rather than by the rotation. 4.10.4 covers the
Lachman test.

In a knee with a previous tenodesis the shift may be abolished mechanically
while the graft is incompetent, so a negative pivot shift no longer excludes a
failed reconstruction. 3.11.7 covers the procedure.

A knee with a posterolateral corner injury gives a larger shift for the same
cruciate injury, and attributing all of it to the cruciate leads to a revision
that does not hold. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

In medicolegal work an awake pivot shift is weak evidence in either direction,
and an operative note recording it under anaesthesia is strong. 4.23.7 covers
that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A firm Lachman with a persisting shift is an anterolateral problem, not a graft problem.
- That knee is the main indication for a lateral extra-articular procedure at revision.
- A persisting shift with a loose Lachman is a failed graft, distinguished by the translation.
- After a tenodesis a negative pivot shift no longer excludes a failed reconstruction.
- A corner injury gives a larger shift for the same cruciate injury.
