# Posteromedial Corner Injuries

The combination that decides, and the point at which the medial side stops
healing reliably.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A posteromedial corner injury involves the posterior oblique ligament, the
capsule, the semimembranosus attachments and the meniscocapsular junction
together. 2.9.4 covers the posteromedial corner.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows them on posterior coronal and axial images.
5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

It matters because the medial side heals reliably until this corner is involved.
5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

### How It Is Acquired

Use thin posterior coronal and axial images. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee
protocol.

Assess each component and report it as intact, injured or not assessable. 5.20.4
covers reporting MCL injuries.

Assess the meniscocapsular junction specifically. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Assess the semimembranosus and its tibial attachments. 2.9.5 covers the
semimembranosus.

Assess the cruciates, since this corner rarely fails alone. 3.9.10 covers the
combined ACL and MCL injury.

### What Normal Looks Like

Each posteromedial component identifiable and continuous. 2.9.4 covers the
posteromedial corner.

An intact meniscocapsular junction. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Normal semimembranosus attachments. 2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

No posteromedial soft tissue oedema. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Injury of two or more components, which is the pattern. 2.9.4 covers the
posteromedial corner.

Posteromedial oedema with the components hard to separate. 5.5.9 covers the
ligaments.

Meniscocapsular separation. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Avulsion at the semimembranosus attachment. 2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

An anterior cruciate tear alongside, which is the usual combination. 5.7.3 covers
complete tears.

### Key Learning Points

- The injury is a combination of components.
- The medial side heals reliably until this corner is involved.
- Each component is reported separately.
- The meniscocapsular junction is part of the corner.
- The corner rarely fails alone.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer lists the components.

The expected points are the posterior images, each component, the
meniscocapsular junction, the semimembranosus, and the cruciates.

Say that the grading is clinical and the imaging is structural. 4.12.1 covers the
valgus stress testing.

Say that this corner carries rotation. 3.9.6 covers the posteromedial corner
function.

Say that a missed corner injury is a recognised cause of cruciate graft failure.
5.7.11 covers the graft failure.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that these structures act together to resist valgus
and internal rotation, so the deficit follows the combination rather than any
single component. 3.9.6 covers the posteromedial corner function.

That is the same argument the lateral corner makes, and the two chapters reach
it from opposite directions: laterally the structures are hard to see, medially
they are easy to see and get merged. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

Acute oedema establishes the pattern and obscures the parts. 5.5.9 covers the
ligaments.

The meniscocapsular separation is the component with its own operation. 5.6.10
covers ramp lesions.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging is the best available structural assessment of this
corner and does not grade the laxity.

Its best contribution is the combination of components and the meniscocapsular
junction. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

It is limited because acute oedema merges the components, because chronic injury
leaves nothing to see, and because the deficit is rotational and clinical.
4.12.6 covers the AMRI assessment.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no laxity
values or grading thresholds appear either: those are clinical grades with
published definitions and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The deficit follows the combination of components.
- The lateral chapter reaches the same point from the other side.
- Medially the structures are easy to see and get merged.
- Acute oedema establishes the pattern and hides the parts.
- The meniscocapsular separation has its own operation.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the imaging decides whether the corner is addressed alongside
a cruciate reconstruction. 3.9.10 covers the combined ACL and MCL injury.

A posterior oblique injury with a meniscocapsular separation is the pattern that
gets operated. 5.9.4 covers POL injuries.

Left untreated this corner loads the graft. 5.7.11 covers the graft failure.

A chronic corner injury with valgus alignment raises an osteotomy. 5.17.4 covers
the osteotomy.

An examination under anaesthesia records what the imaging cannot. 4.12.6 covers
the AMRI assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the superficial ligament and calling the medial
side done. 5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

Absence of a component from the report is read as that component being intact.
5.20.4 covers reporting MCL injuries.

A chronic injury with no oedema is read as normal. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

A semimembranosus avulsion is reported as a hamstring injury. 2.9.5 covers the
semimembranosus.

Rotational laxity is inferred from the appearance. 4.12.6 covers the AMRI
assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Reporting the superficial ligament is not reporting the corner.
- Absence from the report is not evidence of integrity.
- A chronic injury with no oedema reads as normal.
- A semimembranosus avulsion is misattributed.
- Rotational laxity cannot be inferred.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the corner injury was missed and the reconstruction has
stretched. 5.7.11 covers the graft failure.

The original study usually shows it, so obtaining and re-reading that study is
the productive step. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Chronic injury has no oedema and may have no identifiable components, so the
diagnosis becomes clinical and the imaging supports the plan. 4.12.6 covers the
AMRI assessment.

A reconstruction here carries no published expected appearance. 5.9.7 covers the
post-operative medial reconstructions.

Valgus alignment in a chronic medial injury is part of the problem and part of
the operation. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

The useful report lists each component with its state, the meniscocapsular
junction, the cruciates and the alignment. 5.20.4 covers reporting MCL injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- The original study usually shows what was missed.
- Chronic injury may have no identifiable components.
- A reconstruction has no published expected appearance.
- Valgus alignment is part of the problem and the operation.
- The report lists components with their state.
