# Ligaments

The ligaments on magnetic resonance imaging, where the obliquely running ones
need the right plane and the right position.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The knee's ligaments appear as dark bands, because they are dense organised
collagen with little free water. 5.2.12 covers the magnetic resonance basic
principles.

The cruciates run obliquely within the joint and the collaterals run close to the
coronal plane outside it. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

It matters because an obliquely running structure is cut obliquely by every
orthogonal plane, so plane and position decide whether it can be assessed. 5.5.1
covers the standard protocol.

### How It Is Acquired

Assess the cruciates on the sagittal images with the knee in slight external
rotation, which brings the anterior cruciate into plane. 5.7.1 covers the normal
cruciate.

Confirm on the coronal and axial planes, because the sagittal plane alone can make
a normal ligament disappear. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Assess the collaterals on the coronal images along their length. 5.9.1 covers the
medial collateral anatomy.

Assess the posterolateral structures deliberately, since they are oblique and
small. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

Know the normal striated appearance of the anterior cruciate, which is bundle
anatomy. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

### What Normal Looks Like

Dark continuous bands following their expected courses. 5.7.1 covers the normal
cruciate.

A striated anterior cruciate with fine bright lines between its bundles, which is
normal. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

A uniformly dark posterior cruciate with a smooth curve. 5.8.1 covers the normal
posterior cruciate.

Collaterals as thin dark bands with the expected layers medially. 5.9.1 covers the
medial collateral anatomy.

### Key Learning Points

- Ligaments appear dark because they are dense collagen with little free water.
- The cruciates run obliquely and the collaterals near the coronal plane.
- An oblique structure is cut obliquely by every orthogonal plane.
- Slight external rotation brings the anterior cruciate into plane.
- The anterior cruciate's striations are normal bundle anatomy.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer names the plane and the position for each ligament
rather than describing appearances.

The expected points are the sagittal cruciate assessment with rotation, the
confirmation planes, the coronal collaterals, the posterolateral structures, and
the normal striations.

Say that the sagittal plane alone can make a normal cruciate disappear. 5.7.1
covers the normal cruciate.

Say that the striations are normal anatomy. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate tears.

Say that the posterolateral structures need deliberate assessment. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

### What Normal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that an obliquely running ligament crossing a slice
plane is partially volume averaged with the fat and fluid around it, so it can
appear interrupted without being torn. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Knee position changes the obliquity, which is why the external rotation is a
protocol requirement and not a convenience. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

The anterior cruciate is two bundles in a common sheath, so the interfascicular
tissue between them carries signal and produces striations in a normal ligament.
2.7.8 covers the anteromedial bundle.

The posterior cruciate is a thicker more uniform structure, which is why it is
read more easily and why its buckling is used as a translation sign. 5.8.1 covers
the normal posterior cruciate.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging reads the normal ligaments reliably when the plane and
position are right.

Its best contribution is the continuous dark band following a normal course,
which is a strong normal. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

It is limited for the obliquely running small structures of the posterolateral
corner, and for partial tears of the cruciate. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate
tears.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no rotation
angles or thickness values appear either: those are local protocol quantities and
this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- An oblique ligament can appear interrupted through partial volume averaging.
- Knee position changes the obliquity, so rotation is a protocol requirement.
- The anterior cruciate is two bundles, so striations are normal.
- The posterior cruciate is thicker and more uniform, so it reads more easily.
- Its buckling is used as a translation sign.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a confident normal ligament is worth as much as a confident
abnormal one.

A continuous dark cruciate on three planes in a stable knee is a secure normal.
5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

A collateral assessed along its length with its layers separated gives a grade
that informs management. 5.9.3 covers deep medial collateral injuries.

The posterolateral corner needs deliberate assessment and still performs
inconsistently, which bounds what a negative means. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral
corner injuries.

A ligament reported abnormal in a stable knee should be checked against the
protocol and the position before it is believed. 5.1.2 covers clinical
examination versus imaging.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading normal cruciate striation as a partial tear. 5.7.2
covers partial cruciate tears.

An obliquely cut normal ligament is reported as discontinuous. 5.2.5 covers
magnification.

The knee was positioned without rotation and the cruciate is out of plane. 5.5.1
covers the standard protocol.

The posterolateral structures are not assessed and the corner is reported intact.
5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

A single plane is accepted as sufficient. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

### Key Learning Points

- Normal striation is read as a partial tear.
- An obliquely cut normal ligament is reported as discontinuous.
- Without rotation the cruciate is out of plane.
- The corner is reported intact without being assessed.
- A single plane is accepted as sufficient.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the ligaments show most clearly that a magnetic resonance
study measures continuity and the clinical question is restraint.

A continuous ligament can be functionally lax and a dark band says nothing about
stiffness, which is the conclusion every graft, repair and healing page in this
section reaches. 5.7.8 covers cruciate healing.

Normal ligament signal has no agreed definition, so the boundary between normal
striation and a partial tear is set by the reader, which makes the least
reproducible call in the section a definitional problem rather than a technical
one. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate tears.

Knee position in the scanner is not recorded, so a ligament's apparent tension and
line cannot be corrected for by a reader. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Three dimensional isotropic acquisition removes the oblique plane problem by
allowing reformatting along any structure, which is the clearest technical gain
available here. 5.2.18 covers gradient echo imaging.

The useful report describes continuity, line and position as three separate
observations and does not infer function from them. 5.20.3 covers reporting
cruciate injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- A continuous ligament can be functionally lax.
- Normal ligament signal has no agreed definition.
- The least reproducible call is a definitional problem.
- Knee position is not recorded and cannot be corrected for.
- Isotropic acquisition removes the oblique plane problem.
