# Shock Absorption

The meniscus deforms under a fast load and recovers slowly. That is damping,
and it is a smaller job than load spreading but a real one.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A material that deforms and springs straight back stores energy and gives it
all back. A material that deforms and recovers slowly loses some of it as
heat.

Losing energy on the way through is damping. It is what turns a sharp impact
into a softer one.

Car tyres do this. They are springy enough to roll and lossy enough to take
the edge off a bump.

### At the Knee

Meniscal tissue is viscoelastic. Its stiffness depends on how fast it is
loaded, and it takes time to recover its shape.

Under a fast load the meniscus deforms and the fluid in its matrix cannot move
quickly, so it behaves stiffly and transmits more.

Conventional teaching gives the menisci a contribution of around a fifth of
the knee's shock absorbing capacity, with muscle, cartilage and bone making up
the rest. 3.4.10 covers shock absorption.

A meniscectomised knee is therefore a less well damped knee, which is a
separate loss from the contact area loss.

### What Changes It

The rate at which the load arrives, since the tissue's behaviour is rate
dependent.

Tissue hydration and matrix integrity, which set how the fluid moves.

Degeneration, which changes both.

How much meniscus is present and whether it is in the load path.

### Clinical Relevance

Losing a meniscus costs damping as well as area, and the two losses add.

Impact activities are the ones where damping matters, which bears on activity
advice. Section 6 covers management.

Muscle is the largest shock absorber and the only trainable one, so the
meniscal loss can be partly offset by strength. 3.4.8 covers energy
absorption.

This is the second of the five meniscal functions, and it is genuinely
secondary to load spreading. 3.6.1 covers the five.

### Key Learning Points

- A material that recovers slowly loses energy as heat, which is damping.
- Meniscal tissue is viscoelastic: its stiffness depends on loading rate.
- Under a fast load the fluid cannot move, so the tissue behaves stiffly.
- Conventional teaching gives the menisci around a fifth of the knee's shock absorbing capacity.
- Losing a meniscus costs damping as well as contact area.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The tissue is biphasic: a solid collagen and proteoglycan matrix with
interstitial fluid that moves through it. Load is shared between the two
phases, and the sharing depends on time.

Under instantaneous load the fluid carries most of it and the tissue is stiff.
Under sustained load the fluid flows out, the solid matrix takes over, and the
tissue creeps.

Energy is dissipated in that flow, which is where the damping comes from. It
is hysteresis rather than plastic deformation.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the meniscal contribution to shock absorption as
roughly 20 per cent, from early mechanical work, and the figure is quoted more
often than it is qualified.

Meniscectomised knees show increased impact transmission in cadaveric and in
vivo work, which is the functional form of the same claim.

The contribution is rate dependent, so it is larger in impact activities than
in walking. 3.4.10 covers loading rate.

Cartilage and subchondral bone damp too, and the menisci are one element of a
system rather than its main component.

### What Changes It

Loading rate, through the biphasic behaviour.

Matrix integrity and proteoglycan content, which set the fluid's resistance to
flow.

Degeneration and age, which reduce hydration and change the properties.

Resection, in proportion to the tissue removed.

### How It Is Measured

Confined and unconfined compression testing of meniscal plugs measures the
biphasic properties, in excised tissue.

Impact transmission testing in cadaveric knees with and without the meniscus
measures the functional contribution.

Tibial accelerometry in vivo estimates impact transmission in patients and
measures acceleration rather than energy.

The commonly quoted 20 per cent figure comes from a small early literature and
should be held loosely.

### Clinical Relevance

Expect the damping loss after meniscectomy to show in impact activities rather
than in walking.

Treat muscle as the modifiable shock absorber when the meniscal contribution
has been lost.

Hold the 20 per cent figure loosely, since its evidence base is small.

Separate the damping loss from the contact area loss, since they have
different consequences.

### Key Learning Points

- Meniscal tissue is biphasic; load sharing between phases depends on time.
- Damping comes from energy dissipated in fluid flow, which is hysteresis.
- Conventional teaching gives around 20 per cent, from a small early literature.
- The contribution is rate dependent and larger in impact activities.
- The menisci are one element of a damping system rather than its main component.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat damping as the meniscal function with the weakest quantitative evidence
and a sound mechanism. It is worth stating to a patient and not worth building
an argument on.

The clinically useful consequence is activity advice: a meniscus deficient
knee tolerates impact less well, which follows from the mechanism rather than
from a trial.

A substitute's damping properties differ from native tissue, and conventional
teaching holds that allograft and synthetic scaffolds do not reproduce them.
3.6.14 covers transplantation.

### What Changes It

Amount and location of tissue remaining or restored.

Material properties of any substitute, which differ from native tissue.

Muscle capacity, which is the modifiable element of the damping system.

Activity and surface, which set the loading rate the knee meets.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric impact testing compares intact, deficient and substituted states.

Material testing of scaffolds and allograft measures their properties against
native tissue.

In vivo accelerometry estimates impact transmission and is not a measure of
joint energy absorption.

No clinical measurement of a patient's meniscal damping exists.

### Clinical Relevance

Advise on impact activity in a meniscus deficient knee on mechanistic grounds,
and say so.

Expect a substitute not to reproduce native damping properties.

Build the argument for preservation on load distribution rather than on
damping.

Target muscle capacity as the modifiable part of the damping system.

### Key Learning Points

- Damping has the weakest quantitative evidence of the five functions and a sound mechanism.
- It supports activity advice rather than a treatment argument.
- Allograft and scaffolds do not reproduce native damping properties.
- No clinical measurement of a patient's meniscal damping exists.
- Build the preservation argument on load distribution instead.
