# Simple Biomechanics Experiments

Six experiments on how the knee works that need no equipment beyond a phone, a
chair and a tape measure, with the safety limits for each.

## For Young Learners

### What This Is

Biomechanics is the study of how forces move a body. The knee is the easiest
joint to investigate without equipment, because almost everything it does is
visible from the outside.

The six experiments below are observation and measurement. None of them
involves loading a joint heavily, testing how far something will stretch, or
trying any movement that hurts. That is a deliberate limit and the reason the
list is short.

### Why It Matters

Reading that the knee is a hinge that also rotates is one thing. Watching your
own knee rotate while you measure it is another, and the second one stays
learned.

These experiments also produce real data for a project, which makes them more
useful than a demonstration.

### How It Works

1. The screw home movement. Sit with your knee bent, then straighten it slowly
while watching the front of your shin. The shin rotates outwards slightly in
the last part of straightening. Mark the shin with a sticker and film it to
see it clearly.

2. Range of movement. With a protractor or a phone angle application, measure
how far your knee bends and straightens. Measure the same knee five times and
look at how much your readings vary. That variation is the measurement error
every study has to deal with.

3. Standing up without using your hands. Stand from a chair with your arms
folded, then again with your feet further forward. It becomes much harder,
because the force the knee has to produce depends on where the weight sits
relative to the joint.

4. The patella as a pulley. Feel your kneecap with your leg straight and
relaxed, then with the muscle tightened. It locks into its groove. The kneecap
holds the tendon away from the joint, which increases the mechanical advantage
the muscle has.

5. Single leg balance. Stand on one leg with your eyes open, then closed. Time
both. Balance depends on information from the joint itself, and removing
vision shows how much work that information is doing.

6. Landing angle from video. Film a group stepping down from a low step.
Measure the knee angle at the moment the foot lands. People land very
differently, and the differences are consistent for each person.

### How to Prepare

Read these safety limits before starting, and apply them to every experiment
on the list.

Stop if anything hurts. Pain is the instruction to stop and does not need
interpreting.

Do not do any of these on a knee that is currently injured, swollen or
recovering. Use somebody else's.

No step higher than a normal stair for experiment six, and no jumping.

Do these with another person present, not alone, and on a non slip floor.

An adult supervises any version of these done in a classroom or a club.

For measurements on other people you need their consent, and a parent or
guardian's consent for anyone under eighteen. Record readings without names
attached.

### Common Mistakes

Changing the method partway through and keeping the earlier readings.

Measuring once. Three readings tell you something a single reading cannot,
which is how reliable your own measuring is.

Interpreting a reading clinically. A knee that bends less far than another
knee has not been diagnosed with anything, and these experiments cannot tell
you whether a joint is healthy.

Pushing a movement to find the limit. The experiments measure what a joint
does comfortably, not what it can be forced to do.

Comparing readings taken by two different people as though they were the same
measurement. They are not, and showing that is itself a good project.

### Key Learning Points

- The knee rotates slightly as it straightens, and you can see it on your own leg.
- Measuring the same thing several times shows you the error in your own measurement.
- How hard standing up is depends on where the weight sits relative to the joint.
- Stop at pain, never use an injured knee, and get consent before measuring anyone else.
- These experiments measure movement and cannot tell you whether a joint is healthy.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.
