# Menisci

Two crescent shaped pads of fibrocartilage that sit between the thigh bone and the shin bone and spread load across the knee.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

The thigh bone ends in two rounded knuckles. The shin bone is almost flat on top.
A round thing resting on a flat thing touches in only a tiny spot, and a tiny spot
means a lot of pressure.

The menisci solve that problem. They are two rubbery pads shaped like the letter
C, and they sit on top of the shin bone like a pair of shallow dishes. The
rounded end of the thigh bone settles into them. There is one on the inner side
of the knee and one on the outer side.

Each pad is thick at its outer edge and thin at its inner edge, so it works like
a wedge that stops the thigh bone sliding off the side.

### Function

Think of a drawing pin pushed into a wall. All the force goes through one sharp
point. Now think of the same push spread through your whole palm. Same force,
far less pressure on any one spot.

That is what the menisci do. They spread the squeeze from a small patch to a much
wider one, which protects the smooth white cartilage underneath. They also help
the knee feel steady when you turn, and they help the slippery joint fluid reach
the surfaces that need it.

They are not bolted down. They shift as your knee bends, which keeps the contact
area wide through the whole movement. A twist with the foot planted can tear one,
and a torn pad spreads load less well than an intact one.

### Key Learning Points

- The menisci are two C shaped pads of tough cartilage inside the knee.
- They sit between the rounded end of the thigh bone and the flat top of the shin bone.
- Their wedge shape spreads load over a wider area and adds steadiness.
- They move slightly as the knee bends, so the contact area stays wide.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

The menisci are two wedges of fibrocartilage inside the knee joint. Fibrocartilage
is tougher and more rope like than the smooth cartilage that coats the ends of the
bones, which suits it to being squeezed and stretched.

One sits on the inner side of the knee and is called the medial meniscus. It is
the larger of the two and more open in shape, closer to a letter C. The other sits
on the outer side and is called the lateral meniscus. It is smaller and more
circular.

The difference matters. The medial meniscus is firmly attached to the capsule and
to the ligament on the inner side of the knee, so it moves very little. The
lateral meniscus is more loosely held and slides further as the knee bends.

### Function

The menisci share load between the thigh bone and the shin bone, spreading force
across a wider surface so the cartilage underneath is protected. They add
stability by deepening an otherwise shallow surface. They also help spread joint
fluid, which keeps the joint slippery and feeds the cartilage.

### Clinical Relevance

Blood reaches only the outer part of each meniscus. The rim has a supply, the
middle has a partial one, and the inner portion has almost none. Clinicians
describe these as the red zone, the red-white zone and the white zone. Healing
follows the same pattern, which is why a tear at the rim behaves differently from
a tear near the centre.

Tears happen in two broad ways. A younger knee usually tears on a twist with the
foot planted. An older knee may tear gradually, sometimes with no single moment
that caused it.

Losing meniscal tissue raises the pressure on the cartilage underneath, which is
why surgeons preserve it wherever the tear allows. The choice between treatments
is covered on the surgery pages.

A scan can show a tear in a knee that does not hurt, so the examination and the
history carry as much weight as the image.

### Key Learning Points

- The medial meniscus is larger and much less mobile than the lateral meniscus.
- The menisci share load, add stability and help spread joint fluid.
- Only the outer portion has a reliable blood supply, so healing capacity varies by zone.
- Losing meniscal tissue raises contact pressure on the cartilage beneath it.
- A tear seen on a scan is not always the cause of a person's pain.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Are the menisci the same as the cartilage that wears out in arthritis?
No. They are separate structures. The menisci are the two wedge shaped pads. The
cartilage that thins in arthritis is the smooth coating on the ends of the bones.
The two are related, because damaged menisci load that coating more heavily.

**Q.** Why do some tears heal and others do not?
Blood supply. A tear at the outer rim sits in tissue with a blood supply and can
heal. A tear near the inner edge sits in tissue with almost none.

**Q.** Can I feel my menisci?
Not directly. A clinician may press along the joint line, the gap you can feel on
either side of the kneecap when the knee is bent. Tenderness there can point
towards the meniscus.

**Q.** Does everyone have the same shape of meniscus?
No. Shape varies, and a small number of people are born with a lateral meniscus
that is disc shaped rather than crescent shaped.

If your knee locks, gives way, swells quickly after an injury, or will not take
your weight, arrange to be seen by a clinician. Seek same day assessment for a hot
swollen knee with a fever.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

Each meniscus is a semilunar fibrocartilaginous structure interposed between the
femoral condyle and the tibial plateau, triangular in cross section with a thick
peripheral border and a thin free inner edge. The superior surface is concave
and articulates with the femoral condyle. The inferior surface is flat and rests
on the tibial plateau.

The medial meniscus is the larger and more open of the two and covers a smaller
proportion of its tibial plateau. The lateral is more nearly circular and covers
a greater proportion. Each is anchored to the tibia at an anterior and a
posterior root.

The ligaments and capsular attachments that tether each meniscus, and the
vessels that supply them, belong to the Anatomy Academy.

### Function

Collagen is arranged predominantly in circumferential bundles running parallel
to the periphery, with radial tie fibres crossing them. Axial load applied by
the femoral condyle tends to extrude the meniscus outwards. The circumferential
bundles resist that extrusion in tension, which is termed hoop stress, and the
roots anchor the ends so the tension can develop.

The result is load distribution: contact area increases and peak contact stress
on the articular cartilage falls. The menisci also deepen the tibial articular
surface and so contribute secondarily to joint stability, and their movement
assists distribution of synovial fluid across the articular surfaces.

Both menisci translate posteriorly during flexion, the lateral considerably
further than the medial, which reflects the difference in how firmly each is
tethered.

### Clinical Relevance

Vascular penetration is limited to the peripheral portion, producing the
described red, red-white and white zones. The inner zone is nourished by
diffusion from synovial fluid, and healing capacity follows the same gradient.
This single fact explains most of how meniscal injury behaves.

Composition is largely water, with type one collagen dominating the dry weight.
Proteoglycan content is low relative to articular cartilage, consistent with a
tissue loaded principally in tension rather than compression.

Loss of meniscal tissue reduces contact area and raises peak contact stress,
which is the mechanical basis for preferring preservation where a tear allows
it.

Magnetic resonance imaging is the standard investigation for suspected meniscal
injury. Signal grading and the choice between treatment options are covered in
the imaging and surgery sections.

### Key Learning Points

- Each meniscus is triangular in cross section, thick peripherally and thin at its free edge.
- The medial is larger and more open; the lateral is more nearly circular.
- Circumferential collagen bundles convert axial load into hoop stress, which the roots allow to develop.
- Vascularity is peripheral only, and healing capacity follows that gradient.
- Meniscal loss reduces contact area and raises peak contact stress on articular cartilage.
