# Synovium

The thin lining that makes the fluid inside the joint, and the tissue that
produces most of the swelling people notice.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

Inside your knee there is a sealed space, and that space is lined with a thin
membrane called the synovium. It is only a few cells thick in places.

The lining does not cover the smooth cartilage surfaces. It wraps around
everything else: the inside of the capsule, the folds and pockets, and even
sleeves around the ligaments in the middle of the joint.

### Function

The synovium makes synovial fluid. There is normally only a small amount, a
couple of millilitres, roughly a teaspoon at most. It is thick, clear and
slightly yellow, and it feels like egg white.

The fluid does two things. It makes the surfaces slippery, and it feeds the
cartilage, which has no blood supply of its own.

When a knee is injured or irritated, the lining makes much more fluid than
usual. That is the swelling people feel. The swelling is the lining reacting,
rather than the damage itself.

### Key Learning Points

- The synovium is the thin lining of the joint space.
- It does not cover the smooth cartilage surfaces.
- It makes synovial fluid, normally only a few millilitres.
- Swelling is the lining producing extra fluid, not the injury itself.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

The synovial membrane lines the inside of the joint capsule and every surface
within the joint except the articular cartilage and the exposed surfaces of
the menisci. It forms folds and recesses, including a large pouch above the
kneecap that extends several centimetres up the thigh.

It also forms a sleeve around the cruciate ligaments, which is why those
ligaments sit inside the joint but outside the fluid filled space.

Within the joint there are folds called plicae, which are remnants of
development. They are present in many people and usually cause no trouble.

### Function

The membrane produces synovial fluid, normally one to four millilitres in a
healthy knee. The fluid lubricates the joint and carries nutrients to the
cartilage, which has no blood supply.

The membrane itself is well supplied with blood vessels and nerves, in
contrast to the cartilage it serves.

### Clinical Relevance

Almost all knee swelling is synovial fluid, produced in excess by an irritated
lining. The timing is informative. Swelling within a few hours of injury
suggests blood in the joint, which points to a more significant structural
injury. Swelling over a day or two suggests the lining reacting.

Because the lining is richly supplied with nerves, it is a genuine source of
pain, unlike cartilage. Much of the discomfort in a swollen or arthritic knee
comes from here.

The large pouch above the kneecap is why a swollen knee looks full at the
front and above, and why clinicians sweep downwards over that area when
testing for fluid.

Conditions affecting the synovium are covered in the conditions section.

### Key Learning Points

- The synovium lines everything inside the joint except cartilage and meniscal surfaces.
- A healthy knee contains only one to four millilitres of synovial fluid.
- The fluid lubricates the joint and feeds the cartilage.
- Swelling is excess synovial fluid, and its timing after injury is informative.
- The lining is richly innervated and is a real source of pain, unlike cartilage.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is fluid on the knee the same as swelling?
Usually yes. Most knee swelling is extra synovial fluid inside the joint.
Swelling outside the joint, such as over the kneecap, is a different thing.

**Q.** Should fluid be drained?
Sometimes, to relieve pressure or to test the fluid for infection or crystals.
It is not always needed, and the fluid often returns if the cause is
untreated.

**Q.** How quickly does swelling appear after an injury?
Within a few hours suggests bleeding into the joint and a more serious injury.
Over a day or two suggests irritation of the lining.

**Q.** What is a plica?
A fold of the lining left over from development. Most people have them and
never notice. Occasionally one becomes irritated and causes symptoms.

Seek same day assessment for a hot swollen knee with a fever, and see a doctor
if swelling appeared within hours of an injury or will not settle.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The synovial membrane lines the fibrous capsule and all intracapsular surfaces
other than articular cartilage and the free surfaces of the menisci. It is one
to three cell layers thick, comprising type A macrophage like synoviocytes and
type B fibroblast like synoviocytes, overlying a vascular subintima.

The suprapatellar bursa communicates with the joint in most adults, extending
the cavity several centimetres proximally. The membrane reflects around the
cruciate ligaments, rendering them intracapsular but extrasynovial.

Developmental folds persist as plicae, most commonly suprapatellar, medial and
infrapatellar.

### Function

Type B synoviocytes secrete hyaluronan and lubricin, which confer boundary
lubrication. Type A cells are phagocytic and clear debris. Normal synovial
fluid volume in the knee is approximately one to four millilitres, is a
dialysate of plasma with added hyaluronan, and is acellular to near acellular.

The fluid provides the sole route of nutrition for articular cartilage,
exchange being driven by cyclical loading.

### Clinical Relevance

Effusion represents increased synovial fluid production and is the common
final pathway for intra-articular pathology of almost any cause. Onset within
two hours implies haemarthrosis and raises the probability of cruciate
rupture, osteochondral fracture or peripheral meniscal tear. Onset over twenty
four hours implies a reactive effusion.

Aspiration is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Fat globules in a haemarthrosis
suggest an intra-articular fracture. Cell count, Gram stain, culture and
polarised microscopy distinguish septic from crystal and inflammatory causes.

The richly innervated and vascularised synovium is a principal source of pain
and of the inflammatory mediators that drive arthrogenic muscle inhibition,
which is why effusion control is an early rehabilitation target.

Synovial pathology itself is covered in the conditions section.

### Key Learning Points

- The synovium is one to three cells thick, with type A and type B synoviocytes.
- It reflects around the cruciates, making them intracapsular but extrasynovial.
- Type B cells secrete hyaluronan and lubricin; normal volume is one to four millilitres.
- Effusion within two hours implies haemarthrosis; over twenty four hours implies reaction.
- Aspiration distinguishes septic, crystal and inflammatory causes, and fat globules suggest fracture.
