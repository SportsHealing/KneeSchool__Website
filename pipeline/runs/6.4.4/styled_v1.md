# ALL Injuries

The structure the rotational control argument turns on, where the question is no
longer whether it exists but whom to operate on for it.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

The ACL is not the only thing stopping the knee twisting. There are structures
on the outside of the knee that help. 2.11.5 covers the anterolateral complex.

One of them is called the anterolateral ligament. It was argued about for a long
time, including whether it was a separate structure at all. 2.11.3 covers the
anterolateral ligament.

When the ACL tears, these structures are often injured too, and that may be part
of why some knees still twist after an operation. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Investigations and Imaging

A small flake of bone pulled off the outside of the shin, called a Segond
fracture, is a clue that these structures were injured. 2.11.4 covers the
Segond lesion.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL is not the only structure resisting twist.
- Structures on the outside of the knee help.
- The anterolateral ligament is one of them.
- It is often injured when the ACL tears.
- A small flake of bone off the outer shin is a clue.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

The anterolateral structures sit on the outer side of the knee and help control
rotation alongside the ACL. 2.11.5 covers the anterolateral complex.

They are commonly injured at the same time as the cruciate, and their
contribution to the knee's rotational control is why they matter. 3.7.7 covers
the rotational stability.

### Investigations and Imaging

A Segond fracture, a small avulsion from the outer edge of the shin bone, is
the classical sign and is visible on an x-ray. 2.11.4 covers the Segond lesion.

The soft tissue injury itself is harder to see and is often inferred rather than
demonstrated. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

### Management Overview

Some patients have an additional procedure on the outside of the knee at the
time of cruciate reconstruction, intended to control rotation better. 6.8.1
covers graft failure.

It is not done for everybody. It is offered where the risk of the graft failing
is higher, such as in young patients returning to pivoting sport or in revision
surgery. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

It adds an incision, operative time and its own possible problems, which is why
it is selective. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The anterolateral structures help control rotation alongside the ACL.
- They are commonly injured at the same time.
- A Segond fracture is the classical visible sign.
- An additional procedure is offered to some patients, not all.
- It adds an incision, time and its own possible problems.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is the anterolateral ligament real?
Its existence as a distinct named structure was debated for years. The practical
position now is that there is a functional anterolateral complex that resists
rotation, whatever names its parts are given.

**Q.** Should I have the extra procedure?
It depends on your risk of the graft failing: your age, the sport you return to,
how lax the knee is, and whether this is a first or a revision operation. It is
a selective addition rather than a standard one.

**Q.** What does it involve?
An additional small reconstruction or tightening on the outer side of the knee,
done through a separate incision at the same operation. Techniques vary and your
surgeon will describe theirs.

**Q.** What are the downsides?
A second incision, longer surgery, and the possibility of lateral knee
discomfort or over-constraint. These are the reasons it is not done for
everyone.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

The anterolateral complex comprises the iliotibial band with its deep and
capsulo-osseous layers, the Kaplan fibres, and the anterolateral capsule
including what has been described as the anterolateral ligament. 2.11.1 covers
the iliotibial band.

Its function is resisting internal rotation of the tibia, which is the component
of instability the pivot shift reports. 3.7.9 covers the pivot shift mechanics.

Whether the anterolateral ligament is a discrete structure has been contested;
the functional role of the complex is not. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral
ligament.

### Examination

A high grade pivot shift suggests anterolateral involvement and is the clinical
correlate. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

It is the examination finding most degraded by guarding, so it is often obtained
under anaesthesia. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

### Investigations and Imaging

The Segond fracture is an avulsion of the anterolateral capsule from the
proximal lateral tibia and is pathognomonic of associated cruciate rupture.
2.11.4 covers the Segond lesion.

Soft tissue injury to the complex is inconsistently demonstrated on imaging, so
the clinical finding carries more weight here than the report does. 6.4.1 covers
bone bruising.

### Key Learning Points

- The complex includes the iliotibial band's deep layers and Kaplan fibres.
- Its function is resisting internal tibial rotation.
- The ligament's discreteness was contested; the complex's role is not.
- The Segond fracture is pathognomonic of associated cruciate rupture.
- A high grade pivot shift is the clinical correlate.

## MRCS Level

### Examination

In a viva the expected answer separates the anatomical argument from the
clinical one.

The expected points are the complex's components, its rotational role, the
Segond sign, the pivot shift correlation, and the selective nature of any added
procedure. 3.7.7 covers the rotational stability.

Say that the anatomical controversy is largely historical and the clinical
question is selection. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

Say that a high grade pivot shift under anaesthesia is the finding most likely
to change the plan. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

### Management Overview

An anterolateral procedure is added for higher risk patients rather than
routinely. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The usual risk factors cited are young age, return to pivoting sport, high grade
rotational laxity, generalised ligamentous laxity and revision surgery. 6.8.2
covers tunnel malposition.

### Complications and Prognosis

The theoretical risk is over-constraint of the lateral compartment, and lateral
pain and incision related problems are the practical ones. 6.5.1 covers
instability.

### Key Learning Points

- Separate the anatomical argument from the clinical one.
- The anatomical controversy is largely historical.
- The clinical question is selection.
- Risk factors cited are age, pivoting sport, laxity grade and revision.
- Over-constraint is the theoretical risk; lateral pain the practical one.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to have selection criteria and to
justify them.

Adding the procedure in a young pivoting athlete with high grade rotational
laxity, or in a revision, is the defensible core of current practice. 6.8.1
covers graft failure.

Adding it routinely commits every patient to a second incision for a benefit
concentrated in a subgroup. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Omitting it entirely accepts a higher failure rate in the group where failure is
most costly, which is young athletes. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Complications and Prognosis

Graft failure in a young returning athlete is the outcome the procedure exists
to reduce, and it is the outcome against which its cost is weighed. 6.8.1 covers
graft failure.

Lateral compartment over-constraint remains theoretical in most series and is
the stated reason for caution. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is exactly whom to select, since the risk factors are
agreed and their thresholds are not. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The second is which technique, since several are described and compared
inconsistently. 2.11.5 covers the anterolateral complex.

The third is whether long term lateral compartment consequences emerge with
follow up longer than the current series. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Have selection criteria and justify them.
- The defensible core is young pivoting athletes and revisions.
- Routine addition commits everybody for a subgroup benefit.
- Omitting it accepts failure where failure costs most.
- The risk factors are agreed and their thresholds are not.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this topic is a case study in how a structure moves from
contested anatomy to a procedure, and a fellow will meet it mid-journey.

The sequence ran from an anatomical dispute, through a functional consensus, to
a procedure offered selectively, and each step was shorter than the last. 2.11.3
covers the anterolateral ligament.

What has not yet happened is the long follow up that would show whether the
lateral compartment pays for the added constraint, and that is the step this
chapter cannot report on. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

A fellow's useful position is to adopt selection criteria explicitly, record
them, and be able to say which patients they would not do it for. 6.8.1 covers
graft failure.

The failure mode to avoid is adopting the procedure as a default because the
unit does it, which converts a selective indication into a routine one without
anyone deciding to. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- The sequence ran from dispute to consensus to procedure, each step shorter.
- The long follow up that would price the added constraint has not happened.
- Adopt selection criteria explicitly and record them.
- Be able to say which patients you would not do it for.
- A unit's habit can convert a selective indication into a routine one.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant judgement is where to set a threshold on evidence that supports
the direction and not the cut-off.

Setting it low treats many patients for a benefit concentrated in few, and
setting it high leaves the highest risk group with the failure the procedure
reduces. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Neither is wrong on current evidence, which means the threshold is a departmental
position that should be stated rather than a finding that can be cited. 6.1.3
covers the epidemiology.

For the individual the five things set it. The patient's age and physiology,
which is the strongest single risk factor here. Their medical comorbidities,
which bear on healing of an additional construct. The demands they put on the
knee, specifically whether they return to pivoting sport, which is the second
strongest. Their psychological state, because it governs whether they return at
all. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, including their appetite
for a second incision.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and tell the patient the
threshold is a judgement rather than a rule. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The evidence supports the direction and not the cut-off.
- A low threshold treats many for a benefit concentrated in few.
- A high threshold leaves the highest risk group exposed.
- The threshold is a departmental position rather than a citable finding.
- Age and return to pivoting sport are the two strongest individual factors.
