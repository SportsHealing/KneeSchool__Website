# Sweep Test

Moving the fluid round the joint and watching where it goes, which is the
principle all the effusion tests share.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The sweep test moves fluid from one side of the joint to the other with the hand
and reads the result, either as a visible bulge or as a palpable fullness.

It is the same principle as the bulge sign, performed as a continuous sweep
around the joint rather than as two separate strokes, and the two names are used
for overlapping techniques. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Its value is that it detects a small effusion, which the patellar tap does not.
4.8.4 covers the tap.

### How It Is Performed

Lay the patient supine with the knee extended and the quadriceps relaxed.

Place a hand below the medial joint line and sweep upwards along the medial side
and across the suprapatellar region, carrying any fluid with it.

Continue the sweep down the lateral side, so the fluid has been moved from the
medial compartment round to the lateral.

Watch and feel the medial side as you release, looking for the fluid returning
as a visible bulge or a palpable fullness.

Repeat in the opposite direction, since a knee that produces a finding in one
direction and not the other is worth checking twice.

### What a Positive Means

A visible or palpable return of fluid medially after a sweep indicates a small
effusion.

A palpable fullness without a visible bulge is a positive result in a knee whose
contours are hidden, which is why the test is read by hand as well as by eye.
4.23.4 covers those patients.

A negative sweep with a positive patellar tap indicates a larger effusion that
is already filling both sides, so nothing moves. 4.8.4 covers the tap.

A negative sweep and a negative tap in a knee the patient describes as swelling
means the swelling is extra-articular or intermittent. 4.2.3 covers swelling as
a symptom.

Nothing moving in a tense swollen knee indicates a very tense effusion, which is
a different finding. 4.8.6 covers ballottement.

### Key Learning Points

- The test moves fluid from one side of the joint to the other and reads the result.
- It shares its principle with the bulge sign and detects a small effusion.
- A palpable fullness without a visible bulge is a positive result.
- A negative sweep with a positive tap means a larger effusion that is already filling both sides.
- Nothing moving in a tense swollen knee indicates a very tense effusion.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the sweep and the bulge sign are close enough that the useful answer
describes the principle and names both.

The expected points are the extended relaxed knee, the continuous sweep from
medial to suprapatellar to lateral, the release, and reading the medial side by
eye and by hand.

Say that you would read the result by hand as well as by eye, since a palpable
fullness is the finding in a knee whose contours are hidden.

Say that you would repeat in the other direction, which takes seconds and
improves the yield.

Say that the test is for small effusions, and name the test for large ones.
4.8.6 covers ballottement.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a small volume of fluid can be displaced within a
single capsular space, so moving it and watching it return is the only way to
detect it clinically. 4.8.1 covers normal fluid physiology.

The suprapatellar pouch is the route the fluid takes between the two sides, which
is why the sweep passes across it rather than straight across the joint line.

A larger volume already occupies both sides, so there is nowhere for it to move
to, which is why the sweep fails at larger volumes and the tap succeeds.
4.8.4 covers the tap.

The parapatellar hollows are where a returning volume becomes visible, which is
why the reading is taken there. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

### How Good It Is

The sweep is among the more sensitive effusion tests and is read both visually
and by palpation, which gives it an advantage in a knee whose contours are
hidden. 4.23.4 covers those patients.

Its reproducibility is moderate, as with all the small effusion tests, and
better for the palpable than for the visual reading.

It fails at large volumes for a mechanical reason rather than a technical one,
which is why more than one test is performed. 4.8.4 covers the tap.

It cannot say what the fluid is, which no clinical test can. 4.8.7 covers the
acute haemarthrosis.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- A small volume can be displaced within a single capsular space, so moving it is how it is detected.
- The suprapatellar pouch is the route the fluid takes between the two sides.
- A larger volume already occupies both sides, so there is nowhere to move it to.
- The parapatellar hollows are where a returning volume becomes visible.
- It fails at large volumes for a mechanical reason rather than a technical one.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the sweep contributes exactly what the bulge sign does, which
is detecting the effusion that would otherwise be recorded as absent.

Its practical advantage is being readable by hand, which makes it the better
test in an obese knee where the visual reading is unavailable. 4.23.4 covers
those patients.

Recording a small effusion changes the interpretation of the range and the
quadriceps findings taken at the same visit. 4.8.2 covers the significance.

A persistent small effusion detected only by this test is the finding that moves
a patient towards an inflammatory assessment, and the move is consequential.
4.8.9 covers synovitis.

In the post-operative knee it explains a stalled range and a persisting lag at a
volume the other tests miss. 4.7.10 covers post-operative range.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is performing only a patellar tap and concluding a dry knee.
4.8.4 covers it.

The second is reading only by eye, which discards the test's advantage.

The third is performing the effusion tests after the painful parts of the
examination.

The fourth is treating a negative sweep in a tense knee as a dry knee, when
nothing can move. 4.8.6 covers ballottement.

The fifth is recording an effusion without a size. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### Key Learning Points

- The sweep detects the effusion that would otherwise be recorded as absent.
- Being readable by hand makes it the better test in an obese knee.
- Recording a small effusion changes the interpretation of the range and the quadriceps.
- A persistent small effusion moves a patient towards an inflammatory assessment.
- A negative sweep in a tense knee is not a dry knee.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this test and the bulge sign are the two that change the
record from a dry knee to an effused one, and that change is frequently the
answer.

A patient with a persistent small effusion through months of rehabilitation has
an irritated joint, and the programme cannot succeed until the cause is
addressed. 4.3.9 covers wasting.

A patient in whom only the tap was ever performed has a record of dry knees that
cannot be relied on, and repeating the assessment is the first step rather than
further imaging. 4.8.4 covers the tap.

A patient with a chronically effused knee and a popliteal cyst has a joint
producing fluid, and the cyst's behaviour follows the joint. 4.6.10 covers the
popliteal fossa.

In a knee with a scarred or partially obliterated suprapatellar pouch the fluid
cannot be swept across, so a negative result is less informative and the
previous surgery explains it. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

In medicolegal work the distinction between an effusion that was absent and one
that was not looked for is rarely recoverable from a record, and this test is
usually the one that was not performed. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- This test and the bulge sign change the record from a dry knee to an effused one.
- A persistent small effusion through rehabilitation means the programme cannot succeed yet.
- A record of dry knees based on the tap alone cannot be relied on.
- A scarred suprapatellar pouch makes a negative result less informative.
- The distinction between absent and not looked for is rarely recoverable from a record.
