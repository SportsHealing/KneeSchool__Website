# Magnification

Why a radiograph is not life size, which matters the moment anything is
measured on it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A radiographic beam diverges from a small source, so every structure is
projected larger than it is. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

How much larger depends on how far the structure sits from the detector, so two
structures in one knee are magnified by different amounts. 5.2.2 covers the
image formation.

It matters because any measurement made on a radiograph is a measurement of the
projection, and surgical planning uses those measurements. 5.16.6 covers
arthroplasty planning.

### How It Is Acquired

Magnification rises as the structure moves away from the detector, which is why
positioning decides it. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

It falls as the source is moved further away, which is why a long source to
detector distance is standard for alignment films. 5.3.6 covers long leg
alignment films.

A calibration object of known size in the beam at the right depth is what makes
a measurement usable. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

A larger focal spot blurs the projected edge as well, so sharpness and
magnification travel together. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

Rotation changes which part of a structure faces the detector, so it changes the
projected shape as well as the size. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

### Key Learning Points

- A diverging beam projects every structure larger than it is.
- Magnification depends on distance from the detector.
- Two structures in one knee are magnified differently.
- A longer source distance reduces it.
- A calibration object is what makes a measurement usable.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the page behind the answer that a radiographic measurement
needs calibration.

The expected points are the divergent beam, the distance dependence, the source
distance, the calibration object, and rotation.

Say that an uncalibrated radiographic measurement is an estimate. 5.16.6 covers
arthroplasty planning.

Say that a calibration object has to sit at the depth of the structure being
measured. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Say that an angle is less affected than a length, which is why alignment angles
travel better than component sizes. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

### How Good It Is

A radiograph measures angles well and lengths only with calibration.

Its best contribution is the angular measurement, because a projected angle in
the plane of the film is preserved where a length is not. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

It is limited for absolute size, and templating from an uncalibrated film is the
common failure. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
magnification factor or standard source distance appears either: those are local
protocol quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- An uncalibrated radiographic measurement is an estimate.
- A calibration object must sit at the depth being measured.
- An angle in the film plane is preserved where a length is not.
- Alignment angles travel between departments better than sizes.
- Templating from an uncalibrated film is the common failure.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this page decides which radiographic numbers can be trusted
into an operation.

Mechanical axis and joint line obliquity are angular and survive the projection,
which is why alignment planning works off radiographs. 5.3.9 covers joint line
obliquity.

Component templating is a length measurement and needs calibration, otherwise
the plan is systematically wrong in one direction. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty
planning.

Osteotomy correction mixes the two, since the wedge is planned from angles and
executed in millimetres. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Limb length measured from a long leg film carries the magnification of whatever
depth the limb sat at. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is templating from a film with no calibration marker. 5.16.6
covers arthroplasty planning.

A marker is included and placed at the wrong depth, which calibrates the wrong
plane. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

A length is compared between two studies taken at different source distances.
5.2.2 covers the image formation.

Rotation changes the projected shape and is read as a change in anatomy. 5.3.2
covers the lateral view.

Digital measurement tools report to a precision the projection cannot support.
5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

### Key Learning Points

- Templating from an uncalibrated film is the commonest error.
- A marker at the wrong depth calibrates the wrong plane.
- Lengths are compared across different source distances.
- Rotation changes projected shape and is read as anatomy.
- Digital tools report beyond the precision the projection supports.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level magnification is where radiographic measurement quietly
stops being quantitative.

Calibration practice varies between departments and the method is not recorded
on the image, so a measurement arriving from elsewhere has an unknown scale.
5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

A measurement that mixes an angle and a length, which osteotomy planning does,
inherits the length's uncertainty for the whole plan. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy
planning.

Computed tomography based planning removes the projection problem and introduces
dose and segmentation, which is the actual comparison rather than accuracy
against inaccuracy. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional reconstructions.

Automated measurement from radiographs is being deployed without calibration
being solved, so a precise output can rest on an unknown scale. 5.18.8 covers
artificial intelligence in imaging.

The discipline worth keeping is to state what calibrated a length whenever one
is reported, and to prefer an angle where either would answer. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

### Key Learning Points

- Calibration practice varies and is not recorded on the image.
- A plan mixing angles and lengths inherits the length's uncertainty.
- Computed tomography planning trades projection for dose and segmentation.
- Automated measurement can be precise on an unknown scale.
- Prefer an angle where either would answer, and state what calibrated a length.
