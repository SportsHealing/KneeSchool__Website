# Complete Tears

The completely torn cruciate ligament, read with the secondary signs rather than
from the ligament alone.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A complete tear is a cruciate ligament with no intact fibres bridging from femur
to tibia, so the restraint is gone. 3.7.8 covers anterior stability.

The ligament is usually visible as retracted or wavy fibres with fluid in the
gap rather than as nothing at all. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

It matters because this is the one state of the ligament that imaging and
examination agree about, which makes the secondary signs useful rather than
necessary. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

### How It Is Acquired

Look for discontinuity on the sagittal images through the notch, then confirm on
the coronal and axial planes. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Assess the line of any remaining fibres, because a flat ligament lying below the
notch roof is the line sign. 2.7.12 covers the surgical anatomy.

Measure anterior tibial translation against the femoral condyles, which is a
mechanical consequence rather than a ligament appearance. 3.7.8 covers anterior
stability.

Look at the lateral femoral condyle and the posterolateral tibia for marrow
signal, which records the impaction of the injury. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow
lesions.

Look for the associated injuries deliberately: the posteromedial meniscocapsular
junction, the meniscal roots, the anterolateral structures and the collaterals.
5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

An absent or discontinuous ligament with fluid signal filling the expected
course.

Retracted wavy fibres, which is the torn ligament rather than an empty notch.

Anterior translation of the tibia relative to the femur, with a buckled
posterior cruciate as its companion sign. 5.8.1 covers the normal posterior
cruciate.

Marrow signal in the lateral femoral condyle and the posterolateral tibia, and a
deepened lateral femoral notch. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

### Key Learning Points

- A complete tear has no fibres bridging femur to tibia.
- The torn ligament is usually visible as retracted wavy fibres.
- The line sign is a mechanical observation rather than a signal one.
- Anterior translation and a buckled posterior cruciate are companion signs.
- The associated injuries are looked for deliberately rather than noticed.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the study where the expected answer moves quickly past the
ligament to the rest of the knee.

The expected points are the discontinuity, the line, the translation, the marrow
signal, and the deliberate search for associated injuries.

Say that you would report the meniscal roots, the posteromedial corner and the
anterolateral structures as part of the same study. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

Say that the marrow signal records the mechanism as well as the injury. 3.7.14
covers the injury mechanisms.

Say that the secondary signs let you call a tear when the ligament itself is
equivocal. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that losing the ligament lets the tibia translate
forwards, so the translation and the buckled posterior cruciate are measurements
of the mechanical state rather than appearances of the lesion. 3.7.8 covers
anterior stability.

The lateral femoral condyle impacts the posterolateral tibia during the pivoting
injury, which is why the marrow signal sits in that pair of locations. 3.7.14
covers the injury mechanisms.

A deepened lateral femoral notch is the bony record of the same impaction and
persists after the oedema has gone, which makes it useful late. 5.3.14 covers
avulsion injuries.

The associated injuries matter more than the cruciate for the operation, because
the cruciate will be reconstructed either way and the meniscus and corners
change the plan. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

### How Good It Is

The complete cruciate tear is the finding magnetic resonance imaging calls most
reliably in this chapter.

Its best contribution is the associated injuries, which are the part of the
study that changes an operation. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

It is limited by the equivocal acute knee full of blood, where oedema obscures
the ligament and the secondary signs carry the reading. 6.2.5 covers the acute
haemarthrosis.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and the
comparison is the claim: the complete tear is read more reliably than the
partial tear, which is a comparison rather than a number.

### Key Learning Points

- Losing the ligament lets the tibia translate, which the image measures.
- The marrow signal pair records the pivoting impaction.
- A deepened lateral femoral notch persists after the oedema resolves.
- The associated injuries change the operation more than the cruciate does.
- The secondary signs carry the equivocal acute study.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the cruciate tear is rarely the decision, and the study is
read for everything around it.

A root tear or a ramp lesion alongside the cruciate tear changes the operation
and is the reason the study is worth reading carefully. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

Anterolateral injury alongside the cruciate tear is part of the argument for an
extra-articular procedure, which makes it a planning finding. 2.11.3 covers the
anterolateral ligament.

A collateral or corner injury changes the timing as well as the operation. 5.9.2
covers medial collateral sprains.

A deepened lateral femoral notch in a knee presenting late is evidence of the
original injury when the oedema has gone. 5.7.5 covers chronic cruciate injury.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is stopping at the cruciate, so the findings that change the
operation go unreported. 5.20.3 covers reporting cruciate injuries.

The posteromedial meniscocapsular junction is not inspected, and a ramp lesion
is missed. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Marrow signal is reported as a bruise without the mechanism being read from it.
5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

The acute haemarthrosis obscures the ligament and a tear is called indeterminate
when the secondary signs settle it. 6.2.5 covers the acute haemarthrosis.

An empty notch is reported without the retracted fibres being sought, which
loses the chance to describe the tear's level. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

### Key Learning Points

- Stopping at the cruciate loses the findings that change the operation.
- The posteromedial corner goes uninspected and a ramp lesion is missed.
- Marrow signal is reported without reading the mechanism from it.
- The secondary signs settle a study the haemarthrosis obscures.
- The level of the tear is lost when the retracted fibres are not sought.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the complete cruciate tear is the easy part of the study,
and treating it as the finding is the error the whole chapter turns on.

The reconstruction happens on the examination and the patient, so the cruciate's
appearance adds little to a decision already made. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift
test.

What the study can add is the associated injuries, and the ones that matter most
are the ones detected worst: ramp lesions, root tears and anterolateral injury
are all under-reported. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

The tear's level matters for a repair or an augmentation approach and is often
not described, because the report answers whether rather than where. 5.7.8
covers cruciate healing.

Anterior translation is measured on an unloaded supine study, so the mechanical
quantity reported is not the one the patient experiences. 5.18.5 covers weight
bearing imaging.

Reporting conventions here were set when the cruciate was the question, and the
question has moved to everything around it without the report following. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The reconstruction decision is made on examination, not appearance.
- The associated injuries that matter most are detected worst.
- The level of the tear is often not described.
- Translation is measured unloaded and supine.
- The reporting convention has not followed the question.
