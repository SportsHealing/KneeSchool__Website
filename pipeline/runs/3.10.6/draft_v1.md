# Posterolateral Corner Function

Three structures acting as one, with a muscle among them. The corner that
defeats cruciate reconstructions when it is missed.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Structures that resist the same motions and are injured together behave as one
unit, whatever their separate names.

Grouping them is useful because the clinical finding is the group's failure
rather than any member's.

A group containing a muscle is partly active, so its function changes with
whether the muscle is working.

### At the Knee

The posterolateral corner is conventionally the lateral collateral ligament,
the popliteus tendon and the popliteofibular ligament, with the posterolateral
capsule and the biceps femoris contributing. 2.10.4 covers the anatomy.

Together they resist varus, external rotation and posterior translation, with
the share of each changing through the arc. 3.10.1 and 3.10.2 cover the first
two.

The popliteus is a muscle, so part of the corner's restraint is active. 3.10.4
covers it.

The corner heals badly, which is the opposite of the medial side. 3.9.6 covers
the posteromedial corner.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes the members' shares.

Which members are injured, although they usually go together.

Alignment, since varus loads the corner in stance. 3.13 covers alignment.

Concurrent cruciate injury, which raises what the corner carries.

### Clinical Relevance

A missed corner injury is a recognised cause of cruciate graft failure, on
both cruciates, which is why it is looked for rather than noticed. 3.10.9
covers combined injury.

Patients describe giving way into hyperextension and varus, which is the
corner's characteristic account.

The peroneal nerve runs through this region, so a foot drop can accompany the
injury. 2.10.6 covers the nerve.

Poor healing is why corner injuries are reconstructed rather than braced.
Section 6 covers management.

### Key Learning Points

- Structures resisting the same motions and injured together behave as one unit.
- The corner is the lateral collateral, the popliteus tendon and the popliteofibular ligament.
- Together they resist varus, external rotation and posterior translation.
- The popliteus is a muscle, so part of the restraint is active.
- The corner heals badly, which is the opposite of the medial side.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The corner is defined by behaviour, so its membership is conventional and its
boundaries are argued about. What is not argued about is the behaviour: varus
and external rotation restraint with a posterior component. 3.9.6 covers the
medial equivalent.

Apportionment within the corner is less settled than apportionment between the
corner and the cruciates, because the members are adjacent and the sectioning
order changes the figures. 3.2.9 covers that limitation.

The active component makes the corner different from the medial one in kind as
well as in healing.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the lateral collateral the main varus restraint
near extension and the popliteofibular ligament and popliteus complex the main
external rotation restraint. 3.10.5 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

The corner contributes to posterior translation restraint, which is why a
corner injury increases posterior laxity and why a posterior cruciate injury
with a large laxity suggests corner involvement. 3.8.2 covers posterior
stability.

Hyperextension and varus thrust are the gait findings of chronic corner
deficiency, which 3.14 covers in detail.

The biceps femoris and the iliotibial band contribute dynamically and
passively, and are usually included in the wider account. 2.10.5 covers the
biceps.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle and which members are taut.

Which members are injured, and the sectioning order in a quoted study.

Alignment, which loads the corner in stance.

Cruciate integrity, particularly posterior.

### How It Is Measured

Sequential sectioning with varus, rotational and posterior loads apportions
the corner's contribution, with order dependence.

Varus stress radiography and the dial test measure the clinical consequence.
3.10.7 covers the dial test.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates the structures inconsistently,
particularly the popliteofibular ligament. 5.10 covers imaging.

Examination under anaesthetic is where the corner's findings are most reliably
obtained.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess varus, external rotation and posterior laxity together when a corner
injury is suspected.

Read a large posterior laxity as suggesting corner involvement.

Examine under anaesthetic, since the findings are subtle and the imaging
inconsistent.

Expect apportionment within the corner to be less settled than between corner
and cruciate.

### Key Learning Points

- The corner is defined by behaviour; the behaviour is not argued about.
- Apportionment within the corner is less settled than between corner and cruciates.
- The active component makes it different in kind from the medial corner.
- A large posterior laxity suggests corner involvement.
- Imaging demonstrates the popliteofibular ligament inconsistently.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the corner as the structure whose assessment belongs in every cruciate
operation. Conventional teaching identifies a missed corner injury as a
leading cause of graft failure for both cruciates. 3.10.9 covers combined
injury.

It is also the structure whose chronic deficiency is least forgiving, because
it heals badly and because varus alignment loads it. A chronic corner injury
in a varus limb needs both addressed. 3.13 covers alignment.

Reconstruction is anatomic and three component where the graft allows, and the
active function is not restored. 3.10.8 covers the construct.

### What Changes It

Whether the corner was assessed at the index cruciate operation.

Alignment, and whether an osteotomy is included.

Chronicity, since the structures stretch rather than heal.

Peroneal nerve status, which changes the operation and the counselling. 2.10.6
covers the nerve.

### How It Is Measured

Dial test and varus stress under anaesthetic at the time of cruciate surgery
is the practical assessment.

Varus stress radiography quantifies the coronal component.

Long leg standing radiography measures the alignment that loads the corner.

Revision series report corner findings at reoperation, which is the evidence
it was missed.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess the corner under anaesthetic in every cruciate reconstruction.

Correct varus alignment where a corner reconstruction would otherwise be
loaded.

Document peroneal nerve function before and after corner surgery.

Expect a chronic corner injury not to improve without surgery.

### Key Learning Points

- Corner assessment belongs in every cruciate operation.
- A missed corner injury is a leading cause of graft failure for both cruciates.
- Chronic corner deficiency is least forgiving: it heals badly and varus loads it.
- Reconstruction is anatomic and three component where graft allows.
- Revision findings are the evidence the corner was missed.
