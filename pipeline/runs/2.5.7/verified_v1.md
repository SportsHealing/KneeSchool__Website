# Root Attachments

Where the medial meniscal roots attach, what sits next to them, and why a root
is a different structure from the rest of the meniscus.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

A root is the bony attachment of a meniscal horn. The medial meniscus has two:
an anterior root and a posterior root.

The anterior root attaches in the anterior intercondylar area, in front of the
tibial attachment of the anterior cruciate ligament.

The posterior root attaches in the posterior intercondylar area, just in front
of and medial to the tibial attachment of the posterior cruciate ligament.

Both are short, broad and fibrous, and both insert into bone through a
transitional zone rather than simply resting on it.

### Relations

The anterior root lies close to the anterior cruciate's tibial footprint and
to the transverse intermeniscal ligament.

The posterior root lies close to the posterior cruciate's tibial attachment
and to the posterior root of the lateral meniscus.

Both sit in the intercondylar area rather than on the articular surface, so
neither is under the femoral condyle.

The tibial spine lies between them.

### Function

A root anchors the circumferential collagen of the meniscus. The hoop tension
generated when the wedge is loaded is transmitted into bone through the roots.

Lose a root and the hoop cannot be tensioned. The meniscus then moves outwards
under load instead of resisting, which is extrusion, and it stops transmitting
load.

That is why a root is not simply the end of the meniscus. It is the part
through which the whole mechanism works.

### Clinical Relevance

A posterior root tear produces meniscal extrusion and behaves mechanically
like a total meniscectomy, even though almost all the tissue is present.

Root tears are therefore repaired rather than debrided where the tissue
allows, and the aim is to restore the attachment rather than to tidy the tear.

The pathology belongs to 6.34 and the repair to 7.14. What belongs here is why
it matters.

### Key Learning Points

- A root is the bony attachment of a meniscal horn, inserting through a transitional zone.
- The anterior root sits in front of the anterior cruciate footprint; the posterior root in front of and medial to the posterior cruciate.
- Roots transmit hoop tension into bone, so losing one stops the mechanism.
- A posterior root tear behaves mechanically like a total meniscectomy with the tissue present.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe each root by its landmarks rather than by a coordinate. The posterior
root of the medial meniscus attaches in the posterior intercondylar area,
anterior to the posterior cruciate attachment and medial to the posterior root
of the lateral meniscus, with the medial tibial articular margin immediately
lateral to it.

The anterior root attaches in the anterior intercondylar area, anterior and
medial to the anterior cruciate tibial footprint, and the two footprints can
overlap. That overlap is the anatomy behind iatrogenic anterior root injury
during cruciate tunnel drilling.

Each root is an enthesis with the four zone structure of a direct tendon
insertion, so its failure mode is avulsion at the bone rather than tearing in
the substance.

Accessory fibres run from the posterior root region to the tibia and
occasionally to the posterior cruciate, and their presence is variable.

### Relations

Posterior root: posterior cruciate attachment behind, lateral meniscal
posterior root laterally, medial articular margin laterally and slightly
anteriorly, and the posterior capsule behind all of it with the popliteal
vessels beyond.

Anterior root: anterior cruciate footprint behind and laterally, transverse
intermeniscal ligament continuing laterally, and the fat pad in front.

The roots are the only parts of the meniscus with a direct bony blood supply,
which makes them better vascularised than the adjacent body.

Both lie in a region crowded with other attachments, which is covered on
2.2.3.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Root tissue receives vessels from the intercondylar area through the bony
attachment and from the perimeniscal plexus peripherally. It is therefore
among the better supplied meniscal tissue.

That vascularity is the reason root repair heals at all, and it is one of the
arguments for repairing rather than resecting.

Mechanoreceptors are concentrated in the horns and roots, so a root carries
proprioceptive as well as mechanical function. 2.7.10 covers the equivalent
for the cruciate.

### Function

The root converts the meniscus from a loose wedge into a tensioned ring. The
whole load sharing mechanism on 2.5.4 depends on both roots being attached.

Partial root detachment reduces hoop tension in proportion to the fibres lost,
so root pathology is graded rather than binary, and partial lesions matter.

Because the posterior root resists the posterior horn being pushed outwards
and backwards, its loss also reduces the horn's contribution to
anteroposterior stability.

### Clinical Relevance

Recognise posterior root tear as a distinct entity rather than as a posterior
horn tear. The imaging sign is extrusion with a vertical gap at the root on
coronal images, and 5.6 owns the criteria.

The anterior root's overlap with the anterior cruciate footprint means
cruciate reconstruction can injure it, and that is a recognised iatrogenic
lesion rather than a theoretical risk.

Repair aims to restore the footprint, so fixation is placed at the anatomical
attachment and not simply nearby. 7.14 owns the technique.

In the varus knee, root failure and progressive extrusion accelerate medial
compartment loading, which links root pathology to alignment.

### Key Learning Points

- Describe a root by its landmarks: the posterior medial root is anterior to the posterior cruciate and medial to the lateral root.
- The anterior root footprint can overlap the anterior cruciate's, which is how tunnel drilling injures it.
- A root is an enthesis, so it avulses at bone rather than tearing in substance.
- Roots have a direct bony blood supply and are among the better supplied meniscal tissue.
- Partial root detachment reduces hoop tension in proportion, so partial lesions matter.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The crowding of the posterior intercondylar area is the operative problem. The
medial root, the lateral root and the posterior cruciate attachment are within
a small space, and fixation for one can compromise another.

Behind all of them is capsule and then the popliteal artery, which is the
constraint on any transtibial tunnel directed posteriorly.

### Function

Root repair restores a mechanism, which is a different claim from restoring
tissue continuity. The test of success is whether extrusion resolves and the
hoop tensions, not whether the tissue has stuck together.

That distinction explains why anatomical footprint restoration matters: a root
fixed medial or anterior to its footprint shortens the meniscus and tensions
the hoop in the wrong direction.

### Clinical Relevance

In a posterior root tear with a correctable varus deformity, address both.
Repairing a root in a limb that will keep overloading it is a predictable
failure, and the reasoning is anatomical.

In combined cruciate and root pathology, plan tunnel position with the root
footprints in mind, because the space is shared.

In revision, a previously fixed root may have healed in a non anatomical
position, and the extrusion on imaging will say so.

All published root footprint descriptions are quantitative, given as distances
from the cruciate attachments and the articular margins. None appears here,
and that absence is most costly on this page and on 2.2.3. Decision 006
records why, and the figure register is where a reviewer should add them.

### Key Learning Points

- The posterior intercondylar area is crowded; fixation for one structure can compromise another.
- Root repair restores a mechanism, and the test is whether extrusion resolves.
- A root fixed off its footprint shortens the meniscus and tensions the hoop wrongly.
- Repairing a root in a limb that will keep overloading it is a predictable failure.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

Four decisions that turn on root anatomy.

Repair or resect a posterior root tear. The anatomical argument for repair is
that the tissue is vascular and the mechanism is recoverable. The argument
against, in a particular knee, is cartilage already lost: restoring the hoop
helps a joint that still has a surface to protect.

Simultaneous osteotomy. In the varus knee with a root tear, the root and the
alignment are one problem. Section 7 owns the combination; the anatomy says
they cannot be separated.

Tunnel planning in combined injury. The posterior intercondylar area holds
both roots and the posterior cruciate. Where all three need addressing, the
order and the trajectories are planned together, and the posterior capsule and
artery set the limit.

The iatrogenic anterior root. Cruciate tunnel placement that respects the
cruciate footprint can still take part of the anterior root, because the
footprints overlap. Recognising it at the time allows repair; finding it later
usually does not.

The anatomy on this page does not change between these four. What changes is
how much of the mechanism is left to protect.

### Key Learning Points

- Restoring the hoop helps a joint that still has a surface to protect.
- In the varus knee the root and the alignment are one problem.
- The posterior intercondylar area holds both roots and the posterior cruciate, so trajectories are planned together.
- An iatrogenic anterior root injury can be repaired if recognised at the time, and usually not later.
