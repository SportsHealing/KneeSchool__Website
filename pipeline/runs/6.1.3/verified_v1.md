# Epidemiology

Every count here describes who was diagnosed rather than who tore a ligament,
and the two are not the same group.

## For Young Learners

### Epidemiology

ACL tears are one of the most common serious knee injuries in sport. 6.1.1
covers what the ACL is.

They happen most in sports with turning, stopping and landing: football,
netball, rugby, skiing, basketball. 0.2.4 covers girls, boys and knees.

Girls and women tear the ACL more often than boys and men playing the same
sport, and the reasons are still being worked out. 6.6.1 covers the female
epidemiology.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Most tears happen without anyone touching the player. 6.1.5 covers the
mechanisms of injury.

Someone who has torn one ACL is more likely than average to tear the other one
or the same one again. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Warm up programmes that train landing and turning lower the risk, and they work
best when a whole squad does them through the season. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- ACL tears are among the commonest serious sports knee injuries.
- Turning, stopping and landing sports carry the most risk.
- Girls and women tear the ACL more often than boys and men in the same sport.
- Most tears involve no contact with another player.
- Programmes that train landing and turning lower the risk.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Epidemiology

ACL injury is common enough that most clinics see it weekly, and it is
concentrated in people who play pivoting sport. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

There are two groups of patients rather than one. Younger people injured in
competitive sport, and older recreational athletes who are increasingly injured
and increasingly treated. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Women are affected more often than men in the same sport, which is a consistent
finding rather than a disputed one. 6.6.1 covers the female epidemiology.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Previous ACL injury is the strongest single predictor of another one, in either
knee. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The sport and the level played matter more than general fitness. 6.1.5 covers
the mechanisms of injury.

No figure appears anywhere on this page. Rates, risks and ratios are the content
a reader comes to an epidemiology page for, and every one of them is a published
quantity that nothing in this build has checked. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

### Key Learning Points

- ACL injury is concentrated in pivoting sport.
- There are two patient groups, younger competitive and older recreational.
- Women are affected more often than men in the same sport.
- Previous ACL injury is the strongest predictor of another.
- This page carries no rates, because none has been checked.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why does this page give no numbers?
Because none of them has been checked against a source in this build. An
incidence written from memory looks exactly like one taken from a registry, and
the wrong one is worse than none.

**Q.** Is the injury getting more common?
Recorded injuries have risen. How much of that is more injury and how much is
more scanning and more reporting is genuinely contested, so a rise in recorded
cases is not the same claim as a rise in injuries.

**Q.** Does this happen outside sport?
Yes, though less often. Falls, road traffic injuries and workplace twists all
produce it, and those patients are often older and more likely to have other
injuries alongside.

**Q.** If women are at higher risk, should girls avoid pivoting sport?
No. The risk is managed by training rather than by avoidance, and the health
benefits of the sport are not in question. Prevention programmes exist precisely
so the sport can be played.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Epidemiology

The age distribution is bimodal: a large peak in adolescence and early adult
life, and a second, growing group of older recreational athletes. 6.7.1 covers
skeletally immature patients.

The sex difference is consistent across pivoting sports when exposure is
accounted for, and accounting for exposure is the step that separates a real
finding from a participation artefact. 6.6.1 covers the female epidemiology.

Concomitant injury is the rule rather than the exception, which is why an ACL
diagnosis starts a search instead of ending one. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Sport type and level of play dominate, because they set exposure to the
mechanism. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Previous ipsilateral or contralateral injury raises risk substantially, and the
contralateral knee is the one most often forgotten. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Anatomical factors including intercondylar notch dimensions and posterior tibial
slope are recognised, and they are the part most often overstated in
undergraduate teaching. 6.6.3 covers the neuromuscular factors.

### Key Learning Points

- The age distribution is bimodal.
- The older recreational group is growing.
- The sex difference holds when exposure is accounted for.
- Concomitant injury is the rule, so the diagnosis starts a search.
- The contralateral knee is the one most often forgotten.

## MRCS Level

### Epidemiology

In a viva the expected answer describes the distribution and names its
limitations rather than reciting a rate.

The expected points are the bimodal age distribution, the sex difference with
exposure accounted for, the dominance of sport type, the strength of previous
injury as a predictor, and the near universality of associated injury. 6.4.3
covers meniscal root tears.

Say that registry and insurance data count procedures rather than injuries, so
they describe treated patients. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Say that an untreated tear in somebody who stops playing may never be counted at
all. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Investigations and Imaging

Case ascertainment depends on access to magnetic resonance imaging, so an
epidemiology built on scanned patients inherits whoever gets scanned. 5.7.1
covers the normal ACL.

A tear diagnosed late by a clinician who examines carefully never enters a
dataset built from acute scans. 4.10.3 covers chronic ACL deficiency.

That makes the denominator the hardest part of every claim on this page, and the
part most often left unstated. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

### Key Learning Points

- Describe the distribution and name its limitations.
- Registries count procedures rather than injuries.
- An untreated tear in somebody who stops playing may never be counted.
- Ascertainment inherits whoever gets scanned.
- The denominator is the hardest part of every claim here.

## FRCS Level

### Epidemiology

At examination level epidemiology is asked in order to justify a service
decision, so the useful content is what the numbers can and cannot support.

Population counts support capacity planning and prevention programme targeting,
because both operate on groups. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

They support an individual prognosis poorly, because the variance within any
group is wider than the difference between groups. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

Trends in recorded injury conflate incidence with ascertainment, and separating
the two needs a denominator nobody collects routinely. 6.2.1 covers non-contact
injury.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is how much of the rise in recorded ACL injury is real.
6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

The second is whether the sex difference is primarily anatomical, hormonal,
neuromuscular or a product of training history, with the practical consequence
being what a prevention programme should target. 6.6.2 covers the hormonal
factors.

The third is whether the older recreational group should be counselled from data
gathered on young athletes, which is most of the data. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.6.1 covers the female epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Counts support capacity planning and prevention targeting.
- They support individual prognosis poorly.
- Within group variance exceeds between group difference.
- Trends conflate incidence with ascertainment.
- Most data describing this injury was gathered on young athletes.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the epidemiology that matters is of outcome rather than of
injury, and it is thinner than the injury data.

Re-rupture and contralateral rupture are the outcomes that change what a patient
is told, and they vary with age and with return to pivoting sport more than with
technique. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Return to the previous level of sport is reported in a way that mixes several
different questions, so two studies using the phrase can mean different things.
6.5.1 covers instability.

Long term joint outcome is the part most patients ask about and the part with
the longest follow up requirement, which is why it is the thinnest evidence of
all. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

A fellow should present all three as ranges with their sources named, and this
page can supply neither, which is the clearest illustration in the chapter of
what source access would change. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Outcome epidemiology is thinner than injury epidemiology.
- Re-rupture varies with age and return to pivoting more than with technique.
- Return to sport is reported as one phrase covering several questions.
- Long term joint outcome has the longest follow up requirement.
- This page can supply none of the three, which is what source access buys.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant use of this page is in counselling and in service design, and the
two want different things from the same data.

For counselling, population data sets the frame and stops there, because the
person in clinic is not a population. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

For service design, it is the whole basis, and the question a department can
actually answer is whether its own case mix and results match what it quotes.
6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Where the two meet is prevention, which is bought at population level and
delivered to individuals, and whose cost is borne by clubs and schools rather
than by the service that benefits. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

A decision for an individual still turns on the same five things. Their age and
physiology, which set healing and tissue quality. Their medical comorbidities,
because smoking, diabetes and body mass change operative risk without changing
the mechanics. The demands they put on the knee. Their psychological state. And
their own ideas, concerns and expectations. None of the five is in any registry.
6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

### Key Learning Points

- Counselling takes the frame from population data and stops there.
- Service design takes its whole basis from it.
- The answerable departmental question is whether local results match the quote.
- Prevention is bought at population level and delivered to individuals.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations are in no registry.
