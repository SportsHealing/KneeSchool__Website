# Anisotropy

A normal tendon made to look torn by the angle of the probe, which is the
commonest false positive in knee ultrasound.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A tendon is made of parallel fibres, so it reflects sound strongly when the beam
strikes it squarely and weakly when the beam is oblique. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

Tilting the probe therefore makes a normal tendon darker, and a dark tendon is
what a tear looks like. 5.4.6 covers the patellar tendon.

It matters because this is an operator induced appearance that mimics pathology,
and it is produced by the single commonest technical error. 5.1.6 covers imaging
pitfalls.

### How It Is Acquired

Keep the beam perpendicular to the fibres, which is the whole technique. 5.2.8
covers reflection.

Follow the tendon along its length and correct the angle as the tendon curves,
because a curved tendon cannot be perpendicular everywhere at once. 5.4.5 covers
the quadriceps tendon.

Test any dark area by angling onto it, since the appearance of anisotropy
brightens and a tear does not. 5.4.6 covers the patellar tendon.

Confirm in a second plane, where a tear persists and an angle effect does not.
5.4.7 covers the hamstring tendons.

Note that the same physics applies to ligaments, which is why the collateral
appearances behave the same way. 5.4.9 covers the MCL.

### Key Learning Points

- A parallel fibred tendon reflects strongly only when struck squarely.
- Tilting the probe darkens a normal tendon.
- A dark tendon is what a tear looks like.
- Anisotropy brightens when angled onto, and a tear does not.
- A curved tendon cannot be perpendicular everywhere at once.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the artefact to name when asked what makes knee ultrasound
operator dependent.

The expected points are perpendicularity, following the curve, the angle test,
the second plane, and ligaments behaving the same way.

Say that this is the commonest false positive in knee ultrasound. 5.4.6 covers
the patellar tendon.

Say that the angle test resolves it at the time of scanning. 5.2.8 covers
reflection.

Say that the magnetic resonance equivalent is the magic angle effect, which
arises from different physics and produces a comparable error. 5.2.20 covers the
magic angle effect.

### How Good It Is

Anisotropy is the reason an ultrasound study's reliability depends on the person
holding the probe more than on the machine.

Its best feature is that it is completely resolvable during the examination by
an operator who knows it. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

It is limited as an explanation to fibrillar structures, which in the knee means
the tendons and the collaterals. 5.4.9 covers the MCL.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no critical
angle appears either: the angle at which the effect appears depends on the
structure and the equipment, and this environment cannot check published values.

### Key Learning Points

- This is the commonest false positive in knee ultrasound.
- The angle test resolves it during the examination.
- The magnetic resonance analogue is the magic angle effect.
- It applies to tendons and collaterals, the fibrillar structures.
- Reliability depends on the operator more than on the machine.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this page is the reason a tendon tear reported on ultrasound
should be confirmed before it is operated on.

A reported partial quadriceps or patellar tendon tear in a functioning knee is
the classic presentation of this artefact. 5.4.5 covers the quadriceps tendon.

A complete rupture is a clinical diagnosis with a palpable gap and a functional
deficit, so the imaging adds little and the artefact matters less. 5.4.6 covers
the patellar tendon.

The discriminating question is whether the dark area persists through the angle
test, and the answer sits with the operator rather than in the images. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

A magnetic resonance study resolves a genuinely doubtful case, with its own
angle dependent trap. 5.2.20 covers the magic angle effect.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting anisotropy as a partial tendon tear. 5.4.6
covers the patellar tendon.

A curved segment of tendon is darker than the rest and is read as focal
pathology. 5.4.5 covers the quadriceps tendon.

A collateral ligament is reported as sprained from the same effect. 5.4.9 covers
the MCL.

The angle test is not performed and the appearance is recorded as a finding.
5.2.8 covers reflection.

A second plane is not obtained. 5.4.7 covers the hamstring tendons.

### Key Learning Points

- Anisotropy is reported as a partial tendon tear.
- A curved segment reads as focal pathology.
- A collateral is reported as sprained from the same effect.
- The angle test is skipped and the appearance becomes a finding.
- A second plane is not obtained.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level anisotropy and the magic angle effect are the same lesson
arriving through two different physics, and that pairing is worth holding.

Both make a normal fibrillar structure look abnormal because of its orientation
to the imaging system, and both are resolved by changing the relationship rather
than by looking harder. 5.2.20 covers the magic angle effect.

Both are well described and both continue to generate reports of tears in intact
tendons, which says the problem is in how the examination is conducted and
reported rather than in what is known. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Ultrasound's version is resolvable live and unresolvable afterwards, which makes
the report's silence about the angle test a real loss of information. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

Automated tendon assessment is being developed on image sets that contain this
artefact labelled as pathology, which will reproduce it. 5.18.8 covers
artificial intelligence in imaging.

The general principle, which runs through this chapter, is that an imaging
appearance is a relationship between the tissue and the instrument rather than a
property of the tissue. 5.2.6 covers sound waves.

### Key Learning Points

- Two different physics produce the same orientation dependent error.
- Both are resolved by changing the relationship, not by looking harder.
- Both are well described and both still generate reports of false tears.
- The report's silence about the angle test loses real information.
- An imaging appearance is a relationship between tissue and instrument.
