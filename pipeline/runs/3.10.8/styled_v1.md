# PLC Reconstruction Biomechanics

Three limbs to restore and a crowded fibula to fix them to. The active
function cannot be replaced, and the alignment usually has to be corrected
too.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Replacing a group of structures means replacing each one's job, in its own
direction, at its own position.

A group containing a muscle cannot be fully replaced, because a graft is
passive. Part of the function is lost whatever is done.

If the limb's alignment is loading the group, replacing the group without
correcting the alignment repeats the original failure.

### At the Knee

Anatomic posterolateral corner reconstruction replaces the lateral collateral
ligament, the popliteus tendon and the popliteofibular ligament. 3.10.6 covers
the corner.

Each limb has its own attachment positions and its own orientation, because
each restrains a different combination. 3.10.5 covers the popliteofibular
ligament.

The fibular head takes two of the fixations and is a small, crowded piece of
bone. 2.10.7 covers the surgical anatomy.

The popliteus muscle's active roles are not restored, so unlocking and lateral
meniscal retraction are permanently altered. 3.10.4 covers the complex.

### What Changes It

Which limbs are reconstructed and where they attach.

Fibular bone quality, which decides the fixation.

Alignment, since varus loads the reconstruction. 3.13 covers alignment.

Peroneal nerve position, which constrains the dissection. 2.10.6 covers the
nerve.

### Clinical Relevance

Corner injuries are reconstructed rather than braced because they heal badly,
which is the opposite of the medial side. Section 6 covers management.

Correcting a varus limb is part of the operation in a chronic injury rather
than a later consideration.

The peroneal nerve is at risk, and its function is documented before and
after. 2.10.6 covers the nerve.

7.45 covers the reconstruction technique.

### Key Learning Points

- Replacing a group means replacing each job, direction and position.
- A group containing a muscle cannot be fully replaced by a graft.
- Anatomic reconstruction replaces three structures with three limbs.
- The fibular head takes two fixations and is small and crowded.
- The popliteus muscle's active roles are not restored.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The construct's job is to restore varus restraint near extension and external
rotation restraint through mid flexion, which are two different directions at
two different angles. 3.10.1 and 3.10.2 cover them.

Anatomic and non anatomic approaches differ in whether they reproduce the
native attachments. A tenodesis restrains a motion; an anatomic reconstruction
reproduces a structure.

Limb orientation is as important as attachment position, because a limb in the
wrong orientation restrains the wrong motion. 3.10.5 covers that point.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching describes an anatomic reconstruction with femoral
tunnels at the native lateral collateral and popliteus origins, a fibular
tunnel for the collateral and popliteofibular limbs, and a tibial tunnel for
the popliteus limb.

The two femoral origins are close together, so tunnel convergence and bone
bridge are technical constraints. 2.10.7 covers the surgical anatomy.

Graft tensioning is conventionally performed with the knee in slight flexion
and the tibia in neutral or slight internal rotation, to avoid fixing an
externally rotated position.

Conventional teaching reports anatomic reconstruction restoring varus and
rotational restraint closer to the intact state than a fibular based tenodesis
in cadaveric testing.

### What Changes It

Attachment positions and limb orientations.

Tensioning position and rotation of the tibia at fixation.

Fibular and tibial bone quality.

Alignment and concurrent cruciate reconstruction.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares constructs against the intact knee in varus and
rotation.

Varus stress radiography measures the coronal result clinically.

The dial test measures the rotational result without an instrument. 3.10.7
covers it.

Postoperative imaging confirms tunnel positions and convergence.

### Clinical Relevance

Reproduce attachment positions and limb orientations, since both decide what
is restrained.

Fix with the tibia in neutral or slight internal rotation rather than
externally rotated.

Plan the femoral tunnels to avoid convergence and preserve the bone bridge.

Measure the coronal result radiographically and the rotational one by dial
test.

### Key Learning Points

- The construct restores two directions at two different angles.
- A tenodesis restrains a motion; an anatomic reconstruction reproduces a structure.
- Limb orientation matters as much as attachment position.
- The two femoral origins are close, so convergence and bone bridge constrain the tunnels.
- Fix with the tibia neutral or slightly internally rotated.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the reconstruction as a construct in an environment, where the
environment is varus alignment and the cruciates. A corner reconstruction in
an uncorrected varus limb carries the load that stretched the original. 3.7.20
covers that framing.

Conventional teaching corrects varus alignment, by proximal tibial or distal
femoral osteotomy as the deformity dictates, before or with the reconstruction
in a chronic case. 3.13 covers alignment.

The peroneal nerve is the complication that defines the operation's risk
profile, and documenting its function before surgery is part of the consent
rather than a formality. 2.10.6 covers the nerve.

### What Changes It

Alignment and whether an osteotomy is included.

Which limbs are reconstructable and the graft available.

Cruciate status and the sequence of reconstruction.

Previous surgery and the state of the fibular head.

### How It Is Measured

Long leg standing radiography measures the alignment that loads the construct.

Varus stress radiography before and after measures the coronal result.

Dial test under anaesthetic measures the rotational result.

Revision series report what was found at reoperation, which is the evidence
for the environmental factors.

### Clinical Relevance

Correct varus alignment before or with a chronic corner reconstruction.

Document peroneal nerve function before surgery and after.

Reconstruct all three limbs where the graft and bone allow.

Check the dial test under anaesthetic before leaving theatre.

### Key Learning Points

- The environment is varus alignment and the cruciates.
- A corner reconstruction in an uncorrected varus limb carries the load that stretched the original.
- Correct varus by osteotomy before or with reconstruction in a chronic case.
- The peroneal nerve defines the operation's risk profile.
- Document nerve function before surgery as part of consent.

## Fellowship Level

### At the Knee

The fellowship problem is the multiligament knee with a varus deformity and a
stretched corner, where three or four reconstructions and an osteotomy are all
indicated and cannot all be done at once.

Staging is the main tool, and the sequence follows the mechanics: correct the
environment first where it is driving the failure, reconstruct the structures
that others depend on, and accept that a single sitting may not be possible.
3.8.8 covers the comparable posterior problem.

Tunnel convergence is the specific geometric constraint, because a corner
reconstruction's fibular and tibial tunnels compete with a posterior cruciate
tibial tunnel. 3.8.10 covers the inlay option that avoids one.

### What Changes It

Number of reconstructions indicated and graft available.

Tunnel trajectories and convergence risk.

Degree and level of deformity.

Peroneal nerve status, which can make an extensive dissection unwise.

### How It Is Measured

Computed tomography planning assesses tunnel trajectories and convergence.

Long leg radiography plans the correction.

Multiligament series report outcomes in small heterogeneous groups.

No measurement establishes the optimal sequence for a given combination.

### Clinical Relevance

Plan tunnel trajectories before theatre in a multiligament corner
reconstruction.

Correct the deformity that is driving the failure first.

Consider staging where graft, time or soft tissues will not allow one sitting.

Consider an inlay for the posterior cruciate to free tibial tunnel space.

### Key Learning Points

- The problem is a multiligament knee with a varus deformity and a stretched corner.
- Staging is the main tool and the sequence follows the mechanics.
- Corner tunnels compete with a posterior cruciate tibial tunnel.
- An inlay for the posterior cruciate frees tibial tunnel space.
- No measurement establishes the optimal sequence.
