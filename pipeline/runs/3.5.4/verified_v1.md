# Effects of Flexion

Contact moves backwards and gets smaller as the knee bends. Deep flexion is a
small area carrying a large force, which is the worst combination.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

If the contact area changes through the range, so does the pressure, even with
the force held constant.

The femoral condyle is not a circle. It has a larger radius at the front and a
smaller one behind, so the part in contact changes shape as the knee bends.

A smaller radius pressed into a surface gives a smaller contact area. That is
why deep flexion concentrates load.

### At the Knee

Contact migrates posteriorly on both the femur and the tibia as the knee
flexes, which follows from the roll and glide relationship. 3.3.2 covers it.

Contact area falls as flexion deepens, because the posterior condyles are more
sharply curved than the distal ones.

Joint reaction force rises with flexion in loaded tasks, because the extensor
demand rises. 3.4.3 covers it.

Area falling while force rises means pressure rises on both counts, which is
the mechanical account of why deep squatting is demanding.

### What Changes It

The activity, since flexion under load is what matters rather than flexion
alone.

Meniscal integrity, particularly the posterior horns, which are the part
loaded in flexion. 3.6 covers meniscal biomechanics.

Condylar shape, which varies between people.

Whether the knee is bearing weight, since an unloaded flexed knee has no
pressure problem.

### Clinical Relevance

Deep flexion under load is the position most likely to provoke posterior
compartment symptoms, which is why squatting and kneeling appear in the
history.

Posterior horn pathology presents in deep flexion rather than in the mid range
that most testing samples. Section 4 covers the tests.

Advising a patient to limit loaded deep flexion is a mechanical instruction
with a stated target. Section 6 covers management.

A pressure figure is meaningless without its flexion angle.

### Key Learning Points

- Contact area changes through the range, so pressure does too.
- The posterior condyles have a smaller radius than the distal ones.
- Contact migrates posteriorly and the area falls as flexion deepens.
- Joint force rises with flexion in loaded tasks, so pressure rises on both counts.
- A pressure figure is meaningless without its flexion angle.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Three things change together with flexion: contact position, contact area and
joint reaction force. A statement about one at a given angle needs the other
two.

The patellofemoral joint behaves oppositely in one respect. Its contact area
grows with flexion, which partly offsets its steeply rising force. 3.12 covers
it.

The tibiofemoral joint has no such compensation, so its stress rises with
flexion more straightforwardly.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching puts tibiofemoral contact area at its greatest near
extension and describes a fall through flexion, with the posterior condylar
contact in deep flexion the smallest.

The medial and lateral sides differ in how far contact migrates. The lateral
contact point travels considerably further, which follows from the kinematics.
3.3.2 covers it.

Posterior horn loading rises with flexion in both compartments, which is the
mechanical basis of posterior horn tear patterns.

The relationship is not linear. Conventional teaching places the steepest
changes beyond roughly 90 degrees, where the posterior condyles are fully
engaged.

### What Changes It

Posterior condylar geometry and offset, which set the contact in deep flexion.

Meniscal posterior horn status, which determines whether the deep flexion
contact is shared.

Tibial slope, which shifts the whole relationship.

Load, which rises with flexion in closed chain and not in open chain. 3.3.3
and 3.3.4 cover the chains.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric rigs with sensors at a series of flexion angles are the usual method
and give a curve made of static points.

Loaded magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography at chosen angles
measures position in vivo, without a continuous record.

Deep flexion rigs are technically difficult, which is why the deep flexion
literature is thinner than the mid range one.

Fluoroscopy tracks the contact position dynamically and does not measure area
or stress.

### Clinical Relevance

Record the flexion angle with any contact measurement, since all three
quantities move with it.

Expect posterior horn symptoms in deep flexion and absence of findings in mid
range testing.

Treat the thinness of the deep flexion literature as a limit on confidence
rather than as reassurance.

Distinguish loaded from unloaded flexion when advising on activity.

### Key Learning Points

- Contact position, area and joint force all change with flexion together.
- The patellofemoral contact area grows with flexion; the tibiofemoral falls.
- The lateral contact point travels considerably further than the medial.
- Posterior horn loading rises with flexion in both compartments.
- The steepest changes come beyond roughly 90 degrees, by conventional teaching.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat deep flexion as a design constraint with a stated cost. An implant that
permits deep flexion loads a small posterior contact area, and the question is
whether the bearing and the fixation tolerate it.

Posterior condylar offset is the technical variable that decides both the
achievable flexion and the contact in deep flexion, which is why it is
restored rather than approximated. 3.3.11 covers squatting.

In the native knee the same relationship underlies posterior horn root tear
mechanics: the structure under the highest deep flexion load is the one that
fails. 6.34 covers root tears.

### What Changes It

Posterior condylar offset and component sizing, in a replaced knee.

Insert conformity and material, which set the area available and its
tolerance.

Posterior horn and root integrity, in a native knee.

Tibial slope, native or surgically created, which shifts the whole arc.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric and simulator contact studies compare designs through the arc under
a repeatable load.

Retrieval and wear analysis shows where deep flexion load actually fell, after
the event.

Lateral radiography measures offset and slope, which are the technical
proxies.

Patient reported deep flexion ability is the outcome, and it correlates
imperfectly with measured range.

### Clinical Relevance

Restore posterior condylar offset rather than accepting an approximation.

Expect the deep flexion contact area to be small, and choose the bearing
accordingly.

Repair posterior horn roots where feasible, on deep flexion loading grounds.

Account for slope when a knee's deep flexion behaviour is unexpected.

### Key Learning Points

- Deep flexion loads a small posterior contact area; the bearing has to tolerate it.
- Posterior condylar offset decides both achievable flexion and deep flexion contact.
- The structure under the highest deep flexion load is the posterior horn root.
- Retrieval analysis shows where deep flexion load actually fell.
- Measured range and reported deep flexion ability correlate imperfectly.
