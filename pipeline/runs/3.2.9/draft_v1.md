# Coupled Motion

The knee's motions do not occur singly. Flexion carries rotation with it, and
anterior translation carries internal rotation with it.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Coupled motion means that moving a joint in one degree of freedom produces
motion in another, without any separate effort to create it.

Coupling is a consequence of shape and of attachment. Curved surfaces of
unequal radius cannot slide past each other without turning, and a ligament
fixed at two points cannot allow one motion without limiting a second.

The six degrees of freedom described in 3.2.1 are therefore a vocabulary
rather than a list of independent movements. The knee speaks in combinations.

### At the Knee

Flexion and extension couple with axial rotation. The tibia rotates internally
as the knee flexes and externally as it extends, which is the screw home
mechanism described in 3.2.2.

Anterior translation couples with internal rotation. Pulling the tibia forward
does not move it straight forward, because the lateral side travels further
than the medial.

Varus and valgus angulation couples with rotation, since opening one side of
the joint unloads one femoral condyle and lets the tibia turn beneath it.

Flexion also couples with the amount of every other motion available. Rotation
and translation are both small near full extension and both larger in mid
flexion.

### What Changes It

Surface shape. The lateral femoral condyle is more curved and the lateral
tibial plateau convex, so the lateral side moves further for the same flexion
and drags rotation with it.

Ligament integrity. A divided restraint removes a limit in its own direction
and in the coupled direction at the same time.

Meniscal integrity, because the menisci guide where each condyle sits and a
lost posterior horn lets the condyle travel differently.

Load and muscle activity, which compress the surfaces together and make the
coupling tighter.

### Clinical Relevance

A test that applies force in one direction measures the coupled result, not a
pure translation. The anterior drawer produces a rotating tibia.

The pivot shift is the clearest example in clinical use. It is a coupled event
rather than a single motion, and Section 4 covers the test.

Patients describe coupled failure as the knee giving way rather than as the
knee sliding forward, because what they feel is the sudden rotation.

A reconstruction that restores translation without restoring rotation can
leave a patient with the symptom they came with. 3.11 covers that.

### Key Learning Points

- Coupled motion means one movement produces another without separate effort.
- Coupling comes from surface shape and from ligament attachment.
- Flexion couples with internal rotation; anterior translation couples with internal rotation.
- The lateral side travels further, which is the source of most knee coupling.
- The pivot shift is a coupled event, not a single motion.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Describe coupling as a constraint on the solution rather than as a motion of
its own. The knee has six degrees of freedom but far fewer independent ones in
function, because shape and ligament length remove the rest.

The result is a path. For any flexion angle the intact knee adopts a narrow
range of rotation and translation, and that path is reproducible within an
individual.

An injury widens the path rather than adding a new motion. This is why laxity
is described as increased rather than as abnormal in kind.

### At the Knee

Medial and lateral sides are not equivalent. The medial condyle translates
little through mid flexion while the lateral translates considerably, which
produces a rotation about a medially placed axis. 3.3 covers the surface
motion in detail.

Coupling of anterior translation with internal rotation is the mechanism
underlying the pivot shift, and it is the reason a rotatory test is more
specific for anterior cruciate deficiency than a straight translation test.

Varus and external rotation couple on the lateral side, which is why the dial
test and varus stress findings travel together in posterolateral injury.
2.10.4 covers the anatomy.

Abduction and internal rotation couple at the moment of a typical non contact
anterior cruciate injury, which is why the mechanism is described as a
combination rather than as a single force.

### What Changes It

Condylar geometry and tibial slope, which set how far each side travels for a
given flexion angle.

The menisci, through their wedge shape and their horn attachments. A
meniscectomised knee follows a different path at the same flexion angle.

Ligament division, which releases a coupled pair together. Dividing the
anterior cruciate increases anterior translation and internal rotation at
once.

Tunnel position in a reconstruction, since a graft placed away from the native
attachment restrains a different combination from the one it replaced.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing applies a load in one direction and records motion in all six,
which is how coupling is quantified. The apparatus moves the joint and the
figures come from cadaveric specimens.

Dynamic fluoroscopy and tracked imaging measure the path in vivo, within a
limited field of view and a limited range of activity.

Instrumented pivot shift measurement records acceleration or skin motion at
the moment of reduction, which converts a graded clinical sign into a number.

Optical motion capture measures the whole limb but carries soft tissue
artefact, which is largest for exactly the small rotations coupling describes.

### Clinical Relevance

Read a rotatory test as the more informative one when anterior cruciate
function is in question, since it loads the coupled pair.

Record the coupled findings together in posterolateral injury rather than
grading varus alone.

Expect a knee with a meniscal and a ligament injury to behave worse than
either alone, because both act on the same path.

Judge a graft by the combination it restrains rather than by a single
arthrometer reading.

### Key Learning Points

- Coupling removes independent degrees of freedom and leaves a reproducible path.
- Injury widens the path rather than adding a motion of a new kind.
- The medial side translates little and the lateral considerably, giving rotation about a medial axis.
- Dividing the anterior cruciate increases translation and internal rotation together.
- Robotic testing quantifies coupling, and its figures come from cadavers.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat coupling as the reason rotatory laxity and translational laxity
dissociate after reconstruction. A graft can restore the anteroposterior limit
and leave the rotational one, because the two are restrained by different
parts of the same structure.

The anterolateral structures contribute to the rotational half of the coupled
pair, which is the biomechanical argument behind lateral augmentation. 3.11
covers the constructs and 2.11.5 the anatomy.

Coupling also explains residual symptoms with a stable arthrometer reading.
The patient reports the rotational event, and the instrument measures the
translation.

### What Changes It

Bony morphology, particularly posterior tibial slope and lateral plateau
shape, which alter the coupled rotation for the same applied anterior load.

The state of the lateral meniscus posterior horn, which acts as a restraint to
the coupled translation and is frequently injured alongside the cruciate.

Graft position and tension, which decide which part of the coupled pair the
reconstruction controls.

Generalised laxity, which widens the path in every direction and makes a side
to side comparison the only usable baseline.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic sectioning studies apportion the coupled restraint, and they do so in
a knee that is loaded in a chosen sequence. The order of sectioning changes
the apportionment, which is a known limitation rather than a controversy.

Instrumented pivot shift gives a continuous measure but has no agreed
threshold, so it describes change within a study more reliably than it
classifies a patient.

In vivo kinematic measurement during a functional task is the closest approach
to what the patient experiences, and the range it can capture is narrow.

No clinical instrument in routine use measures coupled rotation, which is why
the graded pivot shift remains in practice.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess rotation as well as translation before calling a reconstruction
successful.

Consider the coupled contribution of slope and lateral plateau shape when a
graft fails without a new injury.

Repair or preserve the lateral meniscus posterior horn where possible, on
mechanical grounds related to the coupled motion.

Interpret an apportionment figure with the sectioning order in mind, since the
same structure carries different percentages in different protocols.

### Key Learning Points

- Rotatory and translational laxity dissociate after reconstruction because coupling splits them.
- The anterolateral structures restrain the rotational half of the coupled pair.
- Residual symptoms with a normal arthrometer reading are a coupling problem.
- Posterior tibial slope and lateral plateau shape change the coupled rotation.
- Sectioning order changes apportionment, which is a limitation of the method.
