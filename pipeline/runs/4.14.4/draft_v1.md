# Anteromedial Rotatory Instability

The pattern on the side of the knee that heals, and the reason it still ends up
being reconstructed.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Anteromedial rotatory instability is the medial tibia rotating and subluxing
forwards under load, which a patient describes as the knee opening when they
turn away from the injured side.

It requires the posteromedial restraints to be deficient and, in most
descriptions, the anterior cruciate as well. 3.9.7 covers the biomechanics.

Assessing it means adding rotation to the anterior drawer, and the medial
chapter describes the technique in full. 4.12.6 covers it.

### How It Is Performed

Perform valgus stress at thirty degrees and in full extension, because opening
in extension is the finding that implicates the posteromedial corner.
4.12.2 covers that angle.

Perform the anterior drawer in neutral rotation. 4.10.5 covers it.

Repeat it with the tibia externally rotated by about fifteen degrees, watching
the medial plateau. 4.12.6 covers that assessment.

Perform the Lachman test, because the anterior cruciate's state is part of the
pattern. 4.10.4 covers it.

Ask the patient which direction the knee fails in, since turning away from the
affected leg is the movement this pattern describes. 4.2.2 covers instability as
a symptom.

### What a Positive Means

More anterior movement of the medial plateau in external rotation than in
neutral indicates the pattern.

Valgus opening in full extension points the same way and is the more reliable
finding. 4.12.2 covers it.

A knee with the pattern and a deficient anterior cruciate is the usual
combination, and the medial side rather than the cruciate is what makes the
knee rotationally unstable. 3.9.10 covers the combination.

A knee with valgus laxity at thirty degrees only, no opening in extension and
no rotational change has an isolated superficial medial ligament injury, which
heals. 4.12.3 covers that grade.

### Key Learning Points

- The movement is the medial tibia rotating and subluxing forwards under load.
- It needs the posteromedial restraints deficient and usually the anterior cruciate too.
- The finding is the difference between the drawer in external rotation and in neutral.
- Valgus opening in full extension points the same way and is more reliable.
- Valgus laxity at thirty degrees alone, with no rotational change, is the injury that heals.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the pattern to name when asked why some medial injuries are
reconstructed when the ligament heals, and that is the question behind it.

The expected points are valgus stress at both angles, the anterior drawer in
neutral and in external rotation, watching the medial plateau, the Lachman test,
and the patient's description.

Say that the posterior oblique ligament is the structure that usually makes the
difference, and that it heals less reliably than the superficial ligament.
3.9.5 covers its function.

Say that the pattern's mirror on the lateral side is anterolateral rotatory
instability, and that the two are assessed by the same logic. 4.14.3 covers it.

Say that you would record the findings rather than the label. 4.1.8 covers the
record.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the posteromedial structures resist internal
rotation of the tibia, so their loss lets the medial plateau rotate anteriorly
about an axis the intact lateral side supplies. 3.9.2 covers rotational
stability.

The anterior cruciate resists the anterior component of the same movement,
which is why the pattern is usually combined. 3.9.10 covers it.

The deep medial ligament contributes through its attachment to the meniscus, so
a meniscotibial separation adds to the pattern. 4.12.4 covers that structure.

The pattern is larger in a knee with a healed but lengthened superficial
ligament than in one with an acutely torn one, because the healed ligament holds
a firm endpoint over a lax joint. 4.12.3 covers that trap.

### How Good It Is

The assessment is the least reproducible part of the medial examination,
because separating rotation from translation is a judgement rather than a
measurement. 4.12.6 covers it.

The extension valgus test is the reliable half of the assessment, and in
practice it is what the diagnosis rests on. 4.12.2 covers it.

No other test reads the pattern. A valgus examination is blind to it and the
cruciate tests absorb it into the cruciate.

The patient's description of the direction of failure is the most useful part of
the assessment, which is the same conclusion the anterolateral page reaches.
4.14.3 covers that parallel.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The posteromedial structures resist internal rotation, and their loss lets the medial plateau rotate forwards.
- The deep medial ligament contributes through the meniscus, so a separation adds to the pattern.
- The extension valgus test is the reliable half of the assessment.
- No other test reads the pattern: valgus is blind to it and the cruciate tests absorb it.
- The patient's description of the direction of failure is the most useful part.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this pattern is what makes a medial side surgical, and the
medial side otherwise almost never is.

A patient with a competent cruciate reconstruction, an acceptable valgus grade
and this pattern has an unaddressed posteromedial corner, and that is the
commonest reason to reconstruct the medial side in a chronic knee. 4.12.8
covers the assessment.

In an acute combined injury the pattern decides whether the medial side is
addressed at the cruciate operation, and the examination repeated after bracing
is what makes that decision safely. 3.9.10 covers the combination.

The pattern also changes the risk to the cruciate graft, because a graft in a
knee with uncontrolled anteromedial rotation takes load in rotation and
stretches. 4.10.11 covers post-reconstruction assessment.

Where the examination is equivocal and the decision is real, examination under
anaesthesia with the other knee is how it is settled. 4.14.6 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not looking for the pattern, which leaves it attributed
to the cruciate.

The second is reading the whole tibia translating as a rotation.

The third is examining in external rotation with no neutral comparison.

The fourth is reconstructing the superficial medial ligament for a rotational
complaint, which is a technically successful operation for the wrong structure.
4.12.5 covers the structure responsible.

The fifth is examining once in the acute knee, where the corner may still heal
and an early finding is not a definitive one.

### Key Learning Points

- The pattern is what makes a medial side surgical, and it otherwise almost never is.
- In a chronic knee it is the commonest reason to reconstruct the medial side.
- A graft in a knee with uncontrolled anteromedial rotation takes load in rotation and stretches.
- Reconstructing the superficial ligament for a rotational complaint is the wrong operation done well.
- An early acute finding is not definitive, because the corner may still heal.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this pattern explains the patient whose medial injury was
treated correctly and whose knee still fails on turning.

That patient usually has a posteromedial corner that healed in a lengthened
position, with a firm endpoint over a rotationally lax joint, and the comparison
with the other knee is what reveals it. 4.12.3 covers that combination.

A stretched cruciate graft with this pattern has its explanation rather than a
second diagnosis, and revising the graft alone repeats the failure. 4.10.12
covers revision assessment.

A patient with a varus alignment and this pattern is a poor candidate for a
medial reconstruction, because the medial side is unloaded and the
reconstruction has nothing holding it in place. 4.11.5 covers dynamic
assessment.

In a knee with a previous lateral extra-articular procedure the lateral side is
no longer a free axis, so the rotation this pattern depends on is constrained
and the assessment under reads it. 3.11.7 covers the procedure.

In a hypermobile patient the pattern is bilateral and is not a diagnosis, and
establishing that early avoids an operation. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

### Key Learning Points

- It explains the patient whose medial injury was treated correctly and whose knee still fails on turning.
- A corner healed long gives a firm endpoint over a rotationally lax joint.
- A stretched graft with this pattern has its explanation, not a second diagnosis.
- A varus limb makes a medial reconstruction a poor proposition.
- A previous lateral procedure constrains the rotation and makes the assessment under read.
