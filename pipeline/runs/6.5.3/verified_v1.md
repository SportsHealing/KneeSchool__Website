# Cartilage Degeneration

The step that makes the chain irreversible, and the one that happens without
producing a symptom while it is happening.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

The ends of the bones inside a knee are covered in a smooth white surface that
lets them slide past each other. 1.2.2 covers the articular cartilage.

That surface has no blood supply and no nerves, so it cannot heal itself and it
cannot hurt. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### How It Presents

Because the surface cannot hurt, a knee can be wearing out without feeling
wrong. The ache comes later, from the bone underneath and the lining around it.
6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- A smooth surface covers the ends of the bones.
- It has no blood supply, so it cannot heal.
- It has no nerves, so it cannot hurt.
- A knee can be wearing out without feeling wrong.
- The ache arrives later, from the bone and the lining.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

Articular cartilage is the load bearing surface of the joint. Losing meniscal
tissue concentrates load onto a smaller area of it, and concentrated load wears
it. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

It has no blood supply, so it does not repair in the way a bone or a ligament
does. What is lost stays lost. 1.2.2 covers the articular cartilage.

That is the reason this page sits after the meniscus page rather than before it.
The meniscus is what protects the cartilage, and the cartilage is what the whole
decision was protecting. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### How It Presents

A deep ache after activity, stiffness after sitting, and swelling that follows
use rather than injury. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The important point is the one above: the surface itself cannot produce pain, so
the absence of pain does not mean the absence of damage. 6.5.1 covers
instability.

### Management Overview

Nothing restores a worn surface, so everything useful is aimed at protecting
what remains. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

That means keeping the knee stable so it stops shifting, keeping the remaining
meniscus, keeping the muscles strong, and keeping body weight down. 6.5.1
covers instability.

Surgical procedures exist for small single defects with healthy surroundings.
They are described in the surgical academy and they do not treat general wear.
6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

Reconstructing the ligament stops the giving way. It does not give back surface
that has already gone. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

### Key Learning Points

- Articular cartilage is the load bearing surface of the joint.
- Losing meniscal tissue concentrates load onto less of it.
- It has no blood supply and does not repair.
- The absence of pain does not mean the absence of damage.
- Reconstruction stops the shifting and restores nothing.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** If my knee does not hurt, is the cartilage alright?
Not necessarily. The surface has no nerve supply, so wear is silent until the
bone underneath or the joint lining becomes involved. Pain is a late signal
rather than an early one.

**Q.** Can cartilage be regrown?
Not as the original tissue. Procedures exist that fill a defect with repair
tissue, and they are offered for small defects in otherwise healthy joints. They
do not reverse general wear.

**Q.** Will a reconstruction protect my cartilage?
It removes the episodes of giving way that damage the meniscus, and the meniscus
is what protects the cartilage. Whether that translates into a better joint
decades later is a genuinely open question, and this page does not claim it.

**Q.** Does the damage happen at the moment of injury or afterwards?
Both happen. Some surface damage occurs at the original pivot, and more
accumulates with each later episode and with the load concentration that follows
meniscal loss.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

Hyaline articular cartilage is avascular, aneural and alymphatic, which is the
whole explanation for both its silence and its failure to heal. 1.2.2 covers the
articular cartilage.

A chondral lesion involves the surface alone. An osteochondral lesion takes the
subchondral bone with it, which is why the two behave and look different. 5.11.2
covers chondral defects.

Degeneration in this chapter means diffuse load related loss rather than a
single traumatic defect, although a traumatic defect frequently starts it.
6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Four things drive it in an ACL deficient knee. Repeated subluxation, loss of
meniscal tissue, malalignment that concentrates load on one compartment, and
chondral injury sustained at the index event. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Time is the fifth and it belongs with the others, because all four act
cumulatively rather than once. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Alignment matters here more than it does anywhere else in the chapter, because
it decides which compartment carries the concentrated load. 3.13.8 covers
alignment and OA.

### Investigations and Imaging

Radiographs do not show cartilage. They show the space it occupies, which is a
surrogate and a late one. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

MRI shows the surface directly and shows it before the radiograph changes, which
is why an apparently normal film and an abnormal scan are a common pairing
rather than a contradiction. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

Arthroscopy remains the reference standard for the surface, and it is the only
assessment that happens with the joint open. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Hyaline cartilage is avascular, aneural and alymphatic.
- Chondral and osteochondral lesions behave differently.
- Subluxation, meniscal loss, malalignment and index injury drive degeneration.
- Radiographs show the space rather than the surface.
- MRI changes before the radiograph does.

## MRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer says what each test can and cannot see before it
says what it found.

Say that weight bearing films are the ones that matter, because an unloaded knee
does not demonstrate the narrowing. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

Say that MRI is the pre-operative assessment and arthroscopy is the reference
standard. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

Naming a grading system is expected. The point to make is that grading describes
depth and area and says nothing about symptoms. 6.4.6 covers cartilage
injuries.

### Management Overview

The order is stability, meniscus, alignment, surface. Each earlier item protects
the one after it. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A surface procedure performed in an unstable or malaligned knee is being asked
to survive the conditions that produced the lesion. 3.13.8 covers alignment and
OA.

### Complications and Prognosis

This is the step that cannot be undone, and saying so is the point of the
section. The ligament is replaceable, the meniscus is sometimes repairable, and
the surface is neither. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Progression is variable between patients and poorly predicted in any individual
one. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- Say what each test can see before saying what it found.
- Weight bearing films are the ones that demonstrate narrowing.
- Grading describes depth and area rather than symptoms.
- The order is stability, meniscus, alignment, surface.
- The surface step is the one that cannot be undone.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is given a chondral lesion found at the time
of a reconstruction and asked what to do about it.

The defensible answer treats the lesion as information first and as a target
second. It changes what the patient is told about the future, and it changes the
operation far less often. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

A small contained lesion in a knee about to be stabilised is usually recorded,
debrided if unstable, and left. Adding a procedure extends the rehabilitation
and the risk for a lesion that was found rather than complained of. 6.3.5
covers complete tears.

A large lesion, or one in a malaligned compartment, is a different conversation
and may justify staging. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

### Complications and Prognosis

The finding matters most for what it says about the knee's history. A full
thickness lesion in a young knee with an old ACL tear is the record of the
episodes nobody counted. 6.5.1 covers instability.

That reframes the operation. It is no longer preventing a problem; it is
limiting one already underway. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether reconstruction alters the long term fate of
the articular surface or only the symptom of giving way. 6.5.4 covers
osteoarthritis.

The second is whether cartilage restoration procedures change the natural
history of the joint or only the appearance of the defect. 6.4.6 covers
cartilage injuries.

The third is whether alignment correction should be offered earlier in young
patients with a deficient and malaligned knee. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Key Learning Points

- Treat an incidental lesion as information first and a target second.
- A small contained lesion is usually recorded and left.
- A large or malaligned lesion may justify staging.
- The finding is the record of the episodes nobody counted.
- The operation is limiting a problem rather than preventing one.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the practical problem is that the only record of the
articular surface anyone will ever have is the one written at arthroscopy.

A note saying mild chondral change is worth nothing to the surgeon who opens the
knee eight years later. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

The habit worth keeping is a lesion map: compartment, surface, site within the
surface, depth, approximate area, and whether the base is stable. Six items,
recorded the same way every time. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

The second habit is to record whether this lesion was present at a previous
operation, because the difference between a stable lesion and a progressing one
is the single most useful fact in the later consultation and it only exists if
somebody wrote the first description down. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Photographs help and do not replace the description, because a photograph does
not state depth or stability. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Key Learning Points

- The only record of the surface is the one written at arthroscopy.
- Mild chondral change is worth nothing as a description.
- Record compartment, surface, site, depth, area and base stability.
- Record whether the lesion was present at a previous operation.
- Photographs supplement the description rather than replacing it.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is being asked whether an operation aimed at a symptom
will protect a joint, and having to answer honestly that the evidence does not
support the promise the question expects. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Stability protects the meniscus and the meniscus protects the surface. Each link
is sound and the chain as a whole has never been demonstrated in a way that
would justify promising a knee at sixty. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which set how many
decades of loading are at stake and therefore how much the surface matters.
Their medical comorbidities, including body weight, which is a load variable
here rather than an anaesthetic one. The demands they put on the knee, which
decide how much concentrated load the remaining surface will see. Their
psychological state, because a patient who believes the operation protects the
joint will accept risk they would otherwise decline. And their own ideas,
concerns and expectations, which commonly include a belief that surgery prevents
arthritis.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and correct that last belief
rather than leaving it to do the consenting. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The promise the question expects is not supported by the evidence.
- Each link in the chain is sound and the chain has not been demonstrated.
- Body weight is a load variable rather than an anaesthetic one.
- Patients commonly believe surgery prevents arthritis.
- Correct that belief rather than letting it do the consenting.
