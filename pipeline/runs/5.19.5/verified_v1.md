# Meniscal Imaging

Fibres run round the meniscus, so its angle to the field changes with position,
systematically and in every knee.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The meniscus is built from collagen running around its circumference, which is
the arrangement the orientation effect needs. 2.5.1 covers the gross anatomy.

Because the fibres follow the curve, the angle between them and the field is
different at the anterior horn, the body and the posterior horn. 2.5.5 covers
the MRI anatomy.

It matters because meniscal signal is graded and reported, and part of that
signal varies with position for reasons that have nothing to do with the
meniscus. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

### How It Is Acquired

Read the meniscus on more than one plane, because a plane cuts the circumferential
fibres at a different angle in each part of the joint. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

Expect the angle to vary smoothly from front to back rather than at one site.
2.6.1 covers the gross anatomy.

Compare the two menisci in the same knee, since their curvature differs and the
field does not. 2.6.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

Test any signal change that could alter management on a long echo time sequence.
5.2.14 covers T2 imaging.

Keep the report to what survives that test. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal
tears.

### What Normal Looks Like

A normal meniscus is uniformly dark on the sequences used to judge its
substance. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Regions where the fibres approach the critical orientation can carry a faint
internal signal that is not a tear. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

That signal has no surface and does not reach an articular edge. 5.6.3 covers
the horizontal tears.

The meniscal shape, height and position stay normal while it is present. 5.6.11
covers meniscal extrusion.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A tear reaches a surface, and an orientation signal does not. 5.6.4 covers the
vertical tears.

A tear has an orientation of its own set by the injury rather than by the
scanner. 5.6.5 covers the radial tears.

A displaced fragment is a mechanical finding that no artefact produces. 5.6.6
covers the bucket handle tears.

Degenerate meniscal substance carries signal that persists as the echo time
lengthens. 5.6.8 covers the complex tears.

A root injury shows as extrusion, which is a position rather than a signal.
5.6.9 covers the root tears.

### Key Learning Points

- The meniscus is built from fibres running round its circumference.
- Its angle to the field therefore varies from front to back.
- Part of the graded signal varies with position rather than with disease.
- An orientation signal reaches no surface.
- A tear has an orientation set by the injury, not the scanner.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer separates internal signal from a tear by whether
it reaches a surface.

The expected points are the circumferential fibres, the varying angle, the
absence of a surface, the long echo time test, and the mechanical findings.

Say that the two menisci curve differently in the same field. 2.6.1 covers the
gross anatomy.

Say that signal grading was built on appearances acquired at uncontrolled
angles. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Say that the shape and position of the meniscus are the findings orientation
cannot alter. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

### How Good It Is

The meniscus is where this chapter's claim is weakest and most interesting. The
physics applies to circumferential collagen, and how much of the reported
meniscal signal it accounts for has not been established here.

Its best contribution is a candidate explanation for part of the
over-reporting of meniscal signal, which is a known and costly problem. 5.20.2
covers reporting meniscal tears.

It is limited because degenerate meniscus and orientation both produce internal
signal without a surface, so the appearances overlap. 5.6.8 covers the complex
tears.

It is limited again because the page states a candidate explanation and no
evidence for its size. This environment has no source access, so the chapter
will not put a number or a confident claim on it. 5.1.7 covers evidence-based
imaging.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no angle,
signal grade or meniscal dimension appears either: those are published
quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot
check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The physics applies to circumferential collagen.
- How much reported signal it accounts for is not established here.
- Degenerate meniscus and orientation both give signal without a surface.
- The page offers a candidate explanation and no evidence for its size.
- No source access means no number is put on it.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the question is whether a knee goes to arthroscopy, and
meniscal signal without a surface is the commonest reason it should not.

Internal signal with no surface and no mechanical symptom is a reason to treat
the patient rather than the scan. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

A degenerate meniscus in an arthritic knee changes the operation that is
discussed, so the distinction is not academic. 5.20.6 covers reporting OA.

A tear whose plane is set by the injury and reaches a surface is the finding
that matters. 5.6.5 covers the radial tears.

Extrusion and root integrity decide more than substance signal does, and neither
is orientation dependent. 5.6.9 covers the root tears.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is grading internal signal as a tear. 5.6.2 covers meniscal
signal grading.

The posterior horn is reported as degenerate because it is where the signal
tends to appear. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

One plane is used to call a tear that the other plane does not show. 5.6.4
covers the vertical tears.

An arthroscopy is offered for an appearance without a mechanical symptom. 5.1.4
covers imaging pathways.

The reverse error is also made: a genuine degenerate meniscus is dismissed
because the reader has heard that meniscal signal is unreliable. 5.20.8 covers
common reporting errors.

### Key Learning Points

- Internal signal is graded as a tear.
- The posterior horn is reported as degenerate by habit.
- A tear is called from one plane and not confirmed on the other.
- An arthroscopy is offered for an appearance without a symptom.
- A genuine degenerate meniscus is dismissed as unreliable signal.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the meniscus is the structure where the chapter's unrealised
measurement would be worth most, and where the chapter can promise least.

Meniscal substance quality decides whether a tear is repairable, and no imaging
measurement reports it, which is why repairability is assessed through the
arthroscope. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

An orientation sensitive measurement of circumferential fibre order would be
aimed exactly at that question, and the step from physics to measurement has not
been taken in routine practice. 5.19.6 covers the cartilage applications.

A transplanted or repaired meniscus holds fibres at a new orientation, so the
post-operative appearance is not comparable with the pre-operative one. 5.6.14
covers meniscal transplantation imaging.

The chapter's rule holds hardest here: the signal that is graded most often is
the signal whose driving variable is recorded least. 5.19.1 covers the physics
of magic angle imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- Substance quality decides repairability and no measurement reports it.
- Repairability is assessed through the arthroscope instead.
- A measurement of circumferential fibre order would aim at that question.
- A repaired meniscus holds fibres at a new orientation.
- The most graded signal has the least recorded driving variable.
