# Why Imaging Matters

What imaging adds to a knee assessment, and the narrower question of what it
adds that the history and the examination cannot.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Imaging shows the structures the examination can only infer, so it converts a
clinical suspicion into a named lesion in a named place. 4.1.6 covers the
examination's side of that relationship.

It also shows findings nobody was looking for, which is the cost that comes with
the benefit. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

The useful question on any imaging request is not what the scan will show but
what it will change. 5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

### How It Is Acquired

Imaging is requested rather than performed by the person who asked for it, so
the request itself is part of the technique. 5.1.4 covers imaging pathways.

A request carries the clinical question, the side, the suspected structure and
anything that changes the protocol, such as metalwork or a previous operation.
5.5.3 covers the post operative protocol.

The modality follows the question. Bone and alignment go to radiographs, soft
tissue to magnetic resonance imaging, tendon and dynamic questions to
ultrasound, and bone detail or metalwork to computed tomography. 5.1.3 covers
that choice.

The timing follows the question too, because an effusion resolves and a bone
bruise fades. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

### Key Learning Points

- Imaging shows what the examination can only infer.
- It also shows findings nobody was looking for.
- The question is what the scan will change, not what it will show.
- The request is part of the technique.
- The modality and the timing both follow the clinical question.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer names the modality, says why, and says what the
request has to carry.

The expected points are the clinical question, the modality that answers it, the
timing, the protocol modifiers, and what would change if the result were normal.

Say that you would state the clinical question on the request rather than the
suspected diagnosis, since the first tells the radiologist what to look for.
5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Say that a normal scan that changes nothing was the wrong scan. 5.1.5 covers
cost effectiveness.

### How Good It Is

Imaging is better than examination at naming a structure and worse at saying
whether that structure is the source of the symptoms.

Its best contribution is the structural diagnosis in a knee where the
examination has already localised the problem, because then the two agree and
the finding is load bearing. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

It is poor as a first move in an unlocalised knee, where it finds prevalent
abnormalities that are not the cause. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The request states the clinical question rather than the suspected diagnosis.
- A normal scan that changes nothing was the wrong scan.
- Imaging names a structure better than the examination does.
- It says less than the examination about whether that structure hurts.
- It is weakest as a first move in an unlocalised knee.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level imaging earns its place when it changes the operation, the
timing or the consent, and not otherwise.

A finding that confirms what the examination established supports a decision
already half made, which is the commonest and least interesting use. 4.1.6
covers that relationship.

A finding that contradicts the examination is the valuable one, because one of
the two is wrong and resolving it changes the plan. 5.1.2 covers that
comparison.

A finding nobody was looking for creates a decision that did not exist before
the scan, and declining to act on it is a decision too. 5.1.6 covers imaging
pitfalls.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is imaging to reassure rather than to decide, which produces
incidental findings and no change of plan. 5.1.5 covers cost effectiveness.

A degenerate finding in an older knee is prevalent rather than causal, and the
report will name it either way. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

A scan performed too early misses what has not yet developed and a scan too late
misses what has resolved. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

The report answers the question that was asked, so a vague request produces a
vague report. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Imaging earns its place when it changes the operation, the timing or the consent.
- A finding that contradicts the examination is the valuable one.
- A finding nobody sought creates a decision that did not exist.
- A degenerate finding in an older knee is prevalent rather than causal.
- A vague request produces a vague report.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the problem is that the imaging is usually already done, and
often done badly, before the patient arrives.

A scan performed elsewhere on an unknown protocol cannot be compared with one
performed here, and treating the two as a series is the error. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

A report written without the clinical question answers a different question, and
reading it as if it answered yours is how a plan goes wrong. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

Prevalent findings accumulate with age, so the older the knee the more the scan
offers and the less of it means anything. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

The decision to re-image carries its own cost and is made against what the
previous study could not answer rather than against its age. 5.1.5 covers cost
effectiveness.

### Key Learning Points

- The imaging is usually already done, and often on an unknown protocol.
- Two scans on different protocols are not a series.
- A report written without the clinical question answers a different one.
- Prevalent findings accumulate with age.
- Re-imaging is decided against what the last study could not answer.
