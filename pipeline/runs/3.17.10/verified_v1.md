# Future Research Priorities

The questions the previous nine pages could not answer, collected and ranked
by what answering them would change.

## FRCS Level

### What the Question Was

This page is built from the gaps the rest of the chapter named rather than
from a survey of what the field says it wants.

A gap earns a place here on one test: would answering it change what a surgeon
does. A question that would only produce a better number is interesting and is
not a priority.

Nine pages produced seven such gaps, and they fall into three kinds. Questions
about load in the living knee, questions about who deteriorates, and questions
about what patients actually experience.

### What Was Done

Each previous page states where its literature is weak, and those statements
were collected rather than re-derived, so this page is a summary of this
chapter rather than an independent view.

The ranking below is a judgement about clinical consequence, not a reading of
funding priorities or publication volume.

### What It Showed

First, load in the living knee during the activities that cause injury. Every
ligament page in this chapter ends at the same wall: the restraint hierarchy
is known and the load at the moment of injury is not. 3.7.14 states the
problem.

Second, who deteriorates after an untreated injury. The posterior cruciate,
the medial side and the meniscus all have management arguments that turn on
natural history, and all three natural histories rest on patients who
presented.

Third, why a technically successful knee replacement dissatisfies a reliable
minority. Kinematics and satisfaction are both well measured and unconnected,
which 3.17.8 treats as the field's central unresolved problem.

Fourth, why the front of the knee hurts. Patellofemoral mechanics are well
measured and the symptom is unexplained, and no other subject in the knee has
that shape.

Fifth, whether changing load changes disease. The osteotomy chain is plausible
at every link and untested end to end, which 3.17.7 sets out.

Sixth, what the anterolateral structures are. An anatomical question that is
now gating a procedure in widespread use is an unusual and urgent kind of gap.

Seventh, what a measurement is worth. 3.16 argued that each method buys
precision by giving something up, and no study systematically compares a
single question answered by all five methods.

### What It Changed

Nothing yet, which is what distinguishes this page from the nine before it.

What it is for is to make the gaps explicit, so a reader finishing the chapter
knows which parts of it are settled and which are waiting.

### Key Learning Points

- A gap counts as a priority here only if answering it would change what a surgeon does.
- Load in the living knee at the moment of injury is the gap every ligament page ends at.
- Natural history evidence across three subjects rests on patients who presented, which selects for deterioration.
- Kinematics and satisfaction after arthroplasty are both measured and unconnected.
- No study answers one question by all five measurement methods, so method bias is unquantified.

## Fellowship Level

### What Was Done

Two of these seven gaps are closeable with current methods and five are not,
and telling them apart is more useful than ranking them.

Closeable now: the method comparison question, which needs no new technology,
only a study designed to ask one question five ways. And the natural history
questions, which need a cohort recruited at injury rather than at
presentation.

Not closeable now: load at the moment of injury, which needs instrumentation
that does not exist and would not pass ethical review if it did.

Partly closeable: the patellofemoral pain question and the arthroplasty
satisfaction question, both of which may be limited by the outcome measures
rather than by the mechanics.

### What It Showed

The pattern across all seven is that the knee's mechanics are better
understood than the knee's patients, and every gap here sits on the patient
side of that line.

That is not an argument against mechanical research. It is an observation
about where the next useful answers are likely to come from, and several of
them are study design problems rather than measurement problems.

The honest version for a trainee is that the structural biomechanics of the
knee is largely finished work, and the field's open questions are now about
time, selection and experience.

The one exception is the anterolateral question, which is a genuinely open
structural question and is open because the earlier anatomy was taken as
settled.

### What to Read Now

Read a new biomechanical study by asking which of these seven gaps it narrows,
since most narrow none.

Treat a cohort recruited at presentation as unable to answer a natural history
question, however large it is.

Watch the method comparison question, because a study that answers it would
change how every figure in Section 3 should be read.

### Key Learning Points

- Two of the seven gaps are closeable with current methods and five are not.
- Load at the moment of injury needs instrumentation that does not exist.
- The knee's mechanics are better understood than the knee's patients, and every gap sits on the patient side.
- The anterolateral question is open because earlier anatomy was taken as settled.

## Consultant Perspective

### What It Changed

The decision this page informs is what to tell a patient when the evidence
does not reach their question, which is most consultations rather than a few.

Arguing for saying so plainly: a patient told the evidence is uncertain can
participate in the decision, and a patient told a confident answer that later
proves wrong loses trust in everything else that was said.

Arguing for restraint: uncertainty transfers badly, some patients hear it as
incompetence, and a consultation that becomes a seminar on evidence quality
has not served the person who came for help.

The way through is the same weighing the rest of this chapter describes.
Consider their age and physiology, because the time horizon changes which
uncertainties matter. Consider their comorbidities, since these narrow the
options uncertainty applies to. Consider the demands on their knee, because
that is what makes one gap relevant and another academic. Consider their
psychological state, since tolerance of uncertainty varies more between
patients than almost anything else here. And elicit their ideas, concerns and
expectations first rather than last, because what they want to know determines
which uncertainty is worth raising.

Say what is known, say what is not, say which way you would go and why, and
record it.

### What to Read Now

This chapter's closing point applies to this site as much as to the
literature. Every measurement in Section 3 is written from standard teaching
and none has been checked against a source, and every clinical direction on it
is unsigned. The current counts are on the build status page rather than here,
so they cannot go stale in prose.

3.16.10 made that argument about measurement methods. This page makes it about
evidence generally: a page that lists what it does not know is more useful
than a page that does not, and this site's registers exist so that list can be
worked rather than admired.

The seven gaps above are the field's. The two registers are this site's.
Neither closes by being written down, and both are harder to ignore once they
are.

### Key Learning Points

- What to tell a patient when the evidence stops short of their question is itself a balance.
- Plain uncertainty supports shared decisions; unstructured uncertainty damages them.
- Elicit what the patient wants to know first, because it determines which uncertainty is worth raising.
- This site's measurements are unchecked and its clinical directions unsigned, and both are listed so they can be worked rather than admired.
- A page that lists what it does not know is more useful than one that does not.
