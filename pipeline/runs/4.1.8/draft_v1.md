# Examination Documentation

The record is the only part of the examination that survives it. What a usable
knee examination record contains, and why most of them are not usable.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The record is what the examination becomes. Everything else is gone the moment
the patient leaves the room.

A usable record has one property: another clinician can repeat the examination
and say whether the knee has changed. That is a higher standard than
describing what was found.

Most knee examination records fail that test, and the reason is almost always
the same. They record conclusions rather than observations.

### How It Is Performed

Record what you looked at as well as what you found, because a normal finding
is only informative if the reader knows it was sought.

Record the effusion, the range in degrees, and the extensor mechanism on every
knee, because all three change and all three matter. 4.7.4 covers flexion.

Record laxity as a comparison with the other side, with an endpoint, and with
the flexion angle the test was done at.

Record negatives explicitly where they matter. The hip was examined and was
normal is worth writing; silence about the hip is not.

And record what the patient said they cannot do, in their words, because that
is the outcome everything will be measured against.

### What a Positive Means

A finding recorded without its conditions cannot be compared later, which
means it was almost not worth recording.

Grade two laxity is not comparable with anything. A firm endpoint at thirty
degrees with five millimetres more translation than the other side is.

Full range is not comparable. Zero to one hundred and thirty degrees with a
soft end feel is.

A positive McMurray is not comparable. Pain and a palpable click at the medial
joint line on the third attempt is. 4.9.3 covers the test.

### Key Learning Points

- The record is what the examination becomes; everything else is gone.
- A usable record lets another clinician repeat the examination and say whether the knee changed.
- Most records fail that test because they record conclusions rather than observations.
- Record laxity as a side to side comparison, with an endpoint and a flexion angle.
- A negative is worth recording only if the reader can tell it was sought.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

Four elements make a knee laxity record complete, and omitting any one of them
makes the entry close to unusable.

The direction loaded, the flexion angle, the endpoint, and the comparison with
the other side.

Rotation needs the same four, and rotation is where records are weakest. A
dial test recorded without both flexion angles cannot distinguish an isolated
posterolateral injury from a combined one, which is the whole point of the
test. 4.13.4 covers it.

Effusion is recorded by the method used, because the methods differ in what
they detect. A negative patellar tap and a negative sweep test are not the
same claim. 4.8.5 covers the sweep.

### How Good It Is

A well recorded examination outperforms a better examination that was recorded
badly, which is an uncomfortable claim and holds in practice.

The reason is that single examinations rarely decide anything in the knee. A
trajectory does, and a trajectory needs two comparable examinations.

An acute examination recorded with endpoints and angles becomes useful six
weeks later. The same examination recorded as a grade becomes a number nobody
can reinterpret.

Inter-observer agreement for knee laxity tests is moderate at best, and
recording conditions is the main thing within a clinician's control that
improves it. No figure is quoted here, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- A complete laxity record has four elements: direction, angle, endpoint and side to side comparison.
- Rotation records are the weakest, and a dial test needs both flexion angles.
- Record effusion by the method used, because the methods detect different things.
- A single knee examination rarely decides anything; a trajectory does, and that needs two comparable records.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the record has a second job: it has to show the reasoning as
well as the findings.

An entry that records findings and then a plan, with nothing between them, is
unreviewable. What is missing is why those findings produced that plan.

That matters most where the decision was close. A knee reconstructed on a
borderline rotational grade and a high functional demand is a defensible
decision and only if the record says both things.

It matters again where the decision was to wait. A recorded decision to
observe, with the reason and the review interval, is a clinical act. The same
decision unrecorded looks like an omission.

4.22 covers the scored instruments, which do part of this job and not the
reasoning part.

### Where It Misleads

Records mislead in ways that are worth naming, because each has a specific
remedy.

A conclusion recorded as a finding. Anterolateral rotatory instability is an
interpretation; what the knee did is the finding. Record the second and the
first can be revised.

A template that fills itself. A proforma with default normal values produces
records that say an examination happened when it did not, and these are worse
than a blank note because they are believed.

A copied forward entry. The previous examination duplicated into today's note
destroys the trajectory that is the record's main value.

And a record written for one audience. An entry written to justify a decision
reads differently from one written to describe a knee, and the second is what
the next clinician needs. 4.1.7 covers the audiences.

### Key Learning Points

- A record that lists findings and a plan with nothing between them is unreviewable.
- A close decision is defensible only if the record carries both sides of it.
- A decision to observe, with its reason and review interval, is a clinical act; unrecorded it looks like an omission.
- A proforma with default normal values is worse than a blank note, because it is believed.
- A copied forward entry destroys the trajectory that is the record's main value.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In revision and referred practice the record is frequently the only evidence
of what the knee was like before someone operated on it, and it is usually
inadequate.

The commonest gap is a pre-operative examination with no rotational
assessment, which makes it impossible to say afterwards whether a rotational
complaint is new or was always there. 4.10.12 covers the consequence.

The second is range recorded without end feel, so a developing contracture
cannot be dated. 4.7.5 covers end feel.

The third is an examination under anaesthetic recorded without saying it was
under anaesthetic, which makes it non-comparable with everything before and
after it.

The practical conclusion is that the pre-operative examination record is part
of the operation. A reconstruction whose pre-operative rotational state was
never recorded cannot be audited, and a procedure that cannot be audited
cannot be improved.

This page's claims are standard teaching and no frequency is cited for any of
them, which is the condition of the whole site and is stated on every page.

### Key Learning Points

- In revision practice the old record is often the only evidence of the pre-operative knee.
- A pre-operative examination with no rotational assessment makes a later rotational complaint undatable.
- Range recorded without end feel makes a contracture impossible to date.
- An examination under anaesthetic not labelled as such is non-comparable with every other.
- The pre-operative record is part of the operation, because a procedure that cannot be audited cannot be improved.
