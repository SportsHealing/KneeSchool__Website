# Rotational Instability

The chapter's central problem stated directly. The tests measure translation
well and rotation badly, and the patient's complaint is rotational.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Rotational instability is the tibia rotating abnormally under the femur,
rather than sliding forwards.

It is what a patient means when they say the knee gives way on a turn, and it
is what the anterior cruciate and the anterolateral structures resist
together. 3.7.7 covers rotational stability.

This page exists because the gap between what this chapter's tests measure and
what the patient complains about is wider here than anywhere else on the site.

The translation tests are good and the rotational tests are either insensitive
or unobtainable awake, so a knee can test well and still be rotationally
unstable.

### How It Is Performed

The pivot shift is the primary rotational test and is the one that reproduces
the symptom. 4.10.6 covers it.

The Slocum test adds rotation to the anterior drawer and assesses the
anteromedial and anterolateral structures. 4.14.2 covers it.

The dial test assesses external rotation at two flexion angles and separates
an isolated posterolateral injury from a combined one. 4.13.5 covers it.

Dynamic assessment shows rotational control under load, which the static tests
cannot. 4.10.8 covers it.

Perform all of them, record each with its angle, and accept that an awake
examination may not settle the question. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

A positive pivot shift indicates rotational instability and corresponds
closely to the patient's complaint.

A high grade pivot shift suggests an anterolateral or lateral side injury
alongside the cruciate rather than a worse cruciate injury. 4.14.3 covers the
pattern.

External rotation increased at thirty degrees and not at ninety suggests an
isolated posterolateral injury; increased at both suggests a combined one with
the posterior cruciate. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

A normal translation examination with a convincing rotational history means
the rotational component has not been demonstrated rather than that it is
absent.

That last statement is the most important one on the page.

### Key Learning Points

- Rotational instability is the tibia rotating abnormally, not sliding forwards.
- It is what a patient means by giving way on a turn.
- The translation tests are good and the rotational tests are insensitive or unobtainable awake.
- A knee can test well and still be rotationally unstable.
- A normal translation examination with a rotational history means it has not been demonstrated.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the anterior cruciate restrains rotation as
well as translation, and does not do so alone.

The anterolateral structures contribute to rotational restraint, which is why
a cruciate injury with a lateral component produces a larger pivot shift than
a cruciate injury alone. 3.11 covers the anterolateral complex.

That is also why a reconstruction that controls translation can leave rotation
uncontrolled, which is the mechanical basis for the lateral augmentation
argument. 3.17.5 covers that argument and why it is unsettled.

The posterolateral structures restrain external rotation, so external rotation
laxity points there rather than at the cruciate. 4.13.4 covers the assessment.

So rotational instability is a family of findings rather than one, and naming
which plane and which angle is what makes a record useful.

### How Good It Is

The examination's performance for rotation is the weakest part of the knee
assessment, and that is the honest summary.

The pivot shift is specific and insensitive awake and is the best available
test.

Guarding abolishes it, so the test that matters most is the one least likely
to be obtainable in clinic. 4.10.6 covers the problem.

Under anaesthesia the rotational assessment becomes reliable, which is the
main argument for performing one. 4.10.10 covers it.

Imaging does not assess rotational laxity, so there is no alternative
instrument, and that is why this gap persists. No figures appear, under
decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- The cruciate restrains rotation as well as translation, and does not do so alone.
- The anterolateral structures contribute, which is why a lateral component enlarges the pivot shift.
- A reconstruction can control translation and leave rotation uncontrolled.
- Rotational instability is a family of findings; name the plane and the angle.
- Imaging does not assess rotational laxity, so there is no alternative instrument.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this gap produces the two commonest failures in anterior
cruciate practice, and both are avoidable in principle.

The first is a reconstruction performed without the rotational state ever
being assessed, so a residual rotational complaint afterwards cannot be dated
or attributed.

The second is a residual rotational complaint attributed to a stretched graft
when the lateral structures were never addressed. 4.10.12 covers revision
assessment.

The practical response is to record the rotational assessment before every
reconstruction, including recording that it could not be obtained, and to
perform an examination under anaesthetic where the answer would change the
operation.

Whether to add a lateral procedure on the strength of a high grade pivot shift
is a genuine balance, and 3.17.5 sets out why it is the least settled in that
chapter.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording a negative awake pivot shift as rotational
stability.

The second is leaving the rotational assessment blank, which is
indistinguishable in a record from a normal result. 4.1.8 covers the
distinction.

The third is testing anterior translation and concluding about stability,
which conflates two different findings and is the error this whole page exists
to name.

And attributing a rotational complaint after reconstruction to the graft
without considering that the lateral structures were never assessed.

### Key Learning Points

- Two common failures: never assessing rotation, and blaming the graft for an unaddressed lateral injury.
- Record the rotational assessment before every reconstruction, including that it could not be obtained.
- A blank rotational assessment is indistinguishable from a normal one in a record.
- Testing translation and concluding about stability conflates two different findings.
- Whether to add a lateral procedure is a genuine balance, not a settled answer.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

This page is the chapter's conclusion and it is worth stating plainly: the
anterior cruciate examination is the best performing examination in the knee
for the wrong variable.

It measures translation accurately, translation is not the complaint, and the
test that measures the complaint is abolished by the patient being awake.

Almost every dissatisfied reconstruction in referred practice sits in that
gap, either as an unaddressed rotational component or as an undocumented
pre-operative rotational state.

The available responses are all partial: perform the pivot shift and accept
its insensitivity, perform an examination under anaesthetic and accept its
cost, and record the rotational assessment properly so the next clinician can
date a complaint.

The honest teaching point is that this field's best examination has a known
blind spot, that the blind spot is where its failures cluster, and that no
current instrument closes it.

No figure here supports that claim, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- The best performing examination in the knee measures the wrong variable.
- Translation is accurate and is not the complaint; the test for the complaint needs anaesthesia.
- Almost every dissatisfied reconstruction sits in that gap.
- The available responses are all partial.
- The field's best examination has a known blind spot and no instrument closes it.
