# Surgical Anatomy

The approaches to the distal femur described as anatomy: which plane each one
uses, what lies in it, and what is at risk. Technique belongs to Section 7.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The distal femur is approached from the front, the medial side or the lateral
side. There is no posterior approach to the bone itself, because the popliteal
vessels lie on the capsule behind it.

The anterior approach splits or passes beside the extensor mechanism. The
medial and lateral approaches use the border of a muscle rather than its
substance: vastus medialis medially, vastus lateralis and the lateral
intermuscular septum laterally.

### Relations

In front, from skin inwards: skin, subcutaneous fat, the deep fascia, the
quadriceps tendon and retinacula, then the capsule and the suprapatellar
pouch, then bone.

Medially: the saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch cross the approach,
the long saphenous vein runs behind the medial border of the tibia further
distally, and the descending genicular artery lies deep near the adductor
tubercle.

Laterally: the iliotibial band is the first layer of consequence, the superior
lateral genicular artery runs deep to it above the condyle, and the lateral
intermuscular septum carries perforating vessels that bleed when it is
crossed.

Behind everything: the popliteal artery, vein and tibial nerve, with the
artery closest to the bone.

### Function

Each approach trades exposure against something. A wider anterior exposure
costs extensor mechanism integrity. A more posterior medial or lateral
exposure costs proximity to the neurovascular structures.

Skin flaps around the knee depend on the genicular anastomosis, and that
supply runs mainly from medial to lateral. A new incision placed lateral to an
old one can leave the strip between them poorly supplied.

### Clinical Relevance

The structure most often injured in an anterior approach is the infrapatellar
branch of the saphenous nerve, which produces a numb patch lateral to the
scar.

The structure whose injury matters most is the popliteal artery, and it is
behind the bone rather than in any approach. It is injured by displacement and
by instruments passed from front to back, not by the exposure itself.

### Key Learning Points

- There is no posterior approach to the distal femur; the popliteal artery lies on the capsule behind it.
- Medial and lateral approaches use a muscle border rather than its substance.
- The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve is the structure most often injured anteriorly.
- Knee skin flaps are supplied mainly from medial to lateral, which constrains where a second incision goes.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Name the planes. Medial parapatellar: through the retinaculum medial to the
patella, with the quadriceps tendon split proximally. Subvastus: beneath
vastus medialis, leaving the extensor mechanism intact. Midvastus: through the
substance of vastus medialis obliquus. Lateral parapatellar: lateral to the
patella, through the lateral retinaculum.

Lateral approach to the shaft and metaphysis: between vastus lateralis and the
lateral intermuscular septum, elevating the muscle forward off the septum.

Posteromedial exposure of the condyle: anterior to the medial head of
gastrocnemius, which holds the popliteal contents back.

### Relations

Anteromedial: the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the
distal part of the incision obliquely. The saphenous nerve itself emerges
between sartorius and gracilis tendons further proximally and medially.

Anterolateral: the lateral retinaculum, then the superior lateral genicular
artery running transversely deep to the iliotibial band above the condyle.

Lateral shaft: perforating branches of the profunda femoris pierce the
intermuscular septum and are the reliable source of bleeding in that plane.

Posterior: popliteal artery on the capsule, vein superficial to it, tibial
nerve most superficial, common peroneal nerve leaving laterally toward the
fibular neck. The artery is tethered at the adductor hiatus above and the
soleal arch below.

Deep medial: the descending genicular artery and the adductor hiatus lie just
proximal to the adductor tubercle, so dissection above that landmark changes
in character.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The skin of the anterior knee is supplied by the genicular anastomosis through
perforators that arrive predominantly from the medial side. This is the
anatomical basis for using the most lateral of several previous incisions when
re-operating, so the medially based supply to the flap is preserved.

The extensor mechanism's own supply runs in the retinacula and in the fat pad,
and a wide lateral release divides part of it.

Innervation of the anterior capsule is from the femoral nerve through the
nerves to the vasti; the nerve to vastus medialis runs on the deep surface of
the muscle and is the structure at risk in a midvastus split.

### Function

A subvastus approach preserves the extensor mechanism and the nerve to vastus
medialis, at the cost of exposure and of being harder in a stiff or heavy
limb.

A medial parapatellar approach gives the widest exposure and divides part of
the extensor mechanism, which then has to hold a repair.

A midvastus approach sits between the two and crosses the territory of the
nerve to vastus medialis.

### Clinical Relevance

In a knee with previous incisions, choose the most lateral usable one, because
the skin supply arrives medially.

Protect the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve by keeping the distal
dissection where the branch is not, and warn the patient that numbness lateral
to the scar is common whatever is done.

Treat loss of foot pulses after a distal femoral injury or a realignment as
arterial until proven otherwise. The artery's tethering means it does not
tolerate the displacement that the bone does.

Expect bleeding when the lateral intermuscular septum is crossed, and control
it before proceeding rather than after.

### Key Learning Points

- Subvastus spares the extensor mechanism and the nerve to vastus medialis; parapatellar gives the widest exposure.
- When previous incisions exist, use the most lateral usable one, because skin supply arrives medially.
- Perforators crossing the lateral intermuscular septum are the reliable bleeding source in the lateral plane.
- Loss of foot pulses after distal femoral injury is arterial until proven otherwise.
- The nerve to vastus medialis lies on the deep surface of the muscle and is at risk in a midvastus split.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The relations that change a plan are the ones altered by previous surgery.
Scar obliterates the subvastus plane first, because it depends on a mobile
muscle belly. The parapatellar plane survives, which is why it is the
fallback.

Posteriorly, capsular scar brings the popliteal structures closer to the bone
rather than further from it, and flexion no longer reliably moves them back.

### Function

Exposure is a decision about which structure to accept damage to. The extensor
mechanism, the skin flap and the medial nerve supply are the three currencies,
and no approach pays in none of them.

A stiff knee, a previous lateral incision and a thin flap each shift the
choice, and they do so independently of the procedure planned.

### Clinical Relevance

In revision arthroplasty, extensile options extend the parapatellar approach
proximally or detach the extensor insertion distally. Both are anatomical
decisions about which end of the mechanism to compromise, and Section 7 owns
the technique.

In a knee with multiple previous incisions, a plastic surgical opinion before
rather than after the incision is an anatomical judgement about the flap, not
a courtesy.

Where the popliteal artery is at risk, the anatomical fact that matters is its
tethering rather than its position. Proximal and distal control is possible;
control at the level of the condyles is not.

### Key Learning Points

- Scar closes the subvastus plane first, which is why parapatellar is the fallback.
- Capsular scar brings the popliteal structures nearer the bone and flexion no longer moves them away.
- Extensor mechanism, skin flap and medial nerve supply are the three currencies of exposure.
- The popliteal artery can be controlled proximally and distally but not at the condyles.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

Four situations sit outside the standard approaches and each is defined by
anatomy rather than by technique.

The ankylosed or severely stiff knee. The extensor mechanism is short and
adherent, and the decision is where to lengthen it. Proximal options work
within the quadriceps and distal options work at the tubercle. The anatomical
difference is that a proximal lengthening heals in muscle and a distal one has
to heal in bone.

The knee with a compromised flap. Perforator anatomy decides this, and the
relevant fact is the medial origin of the supply. Any plan that leaves a
narrow strip of skin between two incisions is relying on a watershed.

Tumour and massive bone loss around the distal femur. The approach is dictated
by the lesion rather than the joint, and the structure that constrains
everything is the neurovascular bundle behind the bone. Resection proximal to
the adductor hiatus and resection distal to it are different operations
because the artery is tethered at that point.

The infected knee with sinuses. Sinus tracts mark the planes that are already
compromised, and incorporating them is an anatomical choice about which flap
survives.

In all four the structures named on this page do not change. What changes is
how much of each one can be spent.

### Key Learning Points

- A proximal extensor lengthening heals in muscle; a distal one has to heal in bone.
- Any plan leaving a narrow skin strip between two incisions relies on a watershed.
- Resection proximal and distal to the adductor hiatus are different operations, because the artery is tethered there.
- Sinus tracts mark the planes already compromised and constrain which flap survives.
