# Skiing

A sport where the equipment decides the injury. What binding, boot and ability
change about the history.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A skiing history tests how the knee was loaded by a long lever, because the
ski and boot extend the leg and transmit forces the knee has to resist. 3.15.6
covers the mechanics.

Asking about the equipment is unusual in a knee history and is essential here,
because modern boots and bindings moved the injury from the tibia to the knee.

Asking about ability matters too, since the injury patterns differ between
beginners and experienced skiers.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether the bindings released, because a binding that did not release
transmitted the whole force to the knee.

Ask about the fall: a slow twisting fall, a backward fall onto the tails of
the skis, or a high speed fall.

Ask about ability and conditions, since a beginner falling slowly and an
expert falling fast produce different injuries.

Ask whether they could ski down or needed to be taken off the mountain.

Ask about swelling timing as always. 4.2.3 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

A slow twisting fall with the ski acting as a lever and the binding not
releasing is the classic medial collateral or cruciate mechanism in a
beginner.

A backward fall with the knee flexed and the boot pushing the tibia forwards
is the phantom foot mechanism and is a classic anterior cruciate injury in an
experienced skier.

A high speed fall with multidirectional laxity is a high energy injury and may
be a dislocation. 4.23.6 covers the assessment.

A valgus twisting fall produces a medial structure injury, which is the
commonest skiing knee injury overall. 4.12.1 covers the assessment.

Pain without a fall, developing over a ski week, is usually a loading or
patellofemoral problem rather than an injury.

### Key Learning Points

- The ski and boot extend the leg into a lever that transmits force to the knee.
- Ask whether the bindings released, because a binding that did not transmitted everything.
- A slow twisting fall in a beginner is a medial collateral or cruciate mechanism.
- A backward fall with the boot pushing the tibia forwards is a cruciate mechanism in an expert.
- Pain developing over a ski week without a fall is usually a loading problem.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that equipment changes the forces, which is unusual
among knee mechanisms and is the point of this page.

A stiff high boot prevents ankle motion, so rotational and bending forces pass
to the knee rather than being absorbed below it.

The ski adds length, so a small angular displacement at the tip produces a
large moment at the knee.

Bindings are designed to release at thresholds that protect the tibia, and the
knee is loaded below those thresholds, which is why the injury moved upwards
as equipment improved. 3.17.8 covers the general principle of a solution
relocating a problem.

A medial collateral injury is the commonest skiing knee injury and is usually
managed non-operatively. 4.12.1 covers it.

### How Good It Is

The skiing history performs well because the fall is usually a discrete,
memorable event and the equipment question is factual.

The binding question is the single most useful one and it is almost never
asked.

It performs well for distinguishing the beginner pattern from the expert
pattern, which maps onto different structures.

It performs poorly for grading, as all acute histories do, and the examination
at two to six weeks is more informative than the one on return from the
mountain.

### Key Learning Points

- A stiff boot prevents ankle motion, so forces pass to the knee instead.
- The ski adds length, so a small displacement at the tip is a large moment at the knee.
- Bindings protect the tibia at thresholds above those that injure the knee.
- The binding question is the most useful one here and is almost never asked.
- The medial collateral is the commonest skiing knee injury and usually heals.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the skiing history matters because most skiers are
recreational, which changes the reconstruction conversation.

A patient who skis one week a year and has an isolated medial injury does not
need surgery, and the history is what establishes both halves of that.

A patient whose cruciate injury prevents the activity they travel for has a
real indication even though they are not an athlete, and dismissing
recreational demand is a recognisable error.

The timing of the ski season is a legitimate factor in elective planning and
should be named as a preference rather than disguised as a clinical reason.

A combined medial and cruciate injury from a skiing mechanism needs the medial
component assessed properly, and this is the commonest under-treated
combination in this group. 4.12.7 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is assuming a recreational skier has low demand. A week of
skiing is a high demand week.

The second is not asking about the equipment, which loses the most informative
question on the page.

The third is attributing a knee injury sustained abroad to the imaging done
abroad, where the sequences and the reporting may differ and a repeat
assessment is reasonable. 4.2.33 covers previous imaging.

And a skiing injury in an older patient may be a degenerate structure failing,
with a history that sounds like a minor fall.

### Key Learning Points

- Most skiers are recreational, which changes the conversation rather than removing the indication.
- A week of skiing is a high demand week.
- Name the ski season as a preference rather than disguising it as a clinical reason.
- The combined medial and cruciate injury is the commonest under-treated pattern here.
- Imaging done abroad may warrant repeating rather than being accepted.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the skier presents with a chronic medial injury that was
assumed to have healed, or with a knee that has been reconstructed and is
unstable on skis specifically.

A knee stable in daily life and unstable on skis is being loaded by the lever
the sport supplies, and the useful question is whether the rotational
component was addressed. 4.14.3 covers the pattern.

A chronic medial injury with valgus laxity and a reconstructed cruciate is the
combination that most often produces graft stretching, because the medial
laxity loads the graft. 4.12.8 covers the assessment.

Equipment remains relevant at this level: a patient skiing on inappropriately
set bindings is modifiable in a way the knee is not.

No figure here supports these claims, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- A knee stable in daily life and unstable on skis is being loaded by the sport's lever.
- Chronic medial laxity with a reconstructed cruciate is the pattern that stretches grafts.
- Equipment remains modifiable in a way the knee is not.
- Ask whether the rotational component was ever addressed.
