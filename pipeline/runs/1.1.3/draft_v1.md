# Functions of the Knee

Three jobs at once: move through a wide arc, carry large loads, and stay
steady while doing both.

## For Young Learners

### Function

Try this. Stand up, then sit down again slowly. Your knee went from almost
straight to bent past a right angle, holding your whole weight the entire way.
It did not wobble sideways and it did not collapse.

That is three jobs at once. Move through a big arc. Carry a heavy load. Stay
steady while doing both. Most machines are built to do one of those well. The
knee does all three.

There is a fourth job that is easy to miss. The knee softens landings. When
you jump down from a wall, your knees bend as you land. That bending spreads
the impact out over a longer moment instead of letting it hit all at once.

### Key Learning Points

- The knee moves through a wide arc, from almost straight to fully bent.
- It carries your body weight through that whole arc.
- It stays steady sideways while bending and straightening.
- Bending as you land softens the impact and protects the joint.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Function

The knee bends to roughly 135 degrees in most people and straightens fully,
sometimes a few degrees beyond. Losing either end of that range causes
trouble. Full straightening is needed to stand and walk efficiently. Bending
beyond 100 degrees is needed to rise from a low chair, climb stairs
comfortably and get in and out of a car.

At the same time it carries load. Walking on the flat puts several times body
weight through the joint. Stairs and squatting put more. That load is spread
across surfaces designed to share it.

Stability is the third job. The knee resists being pushed sideways and resists
the shin sliding forwards or backwards under the thigh. It allows a small
amount of rotation and resists more than that.

### Clinical Relevance

Losing full extension matters more than most people expect. A knee that will
not straighten makes walking tiring, because the thigh muscle has to work
continuously instead of resting at the top of each step.

Losing flexion shows up in specific tasks: low chairs, stairs, kneeling,
getting into a car. Rehabilitation after injury or surgery usually restores
extension first for that reason, then builds flexion.

A knee that gives way is reporting a stability problem rather than a movement
one, and that distinction shapes what is investigated and what is treated.

### Key Learning Points

- Normal knees bend to roughly 135 degrees and straighten fully.
- Full extension matters for efficient walking. Flexion matters for chairs, stairs and cars.
- The joint carries several times body weight during ordinary activity.
- Stability means resisting sideways push and front to back sliding.
- Giving way points to a stability problem rather than a movement problem.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** How much bend do I actually need?
Roughly 70 degrees to walk comfortably, 90 to climb stairs, and 100 or more to
rise easily from a low chair. Full flexion is useful rather than essential for
most daily tasks.

**Q.** Why does my knee feel worse on stairs?
Stairs load the joint more heavily than level walking and ask for more bend at
the same time. It is one of the first activities to become uncomfortable.

**Q.** Is it bad that my knee bends backwards slightly?
A few degrees beyond straight is common and usually normal, particularly in
women and in younger people. A large amount can alter how the joint is loaded
and is worth mentioning.

**Q.** Should I worry about noises from my knee?
Painless clicking and grinding are very common and usually mean nothing. Noise
with pain, swelling or catching is worth assessing.

Seek assessment if you cannot fully straighten your knee, if it locks in one
position, or if it gives way when you are walking.

## For Medical Students

### Function

The knee serves three mechanical demands simultaneously: a large arc of
sagittal motion, transmission of substantial axial load, and maintenance of
stability in the coronal and transverse planes throughout that arc.

Range is typically zero to about 135 degrees of flexion, with a few degrees of
hyperextension in many individuals. Terminal extension is associated with a
small obligatory external rotation of the tibia on the femur, which locks the
joint into its most stable configuration and allows standing with minimal
quadriceps activity.

Load transmission is shared between the articular cartilage and the menisci,
which increase contact area and lower peak contact stress. Shock absorption
derives from eccentric muscle work, from the deformable soft tissues and from
subchondral bone.

### Clinical Relevance

The three demands conflict, and the compromises explain much of knee
pathology. Mobility requires incongruent surfaces, which sacrifices bony
stability and places the burden on soft tissue restraints that can tear.

Function is best assessed against those three demands in turn. A deficit of
range, a deficit of load tolerance and a deficit of stability have different
causes and different treatments, and a history that separates them is more
useful than one that records pain alone.

The detailed kinematics of rolling, gliding and the screw home mechanism are
taken up in the biomechanics section.

### Key Learning Points

- The knee meets three conflicting demands: range, load transmission and stability.
- Range is roughly zero to 135 degrees, with slight hyperextension in many people.
- Terminal extension involves obligatory tibial external rotation, locking the joint.
- Load is shared between articular cartilage and the menisci, which raise contact area.
- Separating deficits of range, load tolerance and stability directs assessment.
