# Patellar Tendon

The tendon ultrasound is best at, and the one whose abnormal appearance is
commonest in people with no symptoms.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The patellar tendon runs from the patellar apex to the tibial tuberosity, close to
the skin along its whole length. 2.4.1 covers the patellar anatomy.

That makes it the single best imaged structure in the knee by ultrasound. 5.2.7
covers the frequency selection.

It matters because tendinopathy here is common in athletes, and the imaging
appearance occurs in asymptomatic tendons too. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

### How It Is Acquired

Scan in long axis from the patellar apex to the tuberosity with the knee slightly
flexed. 5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

Scan in short axis as well, because the deep posterior portion is where change
begins. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

Keep the beam perpendicular throughout, since the tendon curves at the patellar
apex. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

Apply colour Doppler, because vascularity distinguishes an active process from a
quiescent one. 5.2.11 covers Doppler imaging.

Compare with the other side, since the appearance is best judged comparatively.
5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

### What Normal Looks Like

A uniform bright fibrillar band of even thickness from apex to tuberosity. 5.5.10
covers the tendons.

A thin hypoechoic zone at the deep patellar insertion, which is a normal
appearance. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

No colour Doppler signal within the tendon. 5.2.11 covers Doppler imaging.

Symmetry with the contralateral tendon.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Focal thickening with loss of the fibrillar pattern, usually at the deep
posterior proximal portion. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

Hypoechoic change within the thickened area.

Colour Doppler signal within the tendon, indicating an active process. 5.2.11
covers Doppler imaging.

An anechoic cleft, indicating a tear rather than tendinopathy. 5.4.5 covers the
quadriceps tendon.

### Key Learning Points

- The tendon is close to the skin along its whole length.
- It is the best imaged structure in the knee by ultrasound.
- Change begins at the deep posterior proximal portion.
- A thin hypoechoic zone at the deep insertion can be normal.
- The appearance occurs in asymptomatic tendons.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the tendon to name when asked where ultrasound is the first
line investigation.

The expected points are the two planes, the deep posterior portion, the
perpendicular beam, the Doppler, and the comparison.

Say that the appearance is found in asymptomatic tendons. 5.1.6 covers imaging
pitfalls.

Say that Doppler separates active from quiescent change. 5.2.11 covers Doppler
imaging.

Say that the symptoms rather than the image establish the diagnosis. 5.1.2 covers
clinical examination versus imaging.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that tendinopathy is a disorganisation of collagen with
increased ground substance and water, so the fibrillar pattern is lost and the
tendon swells. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

The deep posterior proximal portion is where the tendon is compressed against the
patellar apex in flexion, which is why change starts there. 3.12.3 covers the
contact pressures.

Neovascularisation accompanies the active phase, which is what Doppler detects
and why its presence changes the interpretation rather than the diagnosis. 5.2.11
covers Doppler imaging.

Because the appearance exists in asymptomatic tendons, the image establishes the
tendon's state and the patient establishes whether it matters. 5.1.2 covers
clinical examination versus imaging.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound describes this tendon's structure better than any other modality and
cannot attribute symptoms to it.

Its best contribution is the structural description plus the activity assessment,
in one quick examination. 5.2.11 covers Doppler imaging.

It is limited by the appearance occurring without symptoms, so a positive finding
does not establish a cause. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no tendon
thickness values or Doppler grades appear either: those are published equipment
dependent quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- Tendinopathy is collagen disorganisation with increased water.
- The deep posterior proximal portion is compressed against the patellar apex.
- Neovascularisation accompanies the active phase.
- The image establishes the tendon's state and the patient whether it matters.
- A positive finding does not establish a cause.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the ultrasound contributes to the loading conversation rather
than to an operative one.

A thickened vascular tendon in a symptomatic athlete supports an active
tendinopathy, which affects the rehabilitation timeline. 5.4.20 covers tendon
procedures.

A thickened avascular tendon suggests chronic structural change, which is a
different conversation with the same patient. 5.4.20 covers tendon procedures.

A discrete tear within a tendinopathic tendon is a separate finding with separate
consequences. 5.4.5 covers the quadriceps tendon.

Whether to intervene is a Section 6 decision, and the imaging supplies the
structural state rather than the indication. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct
investigation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is attributing anterior knee pain to an incidental
appearance. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Anisotropy at the curved patellar apex is read as tendinopathy. 5.2.10 covers
anisotropy.

Probe pressure abolishes the Doppler signal and the tendon is called quiescent.
5.2.11 covers Doppler imaging.

A normal deep insertional hypoechoic zone is reported as a lesion. 5.5.10 covers
the tendons.

A thickness is quoted against a threshold from different equipment. 5.20.8 covers
common reporting errors.

### Key Learning Points

- Pain is attributed to an incidental appearance.
- Anisotropy at the curved apex is read as tendinopathy.
- Probe pressure makes an active tendon look quiescent.
- A normal deep hypoechoic zone is reported as a lesion.
- A thickness is quoted against another system's threshold.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the patellar tendon is the clearest example in knee imaging of
a finding whose prevalence in asymptomatic people undermines its diagnostic use.

The appearance is found in a substantial proportion of asymptomatic athletic
tendons, so its presence shifts the probability of a diagnosis very little.
5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

It is also found in athletes who later become symptomatic, which has made it a
candidate screening finding without the predictive performance being established.
5.1.7 covers evidence based imaging.

Doppler vascularity and symptoms correlate inconsistently across studies, partly
because the acquisition is not standardised between them. 5.2.11 covers Doppler
imaging.

Elastography measures tissue stiffness, which is closer to the mechanical
question than echogenicity is, and it is not in routine knee practice. 5.18.1
covers quantitative magnetic resonance imaging.

The disciplined report says what the tendon looks like, says whether it is
vascular, and says that neither establishes the diagnosis. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The appearance is common in asymptomatic athletic tendons.
- Its presence shifts the diagnostic probability very little.
- Its predictive performance as a screening finding is unestablished.
- Vascularity and symptoms correlate inconsistently across studies.
- Elastography is closer to the mechanical question and is not routine.
