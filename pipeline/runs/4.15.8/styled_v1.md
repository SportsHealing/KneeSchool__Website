# Trochlear Dysplasia Assessment

A shallow groove, which is the factor that most often decides whether a
patellofemoral operation holds, and which the examination can only suspect.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Trochlear dysplasia is a shallow or flat trochlear groove, so the bony
constraint that normally holds the patella central is reduced or absent.
2.12.1 covers the trochlea.

The examination cannot measure it. What the examination can do is raise it, from
the pattern of findings that a dysplastic knee produces. 4.1.6 covers the
relationship with imaging.

It matters because it is the factor that most often determines whether a soft
tissue patellofemoral operation holds, so suspecting it changes what is
investigated before anything is offered. 3.12.4 covers trochlear function.

### How It Is Performed

Assess the active tracking in sitting, looking for an abrupt lateral deviation
near extension. 4.15.2 covers the J sign.

Assess the lateral glide at full extension and at thirty degrees, and note
whether it falls as the knee flexes. 4.15.4 covers the glide.

Palpate the trochlea's edges with the knee flexed and the patella displaced,
which occasionally gives an impression of a flat lateral facet and is not a
reliable measurement.

Ask about the age at first dislocation and about dislocations with minimal
force, since both suggest a structural rather than a traumatic cause. 4.2.22
covers the event.

Ask about the other knee and about family history, since dysplasia is often
bilateral. 4.19.4 covers paediatric patellar instability.

### What a Positive Means

A J sign with a glide that does not reduce at thirty degrees suggests the
patella is not engaging a normal groove. 4.15.2 covers the sign.

A first dislocation in childhood or adolescence with minimal trauma suggests a
structural predisposition rather than an injury. 4.19.4 covers that group.

Bilateral findings and a family history point the same way.

A normal trochlea is suggested by a glide that falls with flexion and a track
that is central through the arc, and that is the knee in which a soft tissue
operation has its best case. 4.15.7 covers the ligament.

None of these findings is diagnostic. The diagnosis is radiographic and the
examination's job is to ensure the imaging is obtained and read for it.
4.1.6 covers that division.

### Key Learning Points

- Dysplasia is a shallow groove, so the bony constraint on the patella is reduced.
- The examination cannot measure it and can raise it.
- A J sign with a glide that does not reduce at thirty degrees is the suggestive pattern.
- A first dislocation in childhood with minimal trauma suggests a structural cause.
- The diagnosis is radiographic; the examination ensures the imaging is obtained.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected answer is honest about what the examination can and
cannot do, and names the imaging.

The expected points are the tracking, the glide at two angles, the history of
the first dislocation, the bilaterality, and the imaging.

Say that the examination suspects and the imaging diagnoses, because claiming
otherwise is the error the question tests.

Say that you would assess patellar height and rotational alignment as well,
since the three bony factors travel together and are assessed together.
4.5.6 covers rotational alignment.

Say that the factor matters because it determines whether a soft tissue
operation holds, which is the clinical consequence. 4.15.7 covers that
operation.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the trochlea's depth provides the bony constraint
on the patella through flexion, so a shallow groove leaves the soft tissues
carrying restraint over a wider arc. 3.12.4 covers trochlear function.

A dysplastic groove is shallowest proximally, which is why the patella engages
later and the abnormality is most evident near extension. 2.12.1 covers the
trochlea.

That late engagement is why the lateral glide fails to fall at thirty degrees,
which is the examination's most specific hint. 4.15.4 covers the glide.

Dysplasia is developmental and often bilateral, which is why the history and
the other knee are part of the assessment rather than an afterthought.
4.19.6 covers developmental variants.

### How Good It Is

The clinical assessment is a screen with poor specificity, and saying so is the
accurate position rather than a failure of technique.

Its best single hint is a glide that does not reduce with flexion, and even that
is suggestive rather than diagnostic.

The history contributes more than the examination: an early first dislocation
with minimal force is a stronger pointer than any physical finding. 4.2.22
covers the event.

Radiographs and cross sectional imaging make the diagnosis and grade it, and
this is the clearest case in Section 4 where the examination defers entirely.
4.1.6 covers that relationship.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The trochlea's depth provides the bony constraint through flexion.
- A dysplastic groove is shallowest proximally, so the patella engages late.
- Late engagement is why the lateral glide fails to fall at thirty degrees.
- The history contributes more than the examination.
- This is the clearest case in Section 4 where the examination defers entirely.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level dysplasia is the factor that most often decides whether a
patellofemoral operation holds, and the examination's role is to make sure it is
assessed before anything is offered.

A deficient medial restraint with a normal trochlea is the best case for a
ligament reconstruction alone. 4.15.7 covers it.

A deficient restraint with marked dysplasia is the group in which that operation
most often fails, and in which a deepening procedure or a combination is
considered. 4.20.6 covers the post-operative assessment.

Patellar height and tubercle position are assessed alongside, because the three
factors interact and an operation addressing one leaves the others. 4.6.7
covers the tubercle.

In a patient with pain and no instability, dysplasia is an incidental finding
and not an indication, which is a distinction worth holding firmly. 4.15.9
covers patellofemoral pain.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is claiming the diagnosis from the examination.

The second is offering a soft tissue operation without assessing the bony
factors.

The third is treating dysplasia found incidentally in a painful knee as an
indication.

The fourth is assessing one knee, when the condition is often bilateral.

The fifth is not asking about the age and circumstances of the first
dislocation, which is the most informative question available. 4.2.22 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Dysplasia most often decides whether a patellofemoral operation holds.
- A deficient restraint with a normal trochlea is the best case for a ligament reconstruction alone.
- Height and tubercle position are assessed alongside, because the factors interact.
- Dysplasia in a painful knee with no instability is not an indication.
- The age and circumstances of the first dislocation is the most informative question.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice unrecognised dysplasia is the commonest explanation for a
patellofemoral reconstruction that failed.

A patient with a stretched or failed medial reconstruction and marked dysplasia
had a soft tissue operation for a bony problem, and the revision is a bony
decision. 4.15.7 covers the ligament.

A patient with a corrected tubercle position, a reconstructed ligament and
persisting instability has an unaddressed trochlea, which is the remaining
factor and the hardest to address. 4.6.7 covers the tubercle.

A patient with bilateral dysplasia and unilateral symptoms has a structural
predisposition that became symptomatic on one side, and the other side's
prognosis belongs in the conversation. 4.19.4 covers that group.

In a skeletally immature patient the trochlea is still developing, which changes
both the assessment and what any operation can achieve. 4.19.1 covers growth
plate assessment.

In medicolegal work dysplasia is the finding that most often establishes a
constitutional predisposition, which matters when a dislocation is attributed to
an injury. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- Unrecognised dysplasia most often explains a failed patellofemoral reconstruction.
- A corrected tubercle and a reconstructed ligament with persisting instability leaves the trochlea.
- Bilateral dysplasia with unilateral symptoms puts the other side's prognosis in the conversation.
- In a skeletally immature patient the trochlea is still developing.
- Dysplasia most often establishes a constitutional predisposition.
