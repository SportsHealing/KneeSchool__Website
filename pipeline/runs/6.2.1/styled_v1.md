# Non-contact Injury

Nobody touched the player, so the force came from the player, which is the only
reason prevention is possible at all.

## For Young Learners

### Causes and Risk Factors

In most ACL tears nobody touches the player. The foot is planted, the body turns
or lands, and the knee is loaded in a direction it cannot resist. 6.1.5 covers
the mechanisms of injury.

That surprises people, because a serious injury usually suggests a collision.
Here the forces come from the player's own movement. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL
is.

It also means the movement can be trained, which a collision cannot. 6.6.4
covers prevention.

### How It Presents

A pop is often felt. The knee swells within a few hours, and most players cannot
carry on. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

The knee often looks normal from outside apart from the swelling, which is one
reason it can be underestimated at the side of a pitch.

### Key Learning Points

- Most ACL tears happen with no contact at all.
- The force comes from the player's own movement.
- A planted foot with a turning or landing body is the pattern.
- Because the movement can be trained, the risk can be lowered.
- Swelling within hours is the sign that matters.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Causes and Risk Factors

The classic sequence is deceleration, landing or a change of direction with the
foot fixed and the knee close to straight. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of
injury.

Because there is no opponent involved, the injury is often described as having
happened for no reason, which is understandable and not quite true. 3.7.13
covers the ACL loading during cutting.

The practical consequence is that this is the pattern prevention programmes are
designed around. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### How It Presents

Swelling within a few hours is bleeding into the joint and points at a
structural injury. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Most people stop playing immediately, though not all, and continuing does not
rule the injury out. 4.10.1 covers the history of ACL injury.

In the days afterwards the knee is stiff and sore, and the instability people
remember comes later, when they try to turn. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- Deceleration, landing or turning with a fixed foot is the pattern.
- No opponent is involved, so the injury feels inexplicable.
- The movement is trainable, which is why prevention programmes exist.
- Swelling within hours means bleeding in the joint.
- Instability is noticed later, when the knee is asked to turn.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** How can a ligament tear with nobody touching me?
The forces in a sudden deceleration or landing are large and arrive quickly. The
ligament is short, so it has little length over which to absorb them, and at the
wrong knee position it takes more of the load than it can carry.

**Q.** Did I do something wrong?
No. Landing and cutting patterns vary between people and shift with fatigue, and
an individual injury cannot be traced to a technique fault with any confidence.
Prevention works across groups, which is a different claim.

**Q.** Would a brace have prevented it?
There is no good evidence that a brace prevents this injury in a knee with an
intact ligament. Training programmes that change how people land and turn are
what has been shown to help.

**Q.** Can it happen again on the other side?
It is more likely than average, yes. That is one reason prevention work
continues after an injury rather than stopping once the first knee is treated.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Causes and Risk Factors

The position is knee near extension, valgus moment, internal rotation of the
tibia, usually with the body decelerating or landing. 3.7.14 covers the ACL
injury mechanisms.

Quadriceps contraction near extension generates anterior tibial translation and
adds to the ligament's load, which is why the knee's angle matters as much as
the force. 3.7.12 covers the ACL loading during landing.

Fatigue, previous injury and the sport's demands all shift exposure to that
position rather than changing the ligament itself. 6.1.3 covers the
epidemiology.

### How It Presents

Immediate haemarthrosis is the cardinal finding, and it is a reason to examine
and image rather than to reassure. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

A reported pop with immediate swelling and an inability to continue is the
classical triad, and the absence of any one of them does not exclude the
injury. 4.10.1 covers the history of ACL injury.

### Examination

Examine early if the knee allows it, because guarding worsens over the first
days. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

The Lachman test is the most reliable acute test and the one least spoiled by
pain. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

The pivot shift is the most informative and the least obtainable in an acute
knee. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

### Key Learning Points

- The position is near extension with valgus and internal rotation.
- Quadriceps contraction near extension adds to the load.
- Fatigue and sport shift exposure rather than changing the ligament.
- Immediate haemarthrosis is the cardinal finding.
- The Lachman test survives acute guarding best.

## MRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

In a viva the expected answer gives the position before the force.

The expected points are the near extension position, valgus and internal
rotation, the quadriceps contribution, the absence of an opponent, and the
trainability that follows from it. 3.7.10 covers the ACL strain during activity.

Say that a non-contact mechanism lowers the prior probability of multi ligament
injury without removing it. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Say that the associated injuries predicted by this mechanism are the meniscus
and the anterolateral structures rather than the collaterals. 6.4.2 covers ramp
lesions.

### Examination

Examine, then repeat, because the first examination of a guarded knee is the
least informative one that will be done. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

An examination under anaesthesia removes guarding entirely and is why it is
arranged where the diagnosis matters and the knee will not permit it. 4.10.10
covers the examination under anaesthesia.

### Investigations and Imaging

The bone bruise pattern corroborates the history, so it is read as evidence
rather than noted as an incidental. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

A lateral femoral condyle and posterolateral tibial pair is the pivot pattern
this mechanism produces. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL injury.

The study is read for what else was injured, because the cruciate was never the
only question. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

### Key Learning Points

- Give the knee's position before the force.
- A non-contact mechanism lowers but does not remove multi ligament risk.
- The predicted company is meniscal and anterolateral.
- The first examination of a guarded knee is the least informative one.
- The bruise pattern is evidence rather than an incidental.

## FRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

At examination level the mechanism is used to argue about prevention and about
what else to look for, which are the two things it genuinely supports.

Prevention programmes target this mechanism and reduce injury across groups,
while which component does the work is not isolated. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The same mechanism predicts the associated injuries, and the candidate is
expected to name them before being asked. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

What it does not support is an individual causal account, which is where it is
most often overreached. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is which element of a prevention programme is active,
because the programmes train several things at once. 6.6.3 covers the
neuromuscular factors.

The second is whether video analysis of elite injuries describes the
recreational mechanism, which is where most of these injuries happen. 6.1.5
covers the mechanisms of injury.

The third is whether a non-contact mechanism should change the threshold for
looking at the anterolateral structures. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- The mechanism supports prevention and the search for associated injury.
- It does not support an individual causal account.
- Which element of a prevention programme is active is unresolved.
- Elite video analysis may not describe the recreational mechanism.
- Whether it should change the anterolateral threshold is open.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the non-contact mechanism matters because it describes a
population that will go back to the same movement.

A patient returning to the sport that produced the injury returns to the
mechanism, and the contralateral knee is exposed to it as much as the operated
one. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

That makes prevention part of the post-operative plan rather than something that
belonged before the injury, and it is the part most often dropped once an
operation is scheduled. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The patient's intention about return is therefore prognostic information, and it
is obtained by asking rather than by imaging. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The honest summary is that the mechanism is the most modifiable thing in this
chapter and the least attended to after the operating list is booked. 6.5.1
covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- A patient returning to the sport returns to the mechanism.
- The contralateral knee is exposed as much as the operated one.
- Prevention belongs in the post-operative plan.
- Intention about return is prognostic and is obtained by asking.
- The most modifiable thing here gets the least attention after booking.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant tension is that this mechanism invites an explanation the
evidence will not carry for an individual.

Across a squad, landing and cutting technique is modifiable and programmes work.
For the person in clinic, attributing their injury to their technique is a claim
about one event that no study supports. 6.6.3 covers the neuromuscular factors.

Getting that wrong has a cost beyond accuracy: a patient who believes they
caused the injury behaves differently in rehabilitation and on return. 6.5.1
covers instability.

What to say turns on the same five things. The patient's age and physiology,
which set healing and what training can still change. Their medical
comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which decide whether the
mechanism will be met again at all. Their psychological state, because belief
about cause shapes confidence. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations,
including whatever they have already concluded about why it happened.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

### Key Learning Points

- Technique is modifiable across a squad and not a verdict on one event.
- Attributing an individual injury to technique outruns the evidence.
- A patient who believes they caused it behaves differently afterwards.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations shape what is said.
- Whether the mechanism will be met again decides how much any of it matters.
