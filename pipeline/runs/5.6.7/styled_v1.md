# Flap Tears

A tear with a mobile leaf that can displace into the gutter or under the
meniscus, which is the pattern most often missed on a report.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A flap tear has a free leaf attached along one edge, so the leaf can lift and
move while the rest of the meniscus stays put. 5.6.3 covers horizontal tears.

The leaf commonly displaces into the gutter beside the meniscus or beneath the
meniscal body, and in either place it is away from where the meniscus is being
looked at. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

It matters because a displaced leaf explains mechanical symptoms in a knee whose
meniscus is reported as only degenerate. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

### How It Is Acquired

Assess the meniscus itself first, since the parent tear is usually a horizontal
or oblique split. 5.6.3 covers horizontal tears.

Look in the gutter, the recess between the meniscus and the capsule, because a
displaced leaf sits there and is outside the usual search area. 5.5.1 covers the
standard protocol.

Look beneath the meniscal body, where a leaf can tuck under and produce a
double layered appearance. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Compare the meniscal height across the slices, since a leaf lifted away makes
the parent thinner.

Assess both the coronal and sagittal images, because a leaf is often visible in
only one. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A tongue of meniscal tissue displaced from its parent, attached along one margin
and lying in the gutter or beneath the meniscus.

A parent meniscus that is thinner or shorter than its opposite number, with a
split running to the displaced leaf.

A double layer where a leaf has tucked under the body, which can be mistaken for
a normal variant. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Fluid outlining the leaf on a fluid sensitive sequence, which is what makes it
visible at all. 5.2.16 covers fat suppression.

### Key Learning Points

- A flap tear has a leaf attached along one edge that can move.
- The leaf displaces into the gutter or beneath the meniscus.
- Both places are outside the usual search area.
- A displaced leaf explains symptoms in a meniscus reported as degenerate.
- The parent meniscus looks thinner where the leaf has lifted.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the mechanical symptom with an unremarkable meniscal report,
and the expected answer says where to look.

The expected points are the parent tear, the gutter, the area beneath the body,
the meniscal height, and both planes.

Say that you would look in the gutter and beneath the meniscus specifically.
5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Say that a flap tear is a common cause of mechanical symptoms with a meniscus
reported as degenerate. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

Say that the leaf may have reduced by the time of scanning. 4.9.8 covers
mechanical locking.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a leaf attached along one margin behaves like a
hinge, so it moves with the knee and catches between the surfaces. 3.6.3 covers
the circumferential fibres.

Because it remains attached it is not a loose body, which is why it produces
catching rather than true locking. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

Its displacement into the gutter takes it out of the joint space, which is why
the meniscus itself can look unremarkable. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

The parent split is usually degenerate, which is why this pattern appears in
older knees with a mechanical history. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

### How Good It Is

This is the meniscal pattern most often missed, because the displaced tissue is
outside the structure being assessed.

Its best contribution, when found, is explaining a mechanical symptom that the
meniscal report did not account for. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

It is missed when the search is confined to the meniscus and when the leaf has
reduced. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- A leaf attached along one margin hinges and catches.
- It is not a loose body, so it catches rather than truly locking.
- Displacement into the gutter leaves the meniscus looking unremarkable.
- The parent split is usually degenerate.
- This is the pattern most often missed.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a displaced flap is often the finding that justifies operating
in a knee that would otherwise be managed without surgery.

A mechanical symptom with a displaced leaf is a different proposition from the
same symptom with degenerate signal alone. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the
worn knee.

The leaf is usually resected rather than repaired, because the parent tissue is
degenerate, which makes this one of the remaining clear indications in a worn
knee. 5.6.3 covers horizontal tears.

The compartment's cartilage still governs the expected benefit, so the flap is a
reason to look rather than a reason to proceed. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

A specific request to look for a displaced flap changes what the report says,
which is the clearest case in this chapter for stating the clinical question.
5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is confining the search to the meniscus. 5.6.1 covers the
normal meniscus.

A reduced leaf gives a study that does not explain the symptoms. 4.9.8 covers
mechanical locking.

A leaf tucked beneath the body is read as a normal double layer. 5.6.1 covers
the normal meniscus.

The parent tear is reported and the displacement is not, which loses the part
that matters. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

A displaced leaf in an arthritic knee is taken as the whole explanation when the
compartment is also worn. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

### Key Learning Points

- Confining the search to the meniscus is the commonest error.
- A reduced leaf gives a study that does not explain the symptoms.
- A leaf tucked beneath the body mimics a normal double layer.
- Reporting the parent tear without the displacement loses what matters.
- A displaced leaf is not the whole explanation in a worn compartment.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the flap tear is the strongest argument in this chapter for
asking the radiologist a question rather than reading a report.

The finding is conspicuous once looked for and invisible when not, so its
detection depends on the request more than on the protocol. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

A leaf's position varies with knee position and time, so a scan is one sample of
a mobile fragment. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

The gutter and the subpopliteal recess are anatomically variable, which makes a
small displaced leaf hard to distinguish from a normal fold. 2.6.2 covers the
popliteomeniscal fascicles.

In a degenerate knee the flap coexists with everything else that is abnormal, and
attributing the symptom to it is a judgement rather than a finding. 4.9.11 covers
the examination in the worn knee.

Resecting a flap in a worn compartment relieves the catching and does not change
the compartment, which is the expectation the consent conversation needs. 3.6.5
covers load sharing.

### Key Learning Points

- Detection depends on the request more than on the protocol.
- A scan is one sample of a mobile fragment.
- The gutter is anatomically variable, so a small leaf is hard to call.
- In a degenerate knee attributing the symptom to the flap is a judgement.
- Resecting it relieves the catching and does not change the compartment.
