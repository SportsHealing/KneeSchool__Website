# Varus Motion

Opening the outer side of the joint. A small motion, easily confused with a
fixed alignment, and the one a varus thrust makes visible.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Varus motion is angulation in the coronal plane that opens the lateral side of
the joint and closes the medial side.

It is a small motion in a normal knee, and most of what is called varus in a
clinic is an alignment rather than a motion. The two are different things and
the distinction is worth holding.

Varus alignment is where the limb sits. Varus motion is how far the joint
opens when a force is applied. A limb can have one without the other.

### At the Knee

In full extension the joint resists varus through the lateral collateral
ligament, the capsule, the cruciates and the congruence of the surfaces
together.

At about 30 degrees of flexion the capsule and cruciates contribute less, so
the test is done at that angle to isolate the collateral ligament. 2.10.1
covers it.

Conventional teaching puts normal lateral opening at a few millimetres, with
the difference between sides being the measurement that matters.

Varus thrust is varus motion seen in gait: the knee moves into varus during
stance and returns as the limb unloads.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which decides how much the secondary restraints contribute.

Alignment. A varus limb puts the load line medial to the knee centre, which
produces a varus moment at every step and loads the lateral structures.

Injury to the lateral collateral ligament increases it at 30 degrees; injury
to the corner and cruciates increases it in extension as well.

Medial compartment cartilage loss increases varus alignment, which then
increases the varus moment, which accelerates the loss.

### Clinical Relevance

Varus laxity at 30 degrees alone suggests the lateral collateral ligament.
Laxity in full extension as well suggests the corner and a cruciate. 4.13
covers the examination.

Varus thrust seen on walking is a stronger finding than a table test, because
it is the knee under its own load rather than the examiner's.

Alignment and laxity are recorded separately, since they have different causes
and different treatments.

### Key Learning Points

- Varus motion opens the lateral side; varus alignment is where the limb sits.
- A limb can have one without the other, and both are recorded separately.
- Testing at 30 degrees isolates the lateral collateral ligament from the secondary restraints.
- Conventional teaching puts normal lateral opening at a few millimetres.
- Varus thrust in gait is a stronger finding than a table test.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Separate three quantities that share the word varus: mechanical alignment,
measured on a long leg radiograph; coronal laxity, measured by stress; and the
dynamic varus thrust observed in gait.

They interact. A varus limb loads the lateral restraints, which stretch, which
increases laxity, which increases thrust, which increases the medial load.

That loop is the mechanical account of why uncorrected varus with lateral
laxity progresses.

### At the Knee

The lateral collateral ligament is the primary restraint to varus, carrying
the large majority at 30 degrees of flexion. 3.10.3 carries the apportionment.

The posterolateral corner and the cruciates are secondary, and their
contribution rises towards extension.

Conventional teaching puts the increase in lateral opening after isolated
collateral sectioning at a few millimetres, and considerably more when the
corner is divided too.

The medial compartment is the fulcrum in a varus limb, so varus motion and
medial compartment load are geometrically linked.

### What Changes It

Alignment is the dominant variable and the only one with an effect at every
step.

Flexion angle changes which structures are being tested.

Cartilage and meniscal loss on the medial side increase varus alignment
directly, by reducing the height of the medial compartment.

Muscle activity resists thrust dynamically, which is why quadriceps strength
changes the gait finding without changing the laxity.

### How It Is Measured

Varus stress testing is graded by the opening felt and by the endpoint, in
millimetres by convention.

Stress radiography quantifies it, and is the method that gives a number rather
than a grade.

Long leg standing radiographs measure alignment, which is a different quantity
measured in degrees. 5.3.2 covers alignment imaging.

Gait observation detects thrust, and no instrument is needed to see it.

### Clinical Relevance

Watch the patient walk before testing on a couch, since thrust is a load
bearing finding that a table test does not reproduce.

Measure alignment on a long leg film in any knee with coronal laxity, because
the two together decide the treatment.

Test at 0 and 30 degrees and read the pair, since the pattern separates the
collateral from the corner.

Expect laxity and alignment to drive each other, which is the argument for
correcting alignment alongside any lateral reconstruction.

### Key Learning Points

- Three quantities share the word varus: alignment, laxity and thrust.
- They form a loop, which is why uncorrected varus with lateral laxity progresses.
- The lateral collateral ligament carries the large majority of varus restraint at 30 degrees.
- Medial cartilage and meniscal loss increase varus alignment directly.
- Thrust is seen by walking the patient, not by testing on a couch.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat varus as a system rather than a sign. Alignment, laxity, thrust and
medial compartment state are four measurements of one problem and each is
treated differently.

A lateral soft tissue reconstruction in an uncorrected varus limb is loaded
every step by the limb rather than by the injury, which 2.10.4 states and
which is the commonest reason such reconstructions stretch out.

The fulcrum argument means an osteotomy changes the lever arm for every
subsequent intervention.

### What Changes It

Osteotomy changes alignment and therefore every other term. It is the
intervention with the largest mechanical effect per unit of surgery at this
joint.

Meniscal and cartilage restoration change compartment height and therefore
alignment, by smaller amounts.

Soft tissue reconstruction changes laxity and leaves alignment untouched,
which is why sequence matters.

### How It Is Measured

Stress radiography gives the number, long leg films give the alignment, and
gait analysis or simple observation gives the thrust. Three methods for one
problem.

Published laxity figures from sectioning studies are measured without
alignment, so they describe a knee that does not exist in the patients who
have the problem.

Weight bearing measurement is the one that matters and the one least often
done.

### Clinical Relevance

Correct alignment before or with a lateral reconstruction in a varus limb, and
say so in the plan rather than treating it as optional.

Measure on weight bearing films, since a supine measurement describes an
unloaded knee.

Record thrust explicitly, because it predicts the mechanical environment a
construct will live in.

Avoid reconstructing laxity in a limb whose alignment caused it.

### Key Learning Points

- Alignment, laxity, thrust and compartment state are four measurements of one problem.
- A lateral reconstruction in an uncorrected varus limb is loaded by the limb, not the injury.
- Osteotomy has the largest mechanical effect per unit of surgery at this joint.
- Sectioning figures describe a knee without the alignment the patient has.
- Reconstructing laxity in a limb whose alignment caused it repeats the failure.
