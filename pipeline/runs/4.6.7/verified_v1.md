# Tibial Tubercle Tenderness

A single bony point, and the four diagnoses it separates by age alone.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The tibial tubercle is the patellar tendon's insertion, and tenderness over it
indicates a problem at that insertion.

Which problem depends mostly on the patient's age: a growing adolescent, a
skeletally mature athlete, and an adult with a previous surgery or injury each
have a different diagnosis at the same tender point. 4.2.9 covers onset.

It is also a landmark the rest of the examination uses, for the drawer tests and
for the rotational assessment, so finding it reliably matters beyond this page.
4.11.2 covers one of those tests.

### How It Is Performed

With the knee flexed to about ninety degrees, find the tubercle as the bony
prominence below the patella where the tendon inserts.

Palpate the tubercle itself, then the tendon immediately above it, then the bone
below it. 4.6.6 covers the tendon.

Note whether the tubercle is enlarged, which it commonly is in a patient who had
a traction problem in adolescence.

Ask the patient to extend the knee against resistance and note whether that
reproduces the pain, which localises it to the insertion.

Look for a scar or a palpable screw, since a tubercle osteotomy or a fixation
device sits exactly here. 4.3.7 covers surgical scars.

### What a Positive Means

Tenderness with an enlarged tubercle in a growing adolescent indicates a
traction apophysitis, which is self limiting.

Tenderness in a skeletally mature athlete indicates an insertional tendinopathy
or, with the right history, an avulsion. 4.6.6 covers the tendon.

Tenderness with an inability to extend the knee after a sudden load indicates an
avulsion of the tendon or the tubercle, which is urgent. 4.7.8 covers extension
lag.

Tenderness over a palpable device after a tubercle osteotomy indicates prominent
hardware, which is a common and treatable cause of persisting anterior pain.
4.3.7 covers scars.

Tenderness here with tenderness at the inferior pole of the patella suggests the
whole tendon rather than one end of it. 4.6.5 covers the patella.

### Key Learning Points

- The tubercle is the patellar tendon's insertion, and tenderness localises to that insertion.
- The diagnosis depends mostly on the patient's age.
- An enlarged tubercle in an adolescent indicates traction apophysitis.
- Tenderness with an inability to extend after a sudden load is an avulsion and is urgent.
- Tenderness over a palpable device after an osteotomy is prominent hardware.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the tubercle is usually a route into a question about the extensor
mechanism or about an adolescent knee, and the age based differential is the
answer.

The expected points are the position, the palpation of the tubercle and the
tendon above it, the enlargement, the resisted extension, and the search for
hardware.

Say that you would test active extension against gravity in any patient with
tubercle tenderness and a history of sudden load. 4.7.1 covers active motion.

Say that an adolescent with this finding and an open apophysis has a different
problem from an adult, and that the radiograph confirms the skeletal maturity.
4.1.6 covers that relationship.

Say that you would use the tubercle as a landmark for the rest of the
examination deliberately, which is a practical point rather than a diagnostic
one. 4.11.2 covers one such test.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the patellar tendon transmits the whole extensor
force to the tibia through this point, so it is a site of high stress and
therefore of overload and avulsion injury. 2.4.3 covers patellar biomechanics.

In a growing skeleton the apophysis is the weakest part of the chain, which is
why traction injury occurs there in adolescence and at the tendon in
adults. 2.4.2 covers ossification.

The tubercle's position relative to the patella and the trochlea determines the
tendon's line of pull, which is why a tubercle osteotomy is used to treat
patellar instability and maltracking. 2.4.4 covers tracking.

Its lateral position relative to the trochlear groove is the basis of the
measurement used in planning those operations, and the examination's estimate of
it is the rotational assessment. 4.5.6 covers rotational alignment.

### How Good It Is

Palpation here is precise, because the landmark is bony and superficial and the
tenderness is focal.

Its specificity comes almost entirely from the age and the history rather than
from the finding, which is unusual and worth stating.

It diagnoses an avulsion reliably when combined with the active extension test,
which is the consequential use. 4.7.8 covers it.

It cannot distinguish an insertional tendinopathy from a partial avulsion, which
imaging does. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The tendon transmits the whole extensor force through this point.
- In a growing skeleton the apophysis is the weakest part of the chain.
- The tubercle's position determines the tendon's line of pull, which is why it is moved surgically.
- Specificity comes from the age and the history rather than from the finding.
- With the active extension test it diagnoses an avulsion reliably.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the finding makes one urgent diagnosis and identifies one
common and treatable cause of persisting pain.

The urgent diagnosis is an avulsion, which is operative and time sensitive, and
the examination makes it. 4.7.8 covers extension lag.

The treatable cause is prominent hardware after a tubercle osteotomy, which is
simple to address and frequently attributed to something else. 4.3.7 covers
surgical scars.

In an adolescent the finding is reassurance rather than a decision, and the
examination's contribution is confidence that nothing further is needed.

In a patient being planned for a tubercle osteotomy the examination's rotational
assessment contributes to the question of whether the tubercle's position is
part of the problem. 4.5.6 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not testing active extension in a patient with acute
tenderness after a load.

The second is attributing adult tubercle pain to a childhood apophysitis, which
has resolved.

The third is missing prominent hardware in a patient whose anterior pain
followed an osteotomy.

The fourth is reading an enlarged tubercle as an active problem, when it is
usually the healed remnant of an old one.

The fifth is attributing anterior pain to the tubercle without examining the
patella and the tendon's whole length. 4.6.5 covers the patella.

### Key Learning Points

- The urgent diagnosis is an avulsion, and the examination makes it.
- Prominent hardware after an osteotomy is a common, treatable and misattributed cause of pain.
- In an adolescent the finding is reassurance rather than a decision.
- An enlarged tubercle is usually the healed remnant of an old problem.
- Not testing active extension after an acute load is how an avulsion is missed.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this point is most often relevant in the knee that has had
a tubercle osteotomy and still hurts.

A patient with localised tenderness over a screw has prominent hardware, and
removing it is a small operation with a reliable result, which makes it worth
finding. 4.3.7 covers scars.

A patient with diffuse tenderness around a healed osteotomy and no palpable
device has a different problem, and the examination has already distinguished
them.

A patient with a previous bone patellar tendon bone graft harvest has a donor
site at exactly this point, and donor site pain is common, under recognised and
not a reconstruction failure. 4.10.11 covers post-reconstruction assessment.

A patient with a non union of a tubercle osteotomy has tenderness with movement
at the site, and that is a finding the examination can detect before the
imaging is reviewed.

In medicolegal work an avulsion diagnosed late is an avoidable failure, and the
missing element is usually the active extension test rather than the palpation.
4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The commonest relevance is the knee that has had a tubercle osteotomy and still hurts.
- Localised tenderness over a screw is prominent hardware, with a reliable small operation.
- A bone patellar tendon bone harvest site sits at exactly this point.
- Tenderness with movement at an osteotomy site suggests a non union.
- A late diagnosed avulsion usually traces to a missing active extension test.
