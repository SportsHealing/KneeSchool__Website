# Superficial MCL

The largest structure on the medial side: where it starts, the two places it
ends, and why it is the one medial injury that usually heals.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The superficial medial collateral ligament is a long flat band running down
the inner side of the knee. Conventional teaching gives its length as about 10
cm, which makes it the longest ligament at the knee.

Its femoral attachment is in a shallow depression close to the medial
epicondyle, slightly above it and slightly behind it. Conventional teaching
places the centre about 3 mm proximal and 5 mm posterior to the epicondyle
itself.

It has two tibial attachments rather than one. The proximal attachment is to
soft tissue about 12 mm below the joint line, over the anterior arm of
semimembranosus. The distal attachment is to bone on the tibia about 6 cm
below the joint line, under the pes anserinus tendons.

The medial side is described in three layers. Layer one is the fascia over
sartorius, layer two is this ligament, and layer three is the capsule. 2.9.2
and 2.9.4 use the same scheme.

It is extra-articular. Nothing about it sits inside the joint, which is the
single fact that explains most of its behaviour after injury.

### Relations

Superficial to it: the fascia of layer one, then the pes anserinus tendons of
sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus running across to their insertion in
front of it.

Deep to it: a bursa, then the deep medial collateral ligament and the capsule.
The bursa is what lets the two ligaments move on each other.

Behind it: the posterior oblique ligament, which 2.9.3 covers, and the
posteromedial corner.

Crossing the field: the saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch, and the
medial inferior genicular artery running under the ligament close to the joint
line.

### Function

The ligament is the primary restraint to valgus force. That is its main job
and it is what a valgus stress test examines.

Its two tibial attachments do different work. The proximal division resists
external rotation and the distal division resists internal rotation, so the
ligament restrains rotation in both directions as well as valgus.

It is tightest in slight flexion rather than in full extension, which is why
the stress test is done at about 20 degrees. 3.9.3 covers its contribution
through range.

### Clinical Relevance

Valgus laxity at about 20 degrees of flexion with a firm endpoint is the
finding that localises an injury to this ligament. Laxity in full extension
points further back, to the posteromedial corner.

It usually heals. Three things favour it: the ligament sits outside the joint,
it has a good blood supply along its length, and its torn ends stay in
contact. The anterior cruciate has none of the three, which is why one injury
is treated in a brace and the other in theatre.

The two tibial attachments mean an injury can involve one and spare the other,
and the pattern of rotation on examination says which.

The saphenous nerve crosses the field, so numbness over the inner shin after a
medial injury is a nerve finding rather than a ligament one.

### Key Learning Points

- Conventional teaching gives a length of about 10 cm, the longest ligament at the knee.
- The femoral attachment is about 3 mm proximal and 5 mm posterior to the medial epicondyle.
- There are two tibial attachments: soft tissue about 12 mm below the joint line and bone about 6 cm below it.
- It is the primary restraint to valgus and it resists rotation in both directions.
- It is extra-articular with a good blood supply, which is why it usually heals.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe the femoral attachment by the depression rather than by the
epicondyle. The epicondyle is a prominence and the attachment sits in the
hollow behind and above it, which is why an attachment described as on the
epicondyle is usually drawn too far forward.

Conventional teaching gives the femoral footprint an area of the order of 100
square millimetres and the distal tibial footprint rather more, spread along
the bone.

The proximal tibial attachment is not to bone. It attaches to the soft tissue
over the anterior arm of semimembranosus, which couples the ligament to a
muscle and gives the medial side a dynamic component. 2.9.5 and 2.9.6 carry
that.

The ligament is broad proximally and narrows as it descends, then widens again
at the distal attachment.

### Relations

The three layer description is the one to use in a viva. Layer one is the deep
crural fascia continuous with the sartorius fascia; layer two is this ligament
with the patellofemoral ligaments anteriorly; layer three is the capsule,
thickened centrally as the deep medial collateral ligament.

Anteriorly the layers are distinct. Posteriorly layers two and three merge,
and the posterior oblique ligament is that merged tissue.

The pes anserinus tendons lie in layer one over the distal attachment, with
the pes bursa between them and the ligament.

The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the field transversely
between sartorius and gracilis. It is the structure most often injured in a
medial approach.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The supply is from the superior and inferior medial genicular arteries, with
contributions from the descending genicular artery proximally.

The vessels reach the ligament along its whole length rather than at one
point, which is the vascular difference from the cruciates and the reason this
ligament repairs itself.

Nerve supply is from the medial articular branches and from branches of the
saphenous nerve. The ligament carries mechanoreceptors near both attachments.

### Function

It provides the large majority of the restraint to valgus through the mid
range. The capsule and the cruciates take a larger share near full extension.

The proximal and distal divisions behave differently through flexion: the
proximal division tightens in flexion and the distal division in extension.
3.9.3 and 3.9.9 carry the detail.

Through its proximal attachment to semimembranosus it is tensioned by muscle
as well as by position, so it is partly a dynamic restraint.

### Clinical Relevance

Examine at 20 to 30 degrees for this ligament and at 0 degrees for the
posteromedial corner. Reading the two together localises the injury, and 4.12
covers the examination.

Grade by the opening and the endpoint rather than by the pain, because an
acute injury is painful whatever its grade.

Expect healing in most isolated injuries. The question is the position in
which it heals, which is why the knee is held rather than simply rested.

Protect the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve in any medial
approach. The nerve crosses where the incision runs, and 2.9.7 covers the
landmarks.

### Key Learning Points

- The femoral attachment is in the depression behind and above the epicondyle, not on it.
- The proximal tibial attachment is to soft tissue over semimembranosus, not to bone.
- Layer one is fascia, layer two is this ligament, layer three is capsule; two and three merge posteriorly.
- Supply is from the medial genicular arteries along its whole length, which is why it heals.
- The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the field transversely.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The anatomical boundary that matters is the point where layers two and three
merge. In front of it the ligament can be isolated; behind it the posterior
oblique ligament and the capsule are one sheet, and an injury there is a
corner injury rather than a ligament one.

The proximal tibial attachment couples the ligament to semimembranosus. An
injury that strips that attachment removes a dynamic tensioner as well as a
static restraint.

The distal tibial attachment lies under the pes. Reaching it means working
through or around three tendons, which is the practical reason the distal end
is approached less often than the proximal.

### Function

Treat valgus restraint as shared rather than solitary. This ligament carries
the large majority through mid flexion; near extension the posteromedial
corner and the cruciates carry more, and a knee lax in extension has lost more
than this ligament.

Rotation restraint divides between the two tibial attachments, so an injury
pattern can produce rotational laxity with little valgus laxity.

Anteromedial rotatory instability is the combination of this ligament with the
posteromedial corner and, usually, the anterior cruciate. 3.9.7 carries the
mechanics and 2.9.4 the anatomy.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess rotation as well as valgus before calling a medial injury isolated. The
anatomy divides the work between two attachments and the examination has to
follow it.

Consider the healing position rather than only the healing. A ligament that
heals long leaves a knee that opens, and the anatomy of the two attachments
explains why position matters more here than in most ligaments.

Where a reconstruction is planned, the femoral attachment is found from the
depression behind the epicondyle and the distal tibial attachment from the
measurement below the joint line, not from the appearance of the remaining
tissue. 2.9.7 covers the landmarks.

Protect the dynamic coupling at the proximal tibial attachment where the
tissue allows it. Restoring the static restraint without it restores part of
the anatomy.

### Key Learning Points

- In front of the merge point the ligament is isolable; behind it, an injury is a corner injury.
- The proximal tibial attachment is a dynamic coupling to semimembranosus, not just a second anchor.
- Valgus restraint is shared, so laxity in extension means more than this ligament has failed.
- The two tibial attachments split rotation restraint between external and internal.
- Landmarks, not residual tissue, locate the attachments in a reconstruction.
