# Valgus OA

The knock kneed worn knee, less common than its varus counterpart and different
in almost every respect the examination tests.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A valgus knee with lateral compartment wear presents as lateral joint line
pain, a knock kneed appearance worse on standing, and sometimes a feeling of
the knee giving way medially. 4.5.3 covers valgus alignment.

The examination is deciding the size of the deformity, whether it corrects, and
whether the medial side has stretched. 4.3.10 covers the deformity.

It matters that this is the less common pattern, because a valgus knee raises
the possibility of an inflammatory or a post traumatic cause rather than simple
wear. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history: the lateral pain, the duration, whether there was a previous
fracture or a previous lateral meniscectomy, and any inflammatory features.
4.2.9 covers the onset.

Look at the patient standing from the front with the knees together, because
the deformity is loaded. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Watch the gait from the front for the knee moving medially during stance, which
is the medial thrust. 4.4.6 covers the arthritic gait.

Palpate the lateral joint line in flexion for tenderness and osteophyte. 4.6.4
covers joint line tenderness.

Assess correctability with a varus force near extension, recording how much the
alignment improves. 4.13.2 covers the lateral collateral assessment.

Assess the medial collateral ligament, since a long standing valgus knee
stretches the medial structures. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Measure the range and record any fixed flexion separately, and look for an
external rotation deformity, which accompanies valgus wear. 4.5.4 covers the
flexion deformity.

### What a Positive Means

Lateral joint line tenderness with a standing valgus deformity and medial
stretching is the characteristic picture.

A correctable valgus deformity means limited lateral bone loss and an
uncontracted lateral side. 4.3.10 covers the deformity.

Medial laxity with a valgus deformity means the medial side has lengthened and
the deformity is structural. 4.12.5 covers the posterior oblique ligament.

A history of lateral meniscectomy or a lateral tibial plateau fracture explains
the pattern and makes it post traumatic rather than primary. 4.9.10 covers
meniscal deficiency.

Symmetrical valgus knees with inflammatory features point away from mechanical
wear. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus wear is the less common pattern and raises secondary causes.
- The deformity is assessed standing, because it is loaded.
- Medial laxity means the medial side has lengthened.
- A previous lateral meniscectomy or plateau fracture explains the pattern.
- An external rotation component commonly accompanies valgus wear.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the valgus knee station and the expected answer names the
secondary causes early.

The expected points are the standing alignment, the gait, the lateral joint
line, the correctability, the medial ligament, the range, the rotational
component and the history of trauma or inflammation.

Say that you would ask about previous lateral meniscal surgery and plateau
fracture, since both produce this pattern. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

Say that you would look for inflammatory features and examine other joints,
because symmetrical valgus knees suggest a systemic cause. 4.8.9 covers
synovitis.

Say that you would assess the hip, since a valgus knee alters hip loading and
referred pain is common in this age group. 4.2.15 covers referred pain.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that lateral wear lowers the lateral side, the limb
angulates into valgus, the lateral load rises, and the cycle continues in the
mirror image of the varus pattern. 3.5.2 covers contact pressures.

The medial side lengthens under the sustained valgus, which is why medial
laxity accompanies an established deformity. 4.12.5 covers the posterior
oblique ligament.

Valgus wear commonly carries an external rotation and a flexion component,
which is why the deformity is not purely coronal. 4.19.7 covers torsional
assessment.

A lateral meniscectomy removes the lateral load distribution, so the lateral
compartment wears faster, which is why the history matters. 4.9.10 covers
meniscal deficiency.

### How Good It Is

The examination is reliable for the direction and the presence of the deformity
and for the medial laxity.

Its best contribution is the history of a secondary cause, which changes the
whole assessment and which no imaging asks about. 4.2.9 covers the onset.

It is imprecise about the magnitude and poor at separating the femoral and the
tibial contributions.

Long leg films measure the deformity and localise it, so the examination
contributes the soft tissue state and the cause. 4.1.6 covers that
relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Lateral wear and valgus alignment form the mirror image of the varus cycle.
- The medial side lengthens under sustained valgus.
- Valgus wear commonly carries external rotation and flexion components.
- A previous lateral meniscectomy accelerates lateral wear.
- The examination contributes the cause, which no imaging asks about.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a valgus knee is sorted by magnitude, correctability, the
state of the medial side and whether the cause is primary or secondary.

A moderate correctable valgus deformity with lateral pain and a competent
medial side is the group where load redistribution is considered, and the
examination identifies it. 4.20.4 covers the osteotomy assessment.

A fixed valgus deformity with medial laxity sits outside it, because correcting
the alignment alone leaves the medial side long. 4.12.5 covers the posterior
oblique ligament.

An inflammatory cause changes the treatment entirely, so the examination for
other joints and for systemic features is part of the decision. 4.8.9 covers
synovitis.

Lateral wear in a young patient after meniscectomy is a different problem from
primary wear in an older one, and the examination finds the difference by
history. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording the deformity supine, where valgus understates
itself as much as varus does. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Medial laxity in a valgus knee can be relative rather than absolute, since the
shortened lateral side makes a normal medial side look long. 4.12.5 covers it.

A valgus knee with an external rotation component is described as a pure
coronal deformity, which loses information the correction needs. 4.19.7 covers
torsional assessment.

Inflammatory disease is attributed to mechanical wear when the knees are
examined one at a time rather than compared. 4.1.4 covers the structured
approach.

Medial laxity measured in extension in a knee with a flexion deficit is
unreliable, since the position changes the restraint being tested. 4.12.3 covers the assessment in flexion.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus understates itself supine as much as varus does.
- Medial laxity may be relative rather than absolute.
- Describing a valgus knee as purely coronal loses information.
- Inflammatory disease is missed when the knees are examined one at a time.
- Medial laxity in a flexion deficit knee is unreliable.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the valgus knee is harder than the varus knee, because its
deformity is more often multiplanar and its secondary causes are more varied.

The combination of valgus, external rotation and fixed flexion is common, and
the examination describes the first two poorly and the third well. 4.5.4 covers
the flexion deformity.

Medial lengthening and lateral bone loss produce the same clinical laxity, and
the examination cannot separate them in a single assessment. 4.12.5 covers the
posterior oblique ligament.

A valgus knee after a plateau fracture carries a joint line that is not where
the examination assumes, which affects every localising sign. 4.6.4 covers the
technique.

Peroneal nerve function is examined in a valgus knee as a preoperative
baseline, not as a diagnostic step, and omitting it creates a later problem.
4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa.

The examination cannot say whether the lateral compartment has bone loss or
cartilage loss alone, and the distinction changes the correction. 4.17.5 covers
articular defects.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus deformity is more often multiplanar than varus deformity.
- Medial lengthening and lateral bone loss give the same clinical laxity.
- A post fracture joint line is not where the localising signs assume.
- Peroneal nerve function is a preoperative baseline in a valgus knee.
- Bone loss and cartilage loss are not distinguishable clinically.
