# Combined PCL and PLC Injury

The combination that defeats a posterior cruciate reconstruction. Each
structure is the other's backup, and neither heals well.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Two injuries together are worse than the sum when each structure was also a
secondary restraint for the other's direction.

If neither structure heals well, the combination does not resolve with time
the way a medial injury does.

So the combination is both mechanically worse and clinically less forgiving.

### At the Knee

The posterior cruciate and the posterolateral corner both resist posterior
translation and external rotation, in different proportions at different
angles. 3.8.2 and 3.10.2 cover them.

Losing both produces a larger posterior and rotational laxity than either
alone, which is the apportionment showing itself. 3.8.1 covers the cruciate's
functions.

Neither heals reliably. The cruciate heals partly and at length, and the
corner heals badly. 3.10.6 covers the corner.

Conventional teaching identifies an unaddressed corner injury as the commonest
cause of posterior cruciate graft failure. 3.8.8 covers the construct.

### What Changes It

Whether the corner is identified and reconstructed.

Alignment, since varus loads both. 3.13 covers alignment.

Chronicity, since the structures stretch.

Peroneal nerve status, which can accompany the corner injury. 2.10.6 covers
the nerve.

### Clinical Relevance

Examining for the corner in every posterior cruciate injury is the practical
consequence, and the dial test is how. 3.10.7 covers the test.

Reconstructing the cruciate without the corner reproduces the failure, which
is why both are addressed. Section 6 covers management.

A varus limb with corner laxity needs the alignment corrected as well, which
makes this a multi procedure problem.

6.24 covers the clinical condition.

### Key Learning Points

- Two injuries together are worse than the sum when each was the other's backup.
- The cruciate and the corner both resist posterior translation and external rotation.
- Losing both produces a larger laxity than either alone.
- Neither heals reliably, so the combination does not resolve with time.
- An unaddressed corner injury is the commonest cause of cruciate graft failure here.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The mechanical statement is shared restraint in two directions, so the
combined deficit exceeds the sum. 3.7.3 covers secondary restraints as a
general idea.

The clinical statement is that neither structure heals, which removes the
option of waiting that the medial side offers. 3.9.10 covers that contrast.

Together they make this the combination where reconstruction of both is the
usual answer rather than a selected one.

### At the Knee

Sectioning studies show that dividing the corner in a posterior cruciate
deficient knee increases posterior translation and external rotation more than
dividing it in an intact knee.

The dial test at 30 and 90 degrees is the clinical separation: worse at 90
implicates the cruciate and a finding at 30 implicates the corner. 3.10.7
covers the mechanics.

Varus thrust and hyperextension in gait are the chronic findings, and 3.14
covers gait in detail.

Alignment interacts with both. A varus limb loads the corner in stance and
increases the posterior and rotational demand. 3.13 covers alignment.

### What Changes It

Whether both structures are reconstructed.

Alignment and whether it is corrected.

Chronicity and the degree of stretching.

Peroneal nerve function.

### How It Is Measured

Stress radiography measures the posterior component reproducibly.

Dial test at two angles measures the rotational component without an
instrument.

Long leg radiography measures the alignment.

Robotic testing apportions the restraint in the combined state, which is the
relevant experiment.

### Clinical Relevance

Perform a dial test at both angles in every posterior cruciate injury.

Reconstruct both structures rather than the cruciate alone.

Measure alignment, since varus loads both.

Document peroneal nerve function.

### Key Learning Points

- Shared restraint in two directions means the combined deficit exceeds the sum.
- Neither structure heals, so waiting is not an option here.
- Reconstruction of both is the usual answer rather than a selected one.
- The dial test at two angles separates the contributions.
- A varus limb loads the corner and raises the demand on both.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat this as the combination where the environment, the structures and the
alignment all have to be addressed, and where a single sitting may not be
possible. 3.10.8 covers the corner construct.

Conventional teaching reconstructs both the cruciate and the corner, and
corrects varus alignment where present, because reconstructing one leaves the
loads that defeated it.

Tunnel convergence is the geometric constraint that decides the technique: a
posterior cruciate tibial tunnel and the corner's tunnels compete, which is an
argument for an inlay. 3.8.10 covers the inlay.

### What Changes It

Alignment and whether an osteotomy is included.

Tunnel trajectories and convergence.

Graft availability for three or four limbs.

Peroneal nerve status, which may limit the dissection.

### How It Is Measured

Computed tomography plans the tunnels and assesses convergence.

Stress radiography and the dial test measure the two components before and
after.

Long leg radiography measures and plans the alignment correction.

Multiligament series report outcomes in small heterogeneous groups, which
limits what can be concluded.

### Clinical Relevance

Reconstruct both structures and correct varus where present.

Plan tunnels to avoid convergence, considering an inlay for the cruciate.

Measure both components before and after, by their own methods.

Counsel that outcomes in this combination are worse than in isolated injury.

### Key Learning Points

- The environment, the structures and the alignment all have to be addressed.
- Reconstructing one leaves the loads that defeated it.
- Tunnel convergence decides the technique and argues for an inlay.
- Multiligament series are small and heterogeneous.
- Outcomes in this combination are worse than in isolated injury.
