# Evidence-Based Imaging

What the literature supports about imaging, and the gap between that and what is
requested.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The imaging literature answers three different questions: whether a modality
detects a lesion, whether detecting it changes management, and whether that
change improves the outcome. 5.1.5 covers cost effectiveness.

Most of it answers the first, some answers the second, and little answers the
third. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

That ordering is the single most useful thing to know about this literature,
because detection studies are read as if they answered the third question.

### Where It Misleads

Detection studies compare a modality against a reference standard, usually
arthroscopy, in patients selected for arthroscopy. That selection inflates the
apparent performance relative to a clinic population. 5.21.1 covers the
meniscal imaging literature.

Management change studies are rarer and are the ones that justify a request.
5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

Outcome studies are rarest and are what a cost effectiveness argument actually
needs.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028.
The ranking of the modalities is standard teaching and recalled robustly; a
percentage is recalled alone and nothing constrains it.

### Key Learning Points

- The literature answers detection, management change and outcome questions.
- Most of it answers detection.
- Detection studies in selected populations overstate clinic performance.
- Management change studies are what justify a request.
- Outcome studies are what a cost argument needs and are rarest.

## MRCS Level

### How Good It Is

In a viva this is the appraisal question and the expected answer separates the
three question types.

The expected points are the reference standard, the selection of the population,
the distinction between detection and management change, and what the result
means for a clinic patient.

Say that arthroscopy as a reference standard carries its own error and is applied
to a selected population. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

Say that a sensitivity from a surgical series does not transfer to a clinic.
5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Say which question a given paper answers before quoting it.

### Where It Misleads

The reasoning expected is that a diagnostic accuracy figure is a property of a
test in a population, not of the test alone, so it moves with the population.

A study in a young athletic cohort and one in an older arthritic cohort produce
different numbers for the same sequence, and both are correct. 5.6.2 covers
meniscal signal grading.

A reference standard that is itself imperfect caps the apparent performance of
anything compared against it.

Technology moves faster than the literature, so the sequence studied is often not
the sequence being used. 5.18.1 covers quantitative imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- An accuracy figure is a property of a test in a population.
- The same sequence gives different numbers in different cohorts, correctly.
- An imperfect reference standard caps apparent performance.
- The sequence studied is often not the sequence in use.
- Name which question a paper answers before quoting it.

## FRCS Level

### Where It Misleads

At decision level the evidence rarely addresses the decision being made, and
pretending otherwise is the error.

The decision is usually whether to operate, and the literature usually reports
whether a lesion was seen. Those are two steps apart. 5.1.5 covers cost
effectiveness.

Guidelines compress this literature into recommendations, which is useful and
hides the question type each recommendation rests on. 5.1.4 covers imaging
pathways.

Where the evidence is thin the honest position is to say so and decide anyway,
because the patient is in the clinic either way.

The literature on advanced techniques is almost entirely detection and
feasibility work, so a clinical claim for them is ahead of its evidence. 5.18.1
covers quantitative imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- The decision is usually two steps from what the literature reports.
- Guidelines hide the question type each recommendation rests on.
- Thin evidence is stated rather than filled in.
- Advanced technique literature is detection and feasibility work.
- A clinical claim for them is ahead of its evidence.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this page's own position is the honest illustration of its
subject.

This section was written without source access, so it states the comparative
standing of the modalities and refuses every figure. That is decision 028 and it
is a worked example of the gap between what is known robustly and what is
recalled precisely. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

A reader who wants the numbers has to go to the literature, and the section says
where rather than approximating. 5.21.8 covers future directions.

Reference standards are shifting, since arthroscopy is being replaced by imaging
in some comparisons, which makes the older accuracy literature hard to compare
with the newer. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

Automated reading is being evaluated against radiologists rather than against
outcomes, which repeats the detection and management confusion one level up.
5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in imaging.

The strongest evidence based statement available about knee imaging is about
which question to ask, and that is a reasoning claim rather than a measured one.
5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

### Key Learning Points

- This section states comparisons and refuses figures, which illustrates its own subject.
- A reader wanting numbers is told where to go rather than given an approximation.
- Shifting reference standards make old and new accuracy work hard to compare.
- Automated reading is evaluated against readers rather than outcomes.
- The strongest claim available is about which question to ask.
