# Post-Reconstruction Assessment

What a treated medial side should feel like, and why a grade on its own tells
you almost nothing about whether the operation worked.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Examining a medial side that has been braced, repaired or reconstructed is
asking two questions: how lax is it now, and is that laxity causing anything.

The second question is the one that matters, because a healed medial ligament
is often slightly lax and entirely asymptomatic. 3.9.3 covers the structure
that heals.

A medial side also has to be read against what was done to it. A reconstructed
ligament, a repaired one and one left to heal in a brace give different
expected findings, and a letter without the operation note is hard to
interpret. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

### How It Is Performed

Establish what was done and when, before touching the knee.

Perform valgus stress at thirty degrees and in full extension, and record both
the opening and the endpoint on each side. 4.12.1 covers the test.

Add the rotational examination, because a corrected valgus laxity with a
residual rotational one is the common incomplete result. 4.12.6 covers the
pattern.

Examine the cruciates, since most medial reconstructions are performed
alongside a cruciate reconstruction and the two results have to be separated.
4.10.11 covers that assessment.

Watch the patient walk and ask them to turn, because the complaint after a
medial operation is usually functional rather than a grade. 4.11.5 covers
dynamic assessment.

### What a Positive Means

A small residual increase in opening at thirty degrees with a firm endpoint is
the expected result after a healed or reconstructed medial side, and it is not a
failure.

A soft endpoint indicates that the repair or graft is not holding, which is a
different finding from a slightly lax firm one.

Opening in full extension after treatment indicates the posteromedial corner
was not addressed or has not held, and that is the commonest incomplete
result. 4.12.2 covers that angle.

A knee that is tighter than the other side has been over constrained, which is
not success and can present as stiffness or as medial compartment pain.

A normal examination in a patient who still complains sends you back to the
history and to the rotational tests rather than to a repeat of the valgus
grade. 4.2.2 covers instability as a symptom.

### Key Learning Points

- A small residual opening with a firm endpoint is the expected result, not a failure.
- A soft endpoint means the repair or graft is not holding.
- Opening in full extension after treatment means the corner was not addressed or has not held.
- A knee tighter than the other side has been over constrained, which is not success.
- A normal examination with a persisting complaint sends you to the rotational tests.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the station where the expected answer starts with what was
done, and a candidate who starts examining has missed the point.

The expected points are the operation note, both valgus angles, the endpoints,
the rotational examination, the cruciates, and the functional assessment.

Say that you would compare with the other knee and with the recorded
pre-operative examination, because the question is almost always whether
something changed. 4.1.8 covers the record.

Say that you would ask when the symptoms returned, since an early return
suggests a technical problem and a late one suggests a graft that stretched.

Say that you would assess alignment, because a varus limb unloads a medial
reconstruction and a valgus limb loads it continuously, and that determines
whether the reconstruction had a chance. 4.11.5 covers the dynamic assessment.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the superficial ligament heals in continuity and
often in a slightly lengthened position, so a residual grade with a firm
endpoint is healing rather than failure. 3.9.3 covers its function.

The posterior oblique ligament heals less reliably, which is why a residual
finding in extension is the more significant one and the one most often
present. 3.9.5 covers it.

A medial reconstruction aims to restore valgus and rotational restraint, and
restoring one without the other leaves the patient's complaint, which is why
the rotational examination is part of the assessment. 3.9.8 covers medial
reconstruction biomechanics.

Where a cruciate reconstruction was performed at the same time, an uncontrolled
medial side loads the cruciate graft, so a stretched cruciate graft and a lax
medial side found together are one problem rather than two. 3.9.10 covers the
combination.

### How Good It Is

The examination is good at establishing the current laxity and poor at
attributing a complaint to it, which is the same limitation the rest of this
chapter has and matters more here.

Comparison with the pre-operative examination is what makes it useful, and the
absence of that record is the commonest reason the assessment cannot conclude.

Stress radiographs are more reproducible than the hands for following a treated
medial side over time, and they are the right investigation where the question
is whether the laxity has increased.

The rotational examination adds the most and is the least reproducible, which
is an uncomfortable combination and is the honest position. 4.12.6 covers it.

No figures appear for any of these comparisons, under decision 023, and the
comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The superficial ligament often heals slightly lengthened, so a residual grade with a firm endpoint is healing.
- The posterior oblique ligament heals less reliably, so the extension finding is the significant one.
- Restoring valgus restraint without rotational restraint leaves the complaint.
- A stretched cruciate graft with a lax medial side is one problem, not two.
- Comparison with the pre-operative examination is what makes this assessment useful.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this examination decides whether anything further is done,
and the answer is usually nothing.

A residual laxity with a firm endpoint and no functional complaint is observed.
Operating on a grade in an asymptomatic patient is the error this page exists
to name.

A patient with a functional complaint, a rotational abnormality and an
acceptable valgus grade has an unaddressed posteromedial corner, and that is
the result that leads to further surgery. 4.12.5 covers the structure.

A patient with a stretched cruciate graft and medial laxity needs both
addressed, and addressing the graft alone repeats the original failure.
4.10.12 covers revision assessment.

Alignment is assessed before any revision soft tissue work, because a valgus
limb will stretch a medial reconstruction again. That is the decision the
examination of the limb, rather than of the knee, contributes.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading a residual grade as failure, which generates
operations on knees that are working.

The second is examining valgus only, which misses the rotational result that
usually explains the complaint.

The third is examining without the operation note, where a reconstruction and a
healed sprain are being held to the same expected finding.

The fourth is attributing a complaint to the medial side without examining the
cruciates, since the cruciate result is the commoner explanation after a
combined operation.

The fifth is examining once. A medial side that is firm at six weeks and lax at
six months has stretched, and that trajectory is the finding. 4.1.8 covers the
record.

### Key Learning Points

- A residual laxity with a firm endpoint and no complaint is observed, not operated on.
- A functional complaint with a rotational abnormality means an unaddressed corner.
- A stretched cruciate graft with medial laxity needs both addressed.
- Alignment is assessed before any revision soft tissue work.
- A medial side firm at six weeks and lax at six months has stretched, and that is the finding.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the treated medial side is usually being examined because
a cruciate reconstruction has not worked, and the medial side is the suspect
rather than the subject.

The commonest finding in that setting is valgus laxity in extension with an
anteromedial rotatory pattern, which together explain a stretched cruciate
graft and are the reason to address both. 4.12.6 covers the pattern.

Scar after a medial repair gives a firm endpoint over a lax joint, so the
endpoint can be reassuring in a knee that fails under load. A patient whose
examination is normal and whose knee gives way in sport is describing that.
4.23.2 covers elite athletes.

An over constrained medial reconstruction presents as medial compartment pain
and loss of flexion rather than as instability, and it is the complication most
often attributed to something else. 4.2.4 covers stiffness.

A medial reconstruction in a valgus limb that was not corrected will stretch,
and finding that at revision means the examination of the limb was not done
rather than that the operation was poor.

In medicolegal work the absence of a recorded pre-operative examination is
usually what makes the question unanswerable, which is an argument for the
record rather than for a better examination. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The treated medial side is usually examined because a cruciate reconstruction failed.
- Valgus laxity in extension with a rotatory pattern explains a stretched cruciate graft.
- Scar can give a reassuring endpoint over a joint that fails under load.
- An over constrained reconstruction presents as medial pain and lost flexion, not instability.
- A reconstruction in an uncorrected valgus limb will stretch, which is an examination failure.
