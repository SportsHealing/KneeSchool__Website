# Referred Pain

The knee is a common destination for pain generated elsewhere. Which sources,
and how the history catches them.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Asking about referred pain tests whether the knee is the source of the symptom
or only the site of it.

The knee is a frequent destination for referred pain, and a patient with knee
pain and a normal knee examination has a cause somewhere else.

The two sources that matter most are the hip and the lumbar spine, and both
are missable if the knee is examined in isolation. 4.1.5 covers the pitfall.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether the pain is only in the knee or whether it extends into the thigh,
the groin or the calf.

Ask about the hip directly: groin pain, pain on putting shoes and socks on,
and difficulty getting in and out of a car.

Ask about the back: back pain, pain radiating below the knee, pins and
needles, numbness and weakness.

Ask whether anything in the back or hip provokes the knee pain.

Then examine the hip and perform a neurological assessment, because the
history selects who needs it and the examination settles it.

### What a Positive Means

Anterior thigh and knee pain with groin pain and restricted hip rotation is
hip disease referring to the knee, and this is the classic missed diagnosis.

Pain radiating from the back below the knee, with altered sensation, is a
radicular cause.

Knee pain in a child or adolescent with a limp and a normal knee is a hip
problem until the hip is imaged, and this is the highest-stakes version.
4.19.1 covers paediatric assessment.

Pain in the calf with swelling is a vascular question rather than a knee one.

Posterior knee pain with no joint findings may be a cyst, a hamstring origin
or a vascular cause. 4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa.

### Key Learning Points

- Referred pain tests whether the knee is the source or only the site.
- The hip and the lumbar spine are the two sources that matter most.
- Knee pain with a normal knee examination has a cause somewhere else.
- A child with knee pain, a limp and a normal knee has a hip problem until imaged.
- The history selects who needs the hip and neurological examination; the examination settles it.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is anatomical: the knee shares innervation with the hip
through the obturator and femoral nerves, which is why hip disease refers
there.

Pain from the hip typically reaches the anterior thigh and knee and does not
reach below the knee.

Radicular pain follows a dermatome and frequently continues below the knee,
with sensory or motor signs.

A knee examination that is entirely normal in a patient with convincing knee
pain is a positive finding, and the next step is the hip and the spine rather
than a knee scan.

Referred pain can coexist with knee pathology, and a degenerate knee with a
degenerate hip is common in older patients, where deciding which is
symptomatic is the clinical problem.

### How Good It Is

The history performs well for identifying who needs the hip and spine examined
and poorly for deciding which of two abnormal joints is responsible.

The distribution of pain is the most useful feature: above the knee only
suggests hip, below the knee suggests spine.

Provocation is the second: pain reproduced by hip rotation rather than by knee
movement settles a great deal, and that is an examination finding the history
should prompt.

Where both joints are abnormal the history alone will not decide, and a
diagnostic injection is often what resolves it.

### Key Learning Points

- The knee shares innervation with the hip, which is why hip disease refers there.
- Hip pain reaches the anterior thigh and knee and not below it; radicular pain often goes below.
- A normal knee examination with convincing knee pain is a positive finding.
- Pain distribution is the most useful historical feature.
- Where both joints are abnormal the history will not decide, and a diagnostic injection may.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the cost of missing referred pain is an operation on the
wrong joint, and that is a recognised and avoidable event.

The patient most at risk is the older patient with radiographic change in both
the hip and the knee, where either could plausibly be blamed and the knee is
the one being complained about.

The history feature that most often resolves it is what the patient cannot do:
difficulty with shoes, socks and car entry points to the hip, while stairs and
walking distance point to either.

A knee arthroplasty in a patient whose pain was coming from the hip is the
clearest example in this chapter of a technically successful operation that
helped nobody.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is a knee-only consultation, which is a structural failure
of the assessment rather than an oversight.

The second is accepting that a previous hip examination was normal, when the
hip was not the reason the patient attended and the examination may have been
cursory. 4.1.5 covers the inherited normal.

The third is in the post-arthroplasty patient with persistent pain, where
referral from the spine or hip is a recognised cause of a painful but
well-functioning replacement. 4.20.8 covers the assessment.

And a patient with knee pain and a known spinal problem may have both, so
attributing everything to the spine is the same error in reverse.

### Key Learning Points

- Missing referred pain means operating on the wrong joint, which is recognised and avoidable.
- The older patient with change in both hip and knee is the one at risk.
- Difficulty with shoes, socks and car entry points to the hip.
- A previous normal hip examination may have been cursory, because the hip was not the complaint.
- Referral from the spine or hip is a recognised cause of a painful well-functioning replacement.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the patient with an unexplained painful knee after
surgery needs referred causes revisited, because they were frequently excluded
before the operation and not since.

A spinal cause can develop after a knee operation and will be attributed to
the operation by everybody including the patient.

Complex regional pain and neuropathic pain after knee surgery are distinct
from referred pain and are also distinct from a mechanical cause, and the
history is where all three are separated. 4.23.5 covers revision patients.

Infrapatellar branch neuroma after arthroscopy or reconstruction is a
specific, recognisable and frequently missed cause of anterior knee pain, and
the history is localised numbness or hypersensitivity in a defined area rather
than joint pain.

The honest limit is that the history can raise all of these and cannot confirm
any, and no figure here supports which features discriminate, under decision
023.

### Key Learning Points

- After surgery, referred causes need revisiting, because they were excluded before and not since.
- A spinal cause arising later will be attributed to the operation by everyone.
- Complex regional pain, neuropathic pain and referred pain are three different things.
- Infrapatellar branch neuroma presents as localised numbness or hypersensitivity, not joint pain.
