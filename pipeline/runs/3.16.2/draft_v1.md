# Robotic Testing Systems

Six degrees of freedom under computer control. The method that made sectioning
studies repeatable, and that still tests a knee with no muscle in it.

## FRCS Level

### The Principle

A knee moves in six degrees of freedom: three translations and three
rotations.

A testing machine that constrains any of them measures a knee that is not free
to behave normally.

A robot that controls all six can apply a load and let the knee find its own
position, which is what a joint does in life.

### At the Knee

A six degree of freedom robot holds the femur and moves the tibia, recording
force and position at every point.

In force control it applies a load and records where the knee goes. In
position control it repeats a recorded path and records the forces required.

The second mode allows the principle of superposition: record a path intact,
cut a structure, repeat the identical path, and the force difference is that
structure's contribution.

That removes the main weakness of sequential sectioning, because the
comparison is the same motion rather than a new one. 3.16.1 covers the
problem.

Most contemporary statements about which structure contributes what proportion
of a restraint come from this method. 3.11.5 covers one example.

### What Changes It

Whether force control or position control was used, which changes what the
result means.

The load applied, since a 134 newton anterior drawer and a simulated pivot
shift are different questions.

Specimen mounting, which can introduce constraint the robot then works
against.

Whether muscle loads were simulated, which most protocols omit.

The number of specimens, which remains small.

### Clinical Relevance

Read a percentage contribution figure as the output of a superposition
experiment under a stated load.

Expect the same structure to contribute different proportions under different
loads and at different flexion angles.

Treat robotic results as the best available attribution of restraint and not
as a measure of clinical laxity.

Quote the load and the flexion angle with any contribution figure.

### Key Learning Points

- A knee moves in six degrees of freedom and a constrained machine measures an abnormal knee.
- Force control finds where the knee goes; position control finds what force a path needs.
- Superposition compares the identical motion before and after a cut.
- That removes the main weakness of sequential sectioning.
- Contribution proportions depend on the load and the flexion angle.

## Fellowship Level

### At the Knee

Superposition assumes the knee behaves the same way on the repeated path,
which requires that cutting changed the structure and not the specimen's
response to handling.

Soft tissue relaxes with repeated cycling, so preconditioning protocols exist
and differ between laboratories.

Robotic systems have excellent precision and unknown accuracy relative to a
living knee, which is the same distinction as everywhere in this chapter.

Simulated muscle loads can be added through cables, and doing so changes
contribution figures substantially for structures that act with muscle. 3.11.1
covers the iliotibial band.

The simulated pivot shift is the clearest success of the method, converting a
clinical sign into a repeatable mechanical input. 3.11.4 covers the test.

### What Changes It

Preconditioning protocol, which affects the baseline every later measurement
is compared with.

Whether muscle loads were simulated, and through how many cables.

The definition of the simulated clinical test, since laboratories simulate the
pivot shift differently.

Coordinate system definition, which determines what counts as rotation and
translation.

Specimen temperature and hydration during long protocols.

### How It Is Measured

Serial robots with six axis load cells, which are the standard configuration.

Optical tracking to define bony coordinate systems, since the robot knows its
own position and not the bone's.

Reported repeatability of a fraction of a millimetre and a fraction of a
degree, which is the machine's figure.

Comparison against cadaveric tensile testing for structural properties, which
is a different question. 3.16.1 covers it.

No study has validated robotic kinematics against the same knees in vivo,
because the same knees cannot be both.

### Clinical Relevance

Ask how the simulated clinical test was defined, since laboratories differ and
the definition drives the result.

Expect muscle simulation to change the contribution of muscle associated
structures materially.

Distinguish the method's precision from its correspondence with life.

Compare robotic findings within a laboratory's protocol rather than across
laboratories.

### Key Learning Points

- Superposition assumes the repeated path behaves the same, which preconditioning addresses.
- Precision is excellent and accuracy relative to a living knee is unknown.
- Simulated muscle loads change contributions for muscle associated structures.
- The simulated pivot shift converted a clinical sign into a repeatable input.
- No study has validated robotic kinematics against the same knees in vivo.

## Consultant Perspective

### At the Knee

Robotic testing is where the chapter's central trade is clearest: it secured
internal validity almost completely and secured no external validity at all.

The decision it forces is how to use a method that answers its own question
definitively and cannot answer whether that was the right question.

On one side, superposition is as close to a controlled experiment as knee
mechanics gets, and reconstructions designed from it have improved rotational
control. 3.11.8 covers one.

On the other, every result is from an unloaded specimen on a defined path, and
the clinical failures that matter happen in loaded, fatigued, unanticipated
conditions. 3.14.6 covers those.

What moves the weight is whether the clinical question is about mechanism or
about outcome. The method is decisive on the first and silent on the second.

### What Changes It

Whether the question asked is mechanistic or clinical.

Whether muscle was simulated, which is the single largest step towards life.

Whether a clinical study has tested the same hypothesis, in which case this is
the explanation rather than the evidence.

The laboratory's protocol, since cross laboratory comparison is weak.

The size of the claimed effect against the specimen variation reported.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic superposition for restraint contribution, which is the reference.

In vivo dynamic imaging for real kinematics, which is available in few centres
and lacks the ability to cut. 3.16.7 covers it.

Clinical trials for outcome, which answer a question the robot cannot ask.

Finite element models calibrated against robotic data, which inherit its
loading assumptions. 3.16.4 covers them.

The external validity of robotic testing has never been measured, and it is
not obvious what study design could measure it.

### Clinical Relevance

Use robotic findings to explain mechanism and clinical studies to establish
outcome, and keep the two roles separate in discussion.

Treat a construct designed from robotic data as mechanically rational and
clinically unproven until a trial says otherwise.

State that the method's external validity has never been measured.

Expect cross laboratory figures to disagree, and compare within a protocol.

Weigh the patient's age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand,
psychological state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations when a
construct is being chosen on mechanical grounds, because mechanical
rationality is not by itself an indication.

### Key Learning Points

- Robotic testing secured internal validity almost completely and external validity not at all.
- It is decisive on mechanism and silent on outcome.
- A construct designed from robotic data is mechanically rational and clinically unproven.
- Its external validity has never been measured and may not be measurable.
- Mechanical rationality is not by itself an indication.
