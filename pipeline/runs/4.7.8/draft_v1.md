# Extension Lag

The gap between what the joint allows and what the patient achieves, and the
quadriceps problem it almost always is.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

An extension lag is an active extension deficit with a full passive extension,
so the joint can reach the position and the patient cannot get it there.

That makes it a quadriceps finding rather than a joint one, and it is the clearest
example in the chapter of why active and passive ranges are recorded separately.
4.7.1 covers active motion.

The causes are quadriceps weakness, reflex inhibition from an effusion or pain,
and a discontinuous extensor mechanism, and the three are separated by the rest
of the examination. 4.6.6 covers the tendons.

### How It Is Performed

Lay the patient supine with both legs straight and nothing under the knees.
4.3.5 covers the supine assessment.

Ask them to straighten the knee fully and measure the angle achieved.

Straighten it passively and measure that angle.

Record both figures, and the lag is the difference between them. 4.1.8 covers
the record.

Lift the leg by the heel and ask the patient to hold it straight, which tests
the extensor mechanism against gravity and is the test that identifies a
rupture. 4.6.6 covers it.

Assess for an effusion and for quadriceps bulk, since both explain the
commonest versions of this finding. 4.6.3 covers the effusion assessment.

### What a Positive Means

A lag with a wasted quadriceps indicates weakness, and rehabilitation is the
treatment. 4.3.9 covers wasting.

A lag with an effusion indicates reflex inhibition, which resolves as the
effusion settles and is not weakness. 4.8.2 covers the mechanism.

A lag with an inability to hold the leg straight against gravity and a palpable
gap indicates a ruptured extensor mechanism, and that is urgent. 4.6.6 covers
it.

A lag after a reconstruction indicates incomplete rehabilitation in most cases,
and it is the finding most predictive of a dissatisfied patient. 4.7.10 covers
post-operative range.

A lag in a knee with anterior pain may be driven by patellofemoral inhibition,
and the sitting examination is where that is seen. 4.3.4 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- An extension lag is an active deficit with a full passive range.
- It is a quadriceps finding rather than a joint one.
- Weakness, reflex inhibition and a discontinuous extensor mechanism are the three causes.
- A lag with an effusion is inhibition and resolves.
- A lag with an inability to hold the leg straight and a palpable gap is urgent.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the lag is the finding that demonstrates why both ranges are measured,
and defining it correctly is the mark.

The expected points are both measurements, the difference, the straight leg hold
against gravity, the effusion, and the quadriceps bulk.

Say that you would test the straight leg hold in every patient with a lag, since
that is what separates the urgent cause from the others. 4.6.6 covers the
tendons.

Say that an effused knee has a lag that is not weakness, since treating it as
weakness delays the treatment of the effusion. 4.8.2 covers it.

Say that you would record the lag as a figure rather than as present, because
the figure is what the next comparison needs. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the quadriceps works at its greatest mechanical
disadvantage in terminal extension, so a deficit in its output appears there
first. 2.4.3 covers patellar biomechanics.

Reflex inhibition reduces quadriceps activation without any change in the
muscle, which is why an effusion produces a lag that resolves rather than one
that needs strengthening. 4.8.2 covers the mechanism.

A discontinuous extensor mechanism cannot transmit force at all, which is why
the straight leg hold fails completely rather than partially. 4.6.6 covers the
tendons.

Patellofemoral pain inhibits the quadriceps in the arc where the patella is
loaded, which is why anterior knee pain and an extension lag travel together.
4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

### How Good It Is

The lag is a reliable and reproducible finding when both ranges are measured,
and invisible when only one is.

It is the single most useful indicator of quadriceps function available in a
clinic without equipment, which is why it earns its own page.

It does not distinguish its causes, which the effusion assessment, the
quadriceps bulk and the straight leg hold do between them. 4.6.3 covers the
first.

It is the finding most predictive of dissatisfaction after knee surgery, which
makes following it worthwhile. 4.7.10 covers post-operative range.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The quadriceps works at its greatest mechanical disadvantage in terminal extension.
- Reflex inhibition reduces activation without changing the muscle.
- A discontinuous extensor mechanism fails the straight leg hold completely.
- Patellofemoral pain inhibits the quadriceps in the arc where the patella is loaded.
- The lag is invisible if only one range is measured.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the lag decides timing, identifies one urgent diagnosis and
predicts outcome.

The urgent diagnosis is an extensor mechanism rupture, identified by the
straight leg hold, and it has an operative answer with a time limit. 4.6.6
covers the tendons.

The timing decision is that a knee with a significant lag is not ready for a
ligament reconstruction, because reconstructing it produces a stiff result and a
worse recovery. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

The outcome prediction is that an unresolved lag after surgery is the finding
most associated with a dissatisfied patient, which makes it worth chasing rather
than observing. 4.7.10 covers it.

In the cruciate deficient knee the quadriceps is the compensating mechanism, so
a lag means the compensation is unavailable and the patient will complain.
4.10.3 covers the chronic knee.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not measuring both ranges, which makes the lag invisible.

The second is not performing the straight leg hold in a patient with a lag.

The third is treating an effusion related lag as weakness, which delays
addressing the effusion.

The fourth is accepting a lag as inevitable after surgery, when it is the finding
most worth treating.

The fifth is reconstructing a ligament in a knee with a significant lag.

### Key Learning Points

- The urgent diagnosis is an extensor mechanism rupture, found by the straight leg hold.
- A knee with a significant lag is not ready for a ligament reconstruction.
- An unresolved post-operative lag is the finding most associated with dissatisfaction.
- In a cruciate deficient knee a lag means the compensation is unavailable.
- Not measuring both ranges makes the lag invisible.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the lag explains a large proportion of the patients who
have a technically satisfactory knee and are unhappy with it.

A patient with a competent reconstruction, a full passive range and a persisting
lag has a quadriceps problem that no further surgery addresses, and the
conversation is a rehabilitation one. 4.3.9 covers wasting.

A patient whose lag has not responded to a proper programme needs inhibition
sought: an effusion, patellofemoral pain or a neurological cause, all of which
are treatable and none of which is weakness. 4.8.2 covers the effusion.

A patient with a lag after an extensor mechanism repair may be at their best
achievable, and framing that is more useful than pursuing a figure. 4.6.6
covers the tendons.

A patient with a lag and a chronically deficient posterior cruciate has lost the
compensating mechanism, and that combination is the one most likely to generate
a reconstruction that was avoidable. 4.11.6 covers that knee.

In medicolegal work a recorded lag is objective evidence of reduced quadriceps
function, and its absence from a record is the commonest reason a functional
claim cannot be assessed. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A persisting lag with a competent graft is a quadriceps problem no surgery addresses.
- A lag that will not respond to a programme needs inhibition sought.
- After an extensor mechanism repair a lag may be the best achievable.
- A lag with posterior cruciate deficiency removes the compensating mechanism.
- A recorded lag is objective evidence of reduced quadriceps function.
