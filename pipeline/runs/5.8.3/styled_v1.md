# Complete Tears

The most conspicuous diagnosis in the chapter, and the report's real content is
what else is torn.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A complete tear interrupts the ligament, which is obvious against a structure
that is normally uniform and continuous. 5.8.1 covers the normal posterior
cruciate.

It occurs by a force driving the tibia backwards, or by hyperflexion, and the
marrow records which. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

It matters because an isolated complete tear of this ligament is uncommon, so the
finding is a reason to search the rest of the knee rather than a diagnosis that
stands alone. 4.11.7 covers the combined ligament injury assessment.

### How It Is Acquired

Confirm the discontinuity on more than one plane, since a tear seen on one
sagittal image can be a partial volume effect. 2.8.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

Locate the tear along the ligament, because the site bears on whether it can
heal. 2.8.1 covers the gross anatomy.

Check the tibial attachment for a bony avulsion, which is a different injury with
a different treatment. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Search the posterolateral corner deliberately, since that is the combination that
changes the operation. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

Search the posteromedial structures for the same reason from the other side.
5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

### What Normal Looks Like

A continuous ligament, which excludes this diagnosis. 5.8.1 covers the normal
posterior cruciate.

Meniscofemoral ligaments intact beside it and not to be counted as residual
fibres. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Normal marrow at both attachments and at the anterior tibia. 5.13.1 covers bone
marrow oedema.

An intact corner, which is a positive finding worth stating. 5.10.4 covers the
popliteofibular ligament.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Complete interruption of the ligament with retracted ends. 5.5.9 covers the
ligaments.

A bony fragment avulsed from the tibial attachment with the ligament attached.
5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Marrow contusion at the anterior tibia, which records a direct blow. 5.13.1
covers bone marrow oedema.

Injury to the corner, the collaterals or the other cruciate, which is the
clinically decisive part of the study. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

Posterior tibial position in the scanner, which is a secondary sign and depends
on how the knee lay. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

### Key Learning Points

- Discontinuity is conspicuous against a normally uniform ligament.
- The marrow records the mechanism.
- An isolated complete tear is uncommon.
- The finding is a reason to search the rest of the knee.
- A bony avulsion is a different injury with a different treatment.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer confirms the tear, then goes looking elsewhere.

The expected points are the multiplanar confirmation, the tear's site, the bony
avulsion, the corner, and the collaterals.

Say that an isolated injury to this ligament is uncommon. 4.11.7 covers the
combined ligament injury assessment.

Say that the corner is the combination that changes the operation. 5.10.3 covers
PLC injuries.

Say that an avulsion is reattached rather than reconstructed. 5.3.14 covers
avulsion injuries.

### How Good It Is

This is the most reliable diagnosis in the chapter, which makes the quality of
the rest of the report the thing that varies.

Its best contribution is the discontinuity itself, which is unambiguous and
rarely in dispute. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Its second contribution is the associated injury, which the same study can show
and which decides the operation. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

It is limited because the degree of resulting instability is not shown, so a
complete tear on a scan does not establish how lax the knee is. 3.8.2 covers the
posterior stability.

It is limited again because the corner is hard to see and is omitted more often
than it is reported negative, which leaves the decisive question unanswered.
5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no grade,
translation distance, fragment size, rotation value or healing interval appears
either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and
this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The discontinuity is unambiguous and rarely disputed.
- The associated injury decides the operation.
- The degree of instability is not shown.
- The corner is omitted more often than it is reported negative.
- An omission leaves the decisive question unanswered.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this study's job is to describe a pattern, because a posterior
cruciate injury is managed as part of one.

A complete tear with an intact corner and little laxity can be managed without
reconstruction, which is a different path from the combined injury. 4.11.6 covers
chronic PCL deficiency.

A complete tear with a corner injury is a reconstruction decision involving more
than one structure. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

A bony avulsion is reattached, so recognising it changes the operation rather
than the prognosis. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

An examination under anaesthesia establishes the laxity that decides the extent
of surgery, and the scan establishes which structures are available to
reconstruct. 4.14.6 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the cruciate and stopping. 5.10.4 covers the
popliteofibular ligament.

A partial volume effect on one sagittal image is reported as a tear. 2.8.5 covers
the MRI anatomy.

A bony avulsion is reported as a complete tear, which sends the patient down the
wrong path. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

The meniscofemoral ligaments are counted as intact fibres, so a complete tear is
reported as partial. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Posterior tibial position in a flexed knee is reported as evidence of the tear's
severity. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

### Key Learning Points

- The cruciate is reported and the search stops.
- A partial volume effect is reported as a tear.
- A bony avulsion is reported as a complete tear.
- The meniscofemoral ligaments are counted as intact fibres.
- Tibial position in a flexed knee is read as severity.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the complete posterior cruciate tear is the chapter's example
of a correct report that is not a useful one.

The diagnosis is almost always right and almost never sufficient, because the
operation is decided by the pattern and the pattern is the part most often
incomplete. 4.11.7 covers the combined ligament injury assessment.

The corner is the structure that decides, it is the hardest structure in the
posterior knee to see, and a report that does not mention it leaves the surgeon
unable to tell whether it was looked at. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

A negative statement about the corner is therefore worth as much as a positive
one, which is the position the lateral chapter reaches from the other direction.
5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

The useful report confirms the tear, locates it, states the attachment, and names
every other structure it assessed with its state. 5.20.1 covers structured
reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The diagnosis is almost always right and almost never sufficient.
- The operation is decided by the pattern.
- The pattern is the part most often incomplete.
- A negative statement about the corner is worth as much as a positive one.
- The report names every structure it assessed with its state.
