# AL Bundle Mechanics

The dominant bundle. Larger, tight in flexion, and the one a single graft is
placed to replace.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

When a structure has two parts of unequal size, the larger one usually carries
more load, because strength scales with cross sectional area.

If the larger part is also tight at the angle where the loads are biggest, the
asymmetry is reinforced rather than balanced.

That combination makes one part functionally dominant, and dominance is what
makes a partial replacement viable.

### At the Knee

The anterolateral bundle is named for its femoral attachment, which sits
anteriorly and laterally within the footprint on the medial femoral condyle
wall. 2.8.2 covers the anatomy.

Conventional teaching gives it roughly twice the cross sectional area of the
posteromedial bundle.

It tightens with flexion and reaches maximal tension in mid to deep flexion,
which is where posterior loads are largest. 3.8.3 covers the bundles.

It is therefore the restraint a posterior drawer at 90 degrees is testing.
3.8.6 covers the drawer.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which it responds to directly.

Rotation, which changes its length as well.

Partial injury, where this bundle's loss produces the larger laxity.

Graft position, since this is the bundle a single graft replaces.

### Clinical Relevance

Most of the measurable laxity after a posterior cruciate injury comes from
this bundle, which is why testing at 90 degrees is informative. Section 4
covers the tests.

A single bundle reconstruction targets it, which is a mechanical decision
rather than a simplification. 3.8.8 covers the construct.

A graft replacing this bundle is tensioned in flexion, because that is when
the bundle works.

Knowing which bundle is dominant makes the reconstruction's design make sense.

### Key Learning Points

- Strength scales with cross sectional area.
- The anterolateral bundle is named for its femoral attachment.
- Conventional teaching gives it roughly twice the area of the posteromedial bundle.
- It reaches maximal tension in mid to deep flexion.
- It is the restraint a posterior drawer at 90 degrees tests.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Dominance here is both structural and functional: a larger area and a tension
peak at the working angle. Either alone would make it important and together
they make it the ligament for practical purposes.

Its length change through the range is large, which makes its behaviour
sensitive to attachment position. 3.7.4 covers the comparable point
anteriorly.

That sensitivity is why femoral tunnel position dominates the reconstruction's
result.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching places maximal anterolateral bundle tension beyond
roughly 70 degrees of flexion, rising through the range from a slack position
near extension.

In situ force measurement shows it carrying the large majority of the
posterior restraint at 90 degrees, with the posteromedial bundle contributing
little there.

Its femoral footprint is large and its tibial attachment broad, so a
cylindrical tunnel spans part of a non circular footprint. 2.8.6 covers the
surgical anatomy.

The meniscofemoral ligaments lie adjacent and share loading, which has to be
accounted for in any apportionment. 2.6.3 covers them.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, as the dominant variable.

Femoral tunnel position within the footprint, in a reconstruction.

Rotation and combined loading.

Whether the meniscofemoral ligaments are intact.

### How It Is Measured

Selective sectioning with in situ force measurement gives its share at each
angle.

Cadaveric length change measurement maps its tension pattern.

Footprint mapping measures the attachment areas, which guides tunnel
placement.

Figures vary between studies because the meniscofemoral contribution is
handled differently.

### Clinical Relevance

Place a single bundle graft in the anterolateral footprint, since that is the
dominant restraint.

Tension in flexion rather than extension for this ligament.

Account for footprint shape when choosing tunnel diameter and position.

Read apportionment figures with the meniscofemoral handling in mind.

### Key Learning Points

- Dominance is structural and functional: larger area and a tension peak at the working angle.
- Conventional teaching places maximal tension beyond roughly 70 degrees.
- The posteromedial bundle contributes little at 90 degrees.
- A cylindrical tunnel spans part of a non circular footprint.
- The meniscofemoral contribution explains variation between studies.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat this bundle's footprint as the target and the tensioning angle as the
decision that goes with it. A graft in the right place tensioned at the wrong
angle behaves like a graft in the wrong place.

Conventional teaching tensions a posterior cruciate graft in flexion, commonly
cited around 70 to 90 degrees, with an anterior drawer applied to reduce the
sag first. 3.8.8 covers the construct.

Reducing the sag before fixation is the step that distinguishes this
reconstruction. Fixing a graft with the tibia sagged locks the deformity in.
3.8.6 covers the drawer.

### What Changes It

Femoral tunnel position within the footprint.

Tensioning angle and whether the sag was reduced before fixation.

Graft diameter relative to the footprint.

Corner integrity, which decides whether the graft works alone.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares tunnel positions and tensioning angles for restored
translation.

Postoperative stress radiography measures the clinical result reproducibly.

Computed tomography locates the tunnel against footprint maps.

No measurement confirms the graft is tensioned as intended once the knee is
closed.

### Clinical Relevance

Reduce the sag before fixing the graft, which is the step that matters most.

Tension in flexion at the angle the dominant bundle works.

Place the tunnel in the anterolateral footprint and confirm it on imaging.

Measure the result with stress radiography.

### Key Learning Points

- A graft in the right place tensioned at the wrong angle behaves like one in the wrong place.
- Conventional teaching tensions in flexion, commonly around 70 to 90 degrees.
- Reducing the sag before fixation distinguishes this reconstruction.
- Fixing with the tibia sagged locks the deformity in.
- No measurement confirms the tension once the knee is closed.
