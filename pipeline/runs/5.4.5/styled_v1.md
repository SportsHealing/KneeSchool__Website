# Quadriceps Tendon

A layered tendon, which is why a partial tear here is a statement about which
layer.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The quadriceps tendon is formed from several layers contributed by the different
quadriceps components, inserting on the patella. 2.4.5 covers the patellar
surgical relevance.

Ultrasound resolves those layers, which no routine magnetic resonance sequence
does as clearly. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

It matters because a partial tear is a tear of one or more layers, and naming the
layer is the useful part of the report. 5.4.6 covers the patellar tendon.

### How It Is Acquired

Scan in long axis with the knee slightly flexed, which tensions the tendon. 5.4.1
covers the anterior knee.

Identify the layers, which appear as parallel bright bands separated by thin dark
lines. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

Keep the beam perpendicular, because the layers are parallel fibred and
anisotropic. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

Scan to the insertion and the suprapatellar region, since the insertion is where
rupture occurs. 5.4.20 covers tendon procedures.

Examine dynamically by asking for a contraction, which demonstrates continuity
functionally. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

### What Normal Looks Like

Parallel bright layers with thin dark interfaces between them. 5.2.8 covers
reflection.

A uniform thickness along the tendon's length.

A clean insertion on the patellar superior pole. 2.4.1 covers the patellar
anatomy.

No colour Doppler signal in the tendon substance. 5.2.11 covers Doppler imaging.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

An anechoic cleft in one layer with the others intact, which is a partial tear.
5.5.10 covers the tendons.

Complete discontinuity with a gap and retraction, which is a rupture. 5.3.12
covers patellar fractures.

Focal thickening with loss of the layered pattern, which is tendinopathy. 5.4.6
covers the patellar tendon.

Calcification with acoustic shadowing within the tendon. 5.14.4 covers crystal
arthropathy.

### Key Learning Points

- The tendon is formed of several layers from the quadriceps components.
- Ultrasound resolves the layers more clearly than routine magnetic resonance.
- A partial tear is a tear of one or more layers.
- Naming the layer is the useful part of the report.
- A contraction demonstrates continuity functionally.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the tendon to name when asked what ultrasound adds over
magnetic resonance imaging.

The expected points are the slight flexion, the layers, the perpendicular beam,
the insertion, and the dynamic contraction.

Say that the layer involved is what the report should name. 5.5.10 covers the
tendons.

Say that a dynamic contraction tests continuity. 5.4.15 covers dynamic
instability.

Say that anisotropy is the commonest reason a normal layer looks torn. 5.2.10
covers anisotropy.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that each layer comes from a different muscle, so a
tear of one layer leaves the others transmitting force and the patient still
extending. 2.4.5 covers the patellar surgical relevance.

That is why a partial tear can present with a functional knee, and why extension
power does not exclude one. 4.7.8 covers the extension lag.

A complete rupture leaves no layer intact, so the clinical deficit is immediate
and the ultrasound confirms rather than discovers it. 4.7.1 covers active motion.

The layered structure is also why anisotropy is so troublesome here: an oblique
beam darkens one layer and imitates exactly the finding being looked for. 5.2.10
covers anisotropy.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound is the best modality for the quadriceps tendon's internal structure
and for demonstrating its continuity dynamically.

Its best contribution is the layer by layer partial tear assessment, which no
other routine study gives. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

It is limited by anisotropy imitating the finding, and by the difficulty of
distinguishing a high grade partial tear from a complete one. 5.2.10 covers
anisotropy.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no tendon
thickness values appear either: those are published quantities and this
environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- Each layer comes from a different muscle.
- A tear of one layer leaves the others transmitting force.
- Extension power does not exclude a partial tear.
- A complete rupture is a clinical diagnosis the ultrasound confirms.
- An oblique beam imitates exactly the finding being sought.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the question is whether the extensor mechanism needs repairing,
and the ultrasound contributes the structural half.

A complete rupture is an operative decision made clinically, and the imaging
adds the level and the retraction. 4.7.8 covers the extension lag.

A high grade partial tear in a functioning knee is the genuinely difficult case,
and the layer assessment is what informs it. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

A dynamic examination showing the tendon failing to transmit a contraction is
functional evidence no static study gives. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

Tendinopathy without a tear is a loading question rather than a surgical one, and
the boundary belongs to Section 6. 5.4.20 covers tendon procedures.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting anisotropy as a partial tear. 5.2.10 covers
anisotropy.

A slack tendon is reported as thickened. 5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

A high grade partial tear is reported as complete, or the reverse. 5.5.10 covers
the tendons.

The layer involved is not named, which removes the report's usefulness. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

Operative indication is taken from the ultrasound rather than the examination.
4.7.1 covers active motion.

### Key Learning Points

- Anisotropy is reported as a partial tear.
- A slack tendon is reported as thickened.
- Partial and complete tears are confused in both directions.
- The layer involved goes unnamed.
- The indication is taken from the imaging rather than the examination.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the quadriceps tendon is where ultrasound's advantage is
clearest and its reporting weakest.

The layer level description is genuinely superior to a routine magnetic resonance
study and almost never appears in a report, so the advantage is realised only
when the surgeon scans. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Distinguishing a high grade partial tear from a complete one changes the
operation and is the hardest call ultrasound makes here, with no established
criterion. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

The dynamic contraction test is the most informative manoeuvre available and is
not recorded in any transferable form. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

Published accuracy for these tears reflects the operators in the study, which is
the general ultrasound caveat and bites hardest where the call is close. 5.21.1
covers the meniscal imaging literature.

The useful practice is to name the layers, state whether a contraction
transmitted, and say who scanned. 5.2.6 covers sound waves.

### Key Learning Points

- The layer level description rarely appears in a report.
- Distinguishing high grade partial from complete has no established criterion.
- The dynamic contraction test is not recorded transferably.
- Published accuracy reflects the operators in the study.
- Name the layers, state whether a contraction transmitted, and say who scanned.
