# Kinematics During Stair Climbing

Going up and coming down are different tasks. Descent is the harder one for
the knee, and it is the one patients report first.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Stairs ask the limb to raise or lower the whole body over a fixed step height,
which walking on the level does not.

Ascent is concentric work: the muscles shorten to lift the body. Descent is
eccentric: the muscles lengthen under load to lower it.

Eccentric control is the harder task and it is where a weak or painful knee
fails first, which is why patients describe stairs down before stairs up.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives peak knee flexion on stairs as roughly 80 to 90
degrees, considerably more than walking on the level.

On ascent the knee flexes to clear the step, then extends under load as the
body rises over the planted foot.

On descent the knee is loaded in flexion while the body lowers, and it reaches
its greatest flexion under load at the moment the trailing foot leaves the
step.

Both are closed chain tasks on the supporting limb, so the hip and ankle share
the work. 3.3.3 covers the chain.

### What Changes It

Step height, which sets the flexion required. A deeper step asks for more.

Speed and whether a handrail is used, both of which change the share the knee
carries.

Quadriceps function, which is the eccentric brake on descent.

Pain, which reduces the flexion the patient will accept under load.

### Clinical Relevance

Ask about stairs separately in each direction, because the two directions test
different things.

Difficulty descending points at eccentric quadriceps control and at loaded
flexion. Difficulty ascending points at strength through range.

Patellofemoral symptoms commonly appear on stairs before they appear on the
level, because the flexion and the load are both greater.

Stairs are a practical functional measure, since they need more range and more
control than level walking.

### Key Learning Points

- Ascent is concentric work and descent eccentric.
- Descent is the harder task and the one patients report first.
- Conventional teaching gives peak flexion on stairs as roughly 80 to 90 degrees.
- Step height sets the flexion required.
- Patellofemoral symptoms appear on stairs before they appear on the level.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Separate the two directions in the description as well as in the history.
Ascent and descent share a range and differ in the type of muscle work and in
the timing of peak load.

Peak knee extensor moment on stairs is greater than on the level, and it
occurs at a greater flexion angle, which multiplies the joint loading. 3.4.5
covers moments.

The combination of a large flexion angle and a large extensor moment is what
makes stairs the discriminating everyday task for the patellofemoral joint.

### At the Knee

Peak flexion of roughly 80 to 90 degrees places the knee in the part of the
range where rotation and translation are greatest. 3.3.8 covers the zones.

Patellofemoral joint reaction force rises with both the quadriceps force and
the flexion angle, and stairs raise both at once. 3.12 covers the joint.

Contact on the tibiofemoral surfaces is further posterior than in level
walking, so the loaded area differs as well as the magnitude.

Descent loads the knee in flexion with the limb decelerating, which is the
closest everyday task to a controlled landing. 3.3.13 covers landing.

### What Changes It

Step geometry, which varies between buildings and is a real source of
variation in reported figures.

Handrail use, which reduces the limb's share substantially and is frequently
unrecorded.

Quadriceps strength and endurance, which determine eccentric control on
descent.

Patellar height and trochlear morphology, which set how the rising
patellofemoral load is distributed.

### How It Is Measured

Instrumented staircases with force plates in the steps are the laboratory
method, and they fix a step geometry that may not match the patient's.

Optical capture on a standard stair rig measures the kinematics, over a few
steps.

Timed stair tests measure performance rather than kinematics and are what is
usually available clinically.

Handrail use and step height are the two variables most often unreported,
which limits comparison between studies.

### Clinical Relevance

Record the direction, the step height and whether a handrail was used when
documenting stair ability.

Expect patellofemoral pathology to present on stairs, given the simultaneous
rise in angle and quadriceps force.

Test eccentric quadriceps control when descent is the problem.

Treat a timed stair test as a performance measure rather than a kinematic one.

### Key Learning Points

- Ascent and descent share a range and differ in muscle work and load timing.
- Peak extensor moment is greater than on the level and occurs at a greater flexion angle.
- Stairs raise quadriceps force and flexion angle at once, which loads the patellofemoral joint.
- Descent is the closest everyday task to a controlled landing.
- Handrail use and step height are the two most often unreported variables.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat stair function as a discriminating outcome measure, since it needs more
range and more eccentric control than the tasks most patients recover first.

The anterior knee pain that follows patellofemoral overload presents on
stairs, which is a loading argument and is why stair questions appear in most
outcome scores.

Mid to deep flexion loading on stairs is the condition that exposes a mid
flexion instability or an unfavourable contact path. 3.3.8 covers mid flexion.

### What Changes It

Patellar height and trochlear geometry, native or reconstructed, which set the
patellofemoral load distribution.

Component position and joint line in replacement, which change the extensor
lever arm through the loaded range.

Tibial slope and alignment, which change the loading the posterior contact
sees.

Quadriceps capacity, which is modifiable and is frequently the limiting factor
rather than the mechanics.

### How It Is Measured

Outcome scores include stair items and measure the patient's account rather
than the mechanics.

Instrumented stair analysis quantifies moments and kinematics in a laboratory
and in a fixed step geometry.

Fluoroscopic step up studies give bone level kinematics for a single step,
which is the method behind several arthroplasty kinematic comparisons.

No clinical measurement separates quadriceps capacity from mechanical
restriction on stairs, which is where examination decides.

### Clinical Relevance

Use stair function rather than level walking when judging whether a knee has
recovered.

Investigate anterior knee pain on stairs as a patellofemoral loading problem
first.

Consider mid flexion instability in a replaced knee whose only complaint is
stairs.

Exclude quadriceps weakness before attributing stair difficulty to the
mechanics.

### Key Learning Points

- Stair function discriminates because it needs range and eccentric control.
- Anterior knee pain from patellofemoral overload presents on stairs.
- Stairs expose mid flexion instability and an unfavourable contact path.
- Fluoroscopic step up studies underlie several arthroplasty kinematic comparisons.
- No clinical measurement separates quadriceps capacity from mechanical restriction here.
