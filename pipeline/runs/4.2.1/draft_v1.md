# Pain

The commonest presenting complaint in the knee, and the least specific. What
asking about pain can establish and what it cannot.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Pain is why most patients attend and it is the symptom that narrows the
differential least, so the history has to work it rather than record it.

What asking about pain establishes is a pattern: where, when it started, what
provokes it, what settles it, and whether it wakes the patient.

Each of those is a separate question with its own page in this chapter, and
the pattern they build is more informative than any single answer. 4.2.8
begins with location.

### How It Is Performed

Ask an open question first and let the patient describe the pain in their own
words before offering any options.

Then work through the pattern deliberately: site, onset, character, radiation,
aggravating and relieving factors, and the effect on sleep and on function.

Ask what the patient can no longer do, because that is the answer that will
define whether treatment worked.

Avoid offering a list of descriptors to choose from. A patient given the word
sharp will frequently adopt it.

### What a Positive Means

Pain in a knee means the knee or something referring to it is irritated, and
little more on its own.

The useful conclusions come from combinations. Anterior pain on stairs and on
rising from a chair points to the patellofemoral joint. 4.15.9 covers it.

Medial pain with a twisting mechanism and mechanical symptoms points to the
meniscus. 4.9.1 covers the presentation.

Diffuse pain with stiffness after rest and limited range points to
degenerative disease. 4.18.1 covers early disease.

### Key Learning Points

- Pain is the commonest knee complaint and the least discriminating on its own.
- The pattern of pain is informative; a single feature rarely is.
- Let the patient describe it before offering any descriptor, because a suggested word gets adopted.
- Ask what the patient can no longer do, since that defines whether treatment worked.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

At viva level the expectation is that pain is characterised rather than noted,
and that the characterisation drives the rest of the assessment.

A patient's description of severity is a report of their experience rather
than a measure of pathology, and the two diverge in both directions.

Pain disproportionate to the findings is a finding in itself, and it points
towards sensitisation, towards a cause outside the knee, or towards distress
rather than towards a missed lesion.

Pain that does not fit any pattern on this chapter's pages should prompt a
search above and below the knee before a scan of the knee.

### How Good It Is

Pain as a single symptom performs poorly for localisation and well for one
thing: establishing whether the problem is mechanical or inflammatory.

Pain worse with use and better with rest is mechanical. Pain worse after rest
and better with movement is inflammatory. That distinction is more reliable
than any question about site. 4.2.14 covers the inflammatory pattern.

The site of pain is less reliable than teaching implies, because referred and
radiating pain are common and the knee is a frequent destination for both.
4.2.15 covers it.

Severity performs worst of all as a guide to pathology and best as a guide to
urgency and to what the patient will accept.

### Key Learning Points

- Characterise pain rather than noting it, and let the characterisation drive the assessment.
- Pain disproportionate to the findings is a finding, not a missed lesion.
- The mechanical against inflammatory distinction is the most reliable thing pain tells you.
- Site of pain is less reliable than teaching implies, because referral to the knee is common.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

The decision-relevant question is whether this pain is the pain an operation
would address, and the history answers it better than imaging does.

A patient whose pain pattern matches their scan finding is a candidate. A
patient whose scan finding is present and whose pain pattern does not match it
is usually not, and that mismatch is common in degenerate knees.

Pain that has changed character is the single most useful historical feature
at this level. A degenerate knee whose diffuse ache has become a localised
mechanical pain has acquired something new.

Pain with no mechanical pattern at all, in a knee with structural findings,
predicts a poor result from a mechanical intervention more reliably than any
imaging feature.

### Where It Misleads

Pain misleads in patterned ways and most of them are avoidable.

A patient asked whether the pain is on the inside will often agree, because
the question supplies the answer. The remedy is to ask them to point with one
finger.

Pain adopts the vocabulary of the last explanation given. A patient told they
have arthritis describes arthritic pain, which is why the history should
precede the notes and the images. 4.1.3 covers the contamination.

Bilateral pain is under-asked. A patient attends about one knee and the other
is often symptomatic, which changes both the differential and the plan.

And pain that is genuinely worse than the knee explains should raise the hip,
the spine, a referred source, or a systemic cause rather than a bigger
operation.

### Key Learning Points

- The question is whether this pain is the pain an operation would address, and the history answers it better than imaging.
- A scan finding that does not match the pain pattern usually is not the cause.
- Pain that has changed character means the knee has acquired something new.
- Ask the patient to point with one finger rather than offering a site.
- Bilateral symptoms are under-asked and change the plan.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice pain has usually been described many times before and the
account has hardened, which makes it less useful rather than more.

The most valuable question in that setting is what has changed since the last
assessment, because a stable pain pattern carries little new information and a
changed one carries a great deal.

Pain after a reconstruction or a replacement needs a differential of its own
rather than the one used before the operation. 4.20.1 and 4.20.8 cover the two
commonest.

Pain in the absence of any mechanical pattern after surgery is the
presentation most likely to be treated by further surgery and least likely to
benefit from it.

patterns discriminate is supported by a figure here. The patterns are standard

### Key Learning Points

- In referred practice the account has hardened, so ask what has changed rather than what it is like.
- Pain after a reconstruction or a replacement needs its own differential.
- Pain with no mechanical pattern after surgery is most likely to be operated on and least likely to benefit.
- No figure in this chapter supports which patterns discriminate; the patterns are standard teaching.
