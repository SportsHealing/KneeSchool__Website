# Personal Statements and Interviews

How to write about an interest in orthopaedics without it sounding like a
hobby, and what medical school interviews are actually assessing.

## For Young Learners

### What This Is

A personal statement is a short piece of writing submitted with your
application. An interview is what happens if the statement and the rest of the
application get you that far.

Both are assessments of reasoning rather than of enthusiasm. That distinction
is the single most useful thing to understand about them.

By the interview stage, every candidate in the room has the grades. Enthusiasm
is universal and therefore worth nothing as a signal.

What separates candidates is whether they can think about a problem out loud,
change their mind when given new information, and talk about a difficult
situation honestly. Those are the things being scored.

### How It Works

A statement has limited space, and the space is better spent on a few things
in depth than on everything you have done.

Interviews are usually one of two kinds. A panel interview is a longer
conversation with two or three interviewers. A multiple mini interview is a
circuit of short stations, each with one task or one question, scored
separately.

Stations commonly cover a reasoning problem, an ethical scenario, a role play
with an actor, and a question about your own experience. Which format a school
uses is published on its admissions pages.

### How to Prepare

An interest in orthopaedics is useful in a statement if you can say something
specific with it, and dead weight if you cannot.

Weak version. I am fascinated by orthopaedic surgery and hope to become a knee
surgeon.

Stronger version. Reading about anterior cruciate ligament injury in young
athletes made me want to know why girls tear the ligament more often than
boys, and the answer turned out to involve anatomy, hormones and training load
rather than any one thing.

The second version shows you followed a question and found it more complicated
than you expected. That is what the reader is looking for, and it works with
any topic.

For interviews, practise saying your reasoning out loud to someone who will
interrupt you. Reading answers silently builds the wrong skill.

### Common Mistakes

Claiming a lifelong ambition. It is unverifiable and every reader has seen it.

Naming a specialty as a decision rather than as an interest. You have not seen
enough to decide, and saying so is a stronger position than pretending
otherwise.

Listing achievements with no reflection attached.

Rehearsing answers word for word. Interviewers can tell, and a memorised
answer collapses as soon as the follow up question changes direction.

Saying what you think they want to hear in an ethical scenario. There is
usually no right answer and the marks are in the reasoning.

### Where to Find Out More

UCAS publishes guidance on writing a personal statement, including the current
format and length, which has changed.

Each medical school publishes which interview format it uses and often what
the stations assess.

The Medical Schools Council publishes guidance on selection, including the
values and attributes schools are assessing against.

### Key Learning Points

- Statements and interviews assess reasoning, not enthusiasm, because every candidate has enthusiasm.
- A specific question you followed is worth more than a declared ambition.
- Multiple mini interviews and panel interviews are scored differently and schools publish which they use.
- Ethical scenarios usually have no right answer; the marks are in the reasoning.
- Practise out loud with interruptions rather than silently.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.
