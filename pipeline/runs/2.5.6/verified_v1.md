# Surgical Anatomy

The medial compartment as seen through an arthroscope: what is reachable, what
is not, and what limits access.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The medial meniscus is reached arthroscopically through two portals in front
of the knee, one either side of the patellar tendon, with the camera usually
in one and the instrument in the other.

The anterolateral portal is made first and is the usual viewing portal. The
anteromedial portal is made under direct vision, which is why it can be placed
to suit the lesion.

From the front, the anterior horn and body are seen directly. The posterior
horn is seen by looking across the joint from the opposite portal, and the far
posterior part of the horn is the hardest thing in the compartment to see.

### Relations

In front of the joint the portals pass close to the patellar tendon medially
and laterally, the fat pad behind it, and the infrapatellar branch of the
saphenous nerve on the medial side.

The medial compartment is bounded above by the femoral condyle, below by the
plateau, and medially by the capsule with the collateral ligament outside it.

Outside the posteromedial capsule, and therefore immediately behind a needle
or suture passed from inside, lie the saphenous nerve and the long saphenous
vein.

### Function

Access to the medial compartment is limited by how far the joint can be
opened. The medial side is the tight one, because the collateral ligament is
short and the meniscus is tethered.

Flexion angle and valgus stress change what is reachable. The posterior horn
is best seen in slight flexion with valgus; the anterior horn needs more
extension.

### Clinical Relevance

A tight medial compartment is the commonest reason a medial meniscal procedure
is difficult, and it is anatomical rather than a matter of skill.

The saphenous nerve behind the posteromedial capsule is the structure at risk
in posterior horn repair, and it is why an accessory posteromedial portal is
made with care.

Operative technique belongs to 7.8 onwards. This page is what is where.

### Key Learning Points

- Two anterior portals either side of the patellar tendon give access; the anteromedial one is made under vision.
- The far posterior part of the posterior horn is the hardest structure in the compartment to see.
- The saphenous nerve and vein lie immediately outside the posteromedial capsule.
- The medial compartment is tight because the collateral is short and the meniscus tethered.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Portal anatomy. The anterolateral portal sits lateral to the patellar tendon,
above the joint line and below the patella. The anteromedial portal sits
medial to the tendon at a level chosen for the lesion, made with a needle
first so its trajectory is checked before the skin is cut.

An accessory posteromedial portal is made for posterior horn work, behind the
medial collateral ligament and above the joint line, with the saphenous
structures the reason it is localised with a needle under vision.

The diagnostic sequence through the medial compartment runs anterior horn,
body, posterior horn, then the posteromedial recess, and the posterior horn is
seen last because it needs the most manipulation.

### Relations

Instrument trajectory matters more than portal position. A portal too far from
the tendon reaches the compartment at a shallow angle; too close and the
tendon limits movement.

The posteromedial capsule separates the joint from the saphenous nerve and
vein. Any needle, suture passer or anchor directed posteromedially from inside
the joint is aimed at them.

The medial collateral ligament is outside the capsule and limits how far the
compartment opens. Pie crusting it is a described manoeuvre and is a decision
to trade stability for access, owned by Section 7.

The fat pad obstructs the view anteriorly and is often partly resected, with
the consequences noted on 2.4.5.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The structures at risk are sensory rather than vascular on this side. The
infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the anteromedial portal
site and the saphenous nerve itself lies posteromedially.

The vascular structure that matters is the perimeniscal plexus, which a repair
suture passes through deliberately. Preserving the capsular tissue preserves
the plexus.

The popliteal vessels are a relation of the posterior compartment rather than
the medial one, but a suture passed from the medial compartment in a
posterolateral direction can reach them.

### Function

Visualisation and access are separate problems. The posterior horn can often
be seen and not reached from the same portal, which is why instruments and
camera are swapped.

The compartment opens with valgus stress and with flexion, and the two are
adjusted together. A knee that will not open is a knee in which a repair
cannot be placed accurately.

Accurate suture placement depends on reaching the tear perpendicular to its
plane, so portal position is chosen from the tear rather than from habit.

### Clinical Relevance

Localise an accessory posteromedial portal with a needle under vision every
time. The saphenous structures are the reason and there is no substitute for
seeing where the needle enters.

In a tight medial compartment, forcing instruments risks iatrogenic cartilage
damage, which is the commonest avoidable complication of medial meniscal
arthroscopy.

Repair sutures should pass through the capsular tissue carrying the
perimeniscal plexus, because that tissue is the healing source.

Portal placement should follow the imaging. A posterior horn tear and an
anterior horn tear are reached from different anteromedial positions.

### Key Learning Points

- Make the anteromedial portal with a needle first, so the trajectory is checked before the skin is cut.
- Any instrument directed posteromedially from inside the joint is aimed at the saphenous nerve and vein.
- Visualisation and access are separate problems, which is why camera and instruments are swapped.
- Iatrogenic cartilage damage from forcing a tight compartment is the commonest avoidable complication.
- Portal position is chosen from the tear, because sutures need to reach it perpendicular to its plane.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The anatomical constraint that cannot be worked around is the medial
collateral ligament's length. Everything else about access is adjustable; that
is not, except by releasing it.

In a stiff or previously operated knee, capsular scarring reduces the
compartment further and the posteromedial recess may be obliterated, so the
usual route to the posterior horn may not exist.

### Function

Access determines what operation is possible, which means it determines the
operation. A repairable tear that cannot be reached accurately will be
resected, and that outcome is decided by anatomy and planning rather than by
intent.

Pie crusting the collateral trades a small amount of medial stability for
access. Whether that trade is right depends on what the access buys, and
Section 7 owns it.

### Clinical Relevance

Plan portals and the need for an accessory portal before starting, from the
imaging. Converting mid procedure is harder than planning for it.

Where the compartment will not open enough for accurate repair, the honest
options are to release, to accept resection, or to stop and refer. Forcing is
not one of them.

In revision meniscal surgery assume the posteromedial recess is scarred and
plan a route accordingly.

No measurement of portal position appears on this page. Published portal
descriptions are given in distances from the tendon and the joint line, and
those are values this page cannot verify.

### Key Learning Points

- The medial collateral's length is the one access constraint that cannot be adjusted except by releasing it.
- Access determines what operation is possible, so it determines the operation.
- In a stiff or revised knee the posteromedial recess may be obliterated.
- Where accurate repair is impossible the options are release, resect, or stop; forcing is not one.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

Three situations where medial compartment access is the operation rather than
a step in it.

The tight compartment with a repairable posterior horn tear in a young
patient. The decision is whether to release the medial collateral to gain
access. Releasing it is a deliberate, small, reversible injury that buys the
ability to save a meniscus; not releasing it usually means resecting the
meniscus. Stated that way the trade is clearer than it looks at the time.

The ramp lesion at the meniscocapsular junction. It sits in the posteromedial
recess, it is missed from the standard anterior view, and finding it needs
either a transnotch view or a posteromedial portal. The anatomy is the whole
problem: the lesion is in the one place the routine examination does not look.

The previously operated posteromedial corner. Scar replaces the recess, the
saphenous structures may be tethered to the capsule, and needle localisation
becomes more important rather than less.

In all three the compartment itself is unchanged from the medical student
page. What changes is that the access is the limiting factor rather than the
pathology.

### Key Learning Points

- Releasing the medial collateral for access is a small reversible injury that can save a meniscus.
- The ramp lesion sits in the posteromedial recess and is missed from the standard anterior view.
- Finding it needs a transnotch view or a posteromedial portal.
- In a revised posteromedial corner the saphenous structures may be tethered to the capsule.
