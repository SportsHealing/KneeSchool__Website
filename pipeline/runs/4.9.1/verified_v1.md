# Clinical Presentation

What a meniscal tear actually looks like in clinic, and why the two common
presentations need separating before any test is performed.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The clinical presentation of a meniscal tear is what the whole of this chapter
is trying to detect, so it is worth stating before the tests.

There are two presentations and they behave differently. A traumatic tear in a
younger knee, and a degenerate tear in an older one.

The traumatic tear follows a twisting injury on a loaded knee, produces pain
on the affected joint line, swells over hours to days, and may catch or lock.
4.2.18 covers the mechanism.

The degenerate tear often has no injury at all, or a trivial one, and presents
as activity-related joint line pain in a knee with other degenerative
findings. 4.9.11 covers it.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history before the examination, and take the mechanism, the swelling
timing and the mechanical symptoms specifically. 4.2.3 covers swelling.

Then inspect for an effusion, wasting and the position of rest.

Palpate the joint lines with the knee flexed to about ninety degrees, which
opens the line and makes it accessible. 4.9.2 covers the technique.

Assess range and look for a loss of extension, which is the finding that
changes the urgency. 4.7.8 covers extension lag.

Then perform the provocative tests, which the rest of this chapter covers, and
perform them last because they hurt.

### What a Positive Means

Joint line tenderness on the symptomatic side, with a matching mechanism and
mechanical symptoms, is the core presentation.

A loss of extension with a hard endpoint is a displaced tear and a mechanical
block. 4.9.8 covers it.

An effusion supports an intra-articular problem and does not identify the
meniscus. 4.8.2 covers significance.

Posterior joint line pain in deep flexion, with a history of a squatting
injury, raises a posterior horn or root tear. 4.9.9 covers roots.

No joint line tenderness at all makes a meniscal tear less likely but does not
exclude one, particularly a root tear.

### Key Learning Points

- There are two presentations: a traumatic tear in a younger knee and a degenerate one in an older knee.
- They behave differently and the distinction is made from the history.
- Palpate the joint lines with the knee flexed, which opens the line.
- A loss of extension with a hard endpoint is a displaced tear and changes the urgency.
- Perform the provocative tests last, because they hurt and a sore knee guards.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the meniscal examination is expected as a sequence that ends with
the special tests rather than beginning with them.

Inspect, palpate both joint lines, assess range including a deliberate check
for full extension, assess the effusion, then test.

Say which tests you are doing and what a positive would mean, and say that no
single test is sufficient.

Finish by examining the ligaments, because a meniscal injury and a cruciate
injury share a mechanism and the combination is common. 4.10.2 covers acute
cruciate injury.

### What a Positive Means

A positive finding on a meniscal test means the manoeuvre reproduced pain or a
click at the joint line. It does not mean a tear is present.

The combination of findings is what carries weight: a matching history, joint
line tenderness and at least one positive provocative test.

A single positive test in the absence of joint line tenderness is weak.

Joint line tenderness in the absence of any other finding is also weak,
because it is common in degenerate knees without a symptomatic tear.

A mechanical block is the exception: it stands alone as a finding and it is
the one that determines urgency. 4.9.8 covers it.

### How Good It Is

The meniscal tests share a pattern and it is worth learning as a pattern
rather than test by test.

They are more specific than sensitive. A positive result is informative and a
negative result excludes very little, which is the opposite of what a
screening test should do.

Joint line tenderness is the most sensitive single finding and the least
specific. The provocative tests are the reverse.

The combination outperforms any single test, which is why the sequence exists.

No figures appear anywhere in this chapter, under decision 023, because the
ranking is standard teaching and the percentages written from memory would not
be.

### Key Learning Points

- A positive test means the manoeuvre reproduced a symptom, not that a tear is present.
- The meniscal tests are more specific than sensitive.
- A negative result on a specific, insensitive test excludes very little.
- Joint line tenderness is the most sensitive finding and the least specific.
- The combination outperforms any single test, which is why the sequence exists.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the examination's job is not to diagnose a tear, since
imaging does that better. It is to establish whether the tear is the problem.

That question matters because degenerate tears are common in asymptomatic
knees, so a tear on a scan is not an indication. 4.1.6 covers the era of
imaging.

The examination contributes three things imaging cannot: whether the joint
line that is tender matches the tear, whether there is a mechanical block, and
whether the patient's range and function are limited by it.

In a young knee after a defined injury the examination and the imaging usually
agree and the question is repairability rather than attribution.

In an older knee they frequently disagree and the examination is the
tie-breaker rather than the confirmation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating a positive provocative test as an indication,
when the whole group is specific rather than sensitive and a positive in a
degenerate knee is often incidental.

The second is treating a negative examination as excluding a tear,
particularly a root tear, which examines poorly. 4.9.9 covers roots.

The third is examining a knee too acutely. A swollen guarding knee gives a
meniscal examination of little value, and repeating it at two to six weeks is
more useful than persisting. 4.1.5 covers the timing problem.

And attributing all joint line tenderness to the meniscus, when the
collateral, the pes anserinus and the posteromedial corner are all nearby.
4.16.7 covers one.

### Key Learning Points

- The examination's job is to establish whether the tear is the problem, not that it exists.
- A tear on a scan is not an indication, because degenerate tears are common without symptoms.
- The examination supplies the match between the tender line and the tear, which imaging cannot.
- A negative examination does not exclude a root tear.
- A swollen guarding knee gives a meniscal examination of little value.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the presentation is usually a knee that has been resected
and is no better, or a tear that was repaired and has failed.

The examination then has to distinguish a new or recurrent tear from the
altered mechanics of a deficient meniscus, and the two feel similar to the
patient. 4.9.10 covers deficiency.

Joint line tenderness after a resection is common, persistent and poorly
specific, so it contributes little at this level.

A mechanical block after a repair is the finding that matters most and still
stands alone. 4.20.2 covers meniscal repair assessment.

The honest limit is that the examination after meniscal surgery is
substantially less useful than before it, and the history and the imaging
carry proportionally more.

### Key Learning Points

- After resection the examination has to separate a new tear from altered mechanics.
- Joint line tenderness after resection is common, persistent and poorly specific.
- A mechanical block after a repair still stands alone as a finding.
- The examination is substantially less useful after meniscal surgery than before it.
