# Natural History

What happens to an untreated tear has no single answer, because the knee's fate
depends on what is asked of it.

## For Young Learners

### How It Presents

A torn ACL does not grow back. The knee settles down, the swelling goes, and
walking returns to normal. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

What does not return by itself is the control the ligament gave during turning.
6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Some people get on well without it, especially if they do not play turning
sports. Others find the knee gives way. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Complications and Prognosis

Each time a knee gives way, it can damage the cushions inside the joint. 6.5.2
covers meniscal damage.

Those cushions do not grow back either, and losing them is what matters most for
the knee over a lifetime. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

That is why giving way is taken seriously, even when it does not hurt much at
the time.

### Key Learning Points

- A torn ACL does not grow back.
- Walking usually returns to normal by itself.
- Control during turning does not.
- Giving way can damage the cushions inside the knee.
- Those cushions do not grow back either.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### How It Presents

After the acute injury settles, most people walk comfortably and many run in a
straight line without trouble. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

The difficulty, when it comes, is on turning, pivoting and landing, and it may
only appear when someone returns to those activities. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A knee that has never been asked to pivot since the injury has not yet been
tested, which is why "it feels fine" early on settles little. 6.1.2 covers the
functions of the ACL.

### Management Overview

The choice is not between doing something and doing nothing. It is between
rehabilitation alone and reconstruction followed by the same rehabilitation.
6.5.1 covers instability.

Some people do well with rehabilitation alone, and the best predictor is whether
the knee stays steady during what they actually need to do. 6.1.2 covers the
functions of the ACL.

Delaying a decision is itself reasonable, provided the knee is not giving way in
the meantime. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Complications and Prognosis

Repeated giving way can tear the meniscus and damage the cartilage surface.
6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

That damage, rather than the missing ligament itself, is what carries the long
term risk to the joint. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

An operation reduces episodes of giving way. Whether it prevents the long term
joint changes is a genuinely open question and should not be presented as
settled. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- Walking usually recovers; turning control does not.
- A knee that has not pivoted has not been tested.
- The choice is rehabilitation alone or reconstruction plus rehabilitation.
- Repeated giving way damages the meniscus and the cartilage.
- Whether surgery prevents long term joint change is unsettled.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** If I do not play sport, do I need an operation?
Often not. The ligament's job shows up in turning, so a knee that is never asked
to turn may never miss it. The question is what you need the knee to do, not
what the scan shows.

**Q.** Can I decide later?
Usually yes, as long as the knee is not giving way while you decide. Episodes of
giving way are the thing that causes further damage, so they are the reason to
stop waiting.

**Q.** Will I definitely get arthritis?
No. The risk is raised, particularly where the meniscus has been damaged or
removed, but it is a raised risk rather than a certainty, and the figures vary
widely between studies.

**Q.** Does an operation remove the arthritis risk?
It reduces giving way, which is one of the routes to further damage. Whether
that translates into less arthritis decades later is not established, and anyone
who tells you it is settled is overstating it.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### How It Presents

The acute phase settles over weeks: the haemarthrosis resorbs, range returns and
gait normalises. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

What persists is functional instability under rotational load, and it is
elicited by demand rather than by time. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A proportion of patients, often called copers, maintain stable function without
reconstruction, and identifying them prospectively is not reliably possible.
6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

### Complications and Prognosis

Secondary meniscal tearing is the central event in the natural history, because
it converts a ligamentous problem into a load distribution problem. 6.5.2 covers
meniscal damage.

Medial meniscal posterior horn tears predominate in the chronically deficient
knee, and a root tear changes load distribution across the whole compartment.
6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Chondral damage follows, and the sequence from instability to meniscal loss to
cartilage loss to radiographic change is the chain this chapter is organised
around. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

### Key Learning Points

- The acute phase settles and functional instability persists.
- Instability is elicited by demand rather than by time.
- Copers exist and cannot be identified reliably in advance.
- Secondary meniscal tearing is the central event.
- The chain runs instability, meniscus, cartilage, radiographic change.

## MRCS Level

### Management Overview

In a viva the expected answer refuses a single prognosis and gives the variables
that determine it.

The expected points are the demand placed on the knee, the presence of giving
way, the state of the menisci, the patient's age, and whether a decision can
safely be deferred. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Say that the meniscus is what is being protected, which is the reason
instability is treated rather than tolerated. 6.5.3 covers cartilage
degeneration.

Say that a stable knee in a low demand patient is a legitimate endpoint and not
a failure to operate. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Complications and Prognosis

Delay is safe while the knee is stable and unsafe once it is not, which makes
the history more informative than the interval. 4.10.3 covers chronic ACL
deficiency.

A meniscus torn before reconstruction may be repairable; one torn repeatedly is
less likely to be. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

The osteoarthritis risk attaches most strongly to meniscal loss, which is why
meniscal preservation is the outcome the natural history argues for. 6.5.4
covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- Give the variables rather than a single prognosis.
- The meniscus is what instability treatment protects.
- A stable low demand knee is a legitimate endpoint.
- Delay is safe while the knee is stable.
- Osteoarthritis risk attaches most strongly to meniscal loss.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to argue a position on timing and
to defend it against the alternative.

Early reconstruction protects the meniscus in a patient who will return to
pivoting, and exposes a patient who would have coped to an operation they did
not need. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Delayed reconstruction after a trial of rehabilitation selects those who need
it, and accepts the risk of a further giving way episode in the interval. 6.5.1
covers instability.

The deciding variable is the demand the knee will face, which is a statement
about the patient rather than about the knee. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Complications and Prognosis

A stiff knee after early surgery is the complication the delayed approach
exists to avoid, and it is the reason inflammation is settled first. 6.2.5
covers acute haemarthrosis.

A meniscus lost during the waiting period is the complication the early approach
exists to avoid, and it is irreversible where stiffness is not. 6.5.2 covers
meniscal damage.

Weighing an irreversible loss against a reversible one is the whole of the
timing argument, and it is why the two are not symmetrical. 6.5.3 covers
cartilage degeneration.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether reconstruction alters the long term
development of osteoarthritis at all. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The second is whether copers can be identified before the event rather than
after it, which would make the timing argument unnecessary. 6.1.2 covers the
functions of the ACL.

The third is whether the older recreational patient should be managed from
evidence gathered on young athletes. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- Early surgery protects the meniscus and may be unnecessary.
- Delay selects those who need it and risks the interval.
- The deciding variable is demand, a fact about the patient.
- Stiffness is reversible and a lost meniscus is not.
- Whether reconstruction changes long term joint outcome is open.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the natural history is the argument a fellow has to make to
a patient who wants a single answer, and the honest version has no single answer
in it.

The knee's fate turns on the meniscus, and the meniscus's fate turns on episodes
of giving way, so the prognosis is a statement about future behaviour rather
than about present anatomy. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

That makes the most useful question in clinic not what the scan shows but what
the patient intends to do, and the second most useful whether the knee has given
way since the injury. 4.10.3 covers chronic ACL deficiency.

A patient who has already lost meniscal tissue is in a different natural history
from one who has not, and the two should not be counselled from the same script.
6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The ligament is the diagnosis and the meniscus is the prognosis, which is the
sentence this chapter exists to earn. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- The prognosis is a statement about future behaviour.
- The most useful clinic question is what the patient intends to do.
- The second is whether the knee has given way since the injury.
- A knee that has lost meniscal tissue has a different natural history.
- The ligament is the diagnosis and the meniscus is the prognosis.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that the natural history is the part of this
condition patients most want quantified and the part least safely quantified.

Quoting a figure for arthritis risk implies a precision the evidence does not
carry, and declining to quote anything leaves the patient to assume the worst.
6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The defensible middle is to give the mechanism, name meniscal loss as the hinge,
and say which parts are established and which are not. 6.5.2 covers meniscal
damage.

What is said still turns on the same five things. The patient's age and
physiology, which set healing and how many decades the joint must last. Their
medical comorbidities, because body mass changes joint loading as well as
operative risk. The demands they put on the knee, which decide whether the
natural history is even engaged. Their psychological state, because a patient
who will not return to pivoting has a different natural history from one who
will. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which decide what counts
as an acceptable outcome.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.3 covers the
epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- This is the part patients most want quantified and it is least safe to
  quantify.
- A figure implies precision the evidence does not carry.
- Saying nothing leaves the patient assuming the worst.
- The defensible middle gives the mechanism and names what is unsettled.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations change what should be said.
