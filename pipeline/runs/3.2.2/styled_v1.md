# Flexion and Extension

The knee's largest motion, the ranges that daily life needs, and why the last
ten degrees of extension matter more than the last thirty of flexion.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Flexion and extension is rotation in the sagittal plane, and it is the knee's
dominant motion by an order of magnitude.

It is not rotation about a fixed axis. The axis moves as the knee bends,
because the femoral condyles roll as well as glide on the tibia. 3.3.2 covers
that.

The practical consequence is that a description of knee flexion as hinge
motion is close enough for a drawing and wrong for a measurement.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the range as about 0 to 140 degrees, with 5 to 10
degrees of hyperextension present in many normal knees.

Functional ranges are smaller. Level walking uses about 0 to 60 degrees,
stairs about 80 to 90, sitting about 90, and squatting beyond 130.

Extension is limited by the posterior capsule, the cruciate ligaments and the
shape of the condyles together. Deep flexion is limited by soft tissue contact
between calf and thigh before any ligament is reached.

The last degrees of extension involve a small rotation as well, which 3.3.5
covers as the screw home mechanism.

### What Changes It

Effusion limits flexion before anything else does, because the capsule has a
volume at which it is most comfortable and that volume corresponds to about 30
degrees.

Pain and guarding limit measured range without any structural cause.

Soft tissue bulk limits deep flexion, so the same knee allows different ranges
in different bodies.

Scarring, arthritis and previous surgery limit it structurally, and the
pattern of loss differs between them.

### Clinical Relevance

Losing the last 10 degrees of extension is more disabling than losing the last
30 of flexion. A knee that cannot straighten is loaded by the quadriceps
throughout stance, which is tiring and painful.

Fixed flexion is measured as the angle at which extension stops, and it is
recorded separately from the flexion range for that reason.

Range of motion is recorded as two numbers rather than one, because the same
arc at different positions means different things functionally.

### Key Learning Points

- Conventional teaching gives about 0 to 140 degrees, with 5 to 10 degrees of hyperextension common.
- Level walking uses about 0 to 60 degrees; stairs about 80 to 90.
- Extension is limited by capsule, cruciates and condylar shape; deep flexion by soft tissue contact.
- Effusion limits flexion first, with the capsule most comfortable around 30 degrees.
- Losing the last 10 degrees of extension is more disabling than losing the last 30 of flexion.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Describe the motion as rotation about a migrating axis. The instant centre of
rotation traces a curve through flexion rather than sitting at a point, which
3.3.7 covers.

Rolling without gliding would roll the femur off the back of the tibia within
about 20 degrees. The cruciates convert most of the roll into a glide, which
is the classical four bar linkage account.

That conversion is why cruciate injury changes the path of the joint as well
as its laxity.

### At the Knee

Posterior femoral rollback in the lateral compartment exceeds that in the
medial, which produces the axial rotation that accompanies flexion. 3.3.3
carries it.

The extension limit is a combination: the posterior capsule tightens, the
anterior cruciate tightens, and the condylar geometry brings the surfaces into
their most congruent position.

Conventional teaching puts the tibiofemoral contact point moving posteriorly
by of the order of 15 mm from extension to deep flexion in the lateral
compartment and considerably less medially.

Patellofemoral contact migrates proximally on the patella with flexion, which
3.12.2 covers.

### What Changes It

Effusion volume, pain, soft tissue bulk and structural change, as at student
level, with posterior capsular contracture added as the usual structural cause
of fixed flexion.

Hamstring tightness limits extension with the hip flexed and not with it
extended, which separates a two joint muscle cause from a capsular one.

Implant design changes the flexion arc achievable, because the surfaces that
permit rollback are replaced.

### How It Is Measured

Goniometry is the clinical standard and its reliability is better for flexion
than for small extension deficits, which is where the clinically important
measurement lies.

Fluoroscopic analysis gives contact point migration in vivo and is the source
of the rollback figures.

Recording convention matters: a knee described as having 10 to 120 degrees has
a fixed flexion deformity, and the same arc described as 110 degrees of
movement hides it.

### Clinical Relevance

Record two numbers and name the deficit, because an arc alone conceals fixed
flexion.

Distinguish capsular from muscular limitation by testing extension with the
hip flexed and extended.

Expect effusion to limit flexion early, and reassess range once it has settled
rather than recording a limited range as fixed.

Prioritise restoring extension over gaining the last degrees of flexion in
rehabilitation, since the functional cost is asymmetrical.

### Key Learning Points

- The instant centre migrates; rolling alone would roll the femur off the tibia within about 20 degrees.
- Lateral rollback exceeds medial, which produces the rotation that accompanies flexion.
- Conventional teaching gives lateral contact point migration of the order of 15 mm.
- A knee recorded as 10 to 120 degrees has a fixed flexion deformity; an arc alone hides it.
- Extension is restored before the last degrees of flexion, because the cost is asymmetrical.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the flexion arc as the product of surface geometry and ligament length,
and note that surgery can alter either independently.

Rollback is asymmetrical between compartments and that asymmetry is the knee's
rotation. An implant that reproduces one without the other reproduces neither.

The extension limit is multifactorial, which is why fixed flexion after
arthroplasty has several candidate causes and the anatomy does not point to
one.

### What Changes It

Tibial slope changes the relationship between flexion and anteroposterior
position, and is now part of both the arthroplasty and the cruciate revision
conversation.

Posterior condylar offset changes the flexion achievable before impingement.

Soft tissue balance decides whether a reconstructed or replaced knee reaches
its geometric range, so the number a design permits and the number a patient
achieves are different quantities.

### How It Is Measured

In vivo fluoroscopy during weight bearing activity is the method that matters
for judging a design, and it is expensive and uncommon.

Passive range under anaesthetic, passive range awake and active range are
three different measurements, and the gap between them carries information.

Published flexion figures for implants are usually passive and usually best
case, which is worth stating when comparing them.

### Clinical Relevance

Measure extension deliberately rather than as part of a sweep, since small
deficits are both the hardest to measure and the most disabling.

Record passive and active range separately when assessing a stiff knee,
because the difference localises the cause.

Consider slope and offset when a knee does not achieve its expected arc after
surgery.

Avoid quoting a design's published flexion as a patient's expected flexion.

### Key Learning Points

- The flexion arc is surface geometry and ligament length, and surgery can alter either.
- Rollback asymmetry is the knee's rotation; reproducing one without the other reproduces neither.
- Tibial slope changes the relationship between flexion and anteroposterior position.
- Passive asleep, passive awake and active are three measurements, and the gaps carry information.
- Published implant flexion is usually passive and best case.
