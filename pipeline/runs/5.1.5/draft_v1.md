# Cost-effectiveness of Imaging

What a scan costs, which is more than its price, and what has to change for it
to be worth it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A scan's cost is its price, the delay it adds, the incidental findings it
produces and the further tests those generate. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Its benefit is only the change in management it produces, which is often nothing
and is occasionally decisive. 5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

The test of whether a scan was worth doing is whether anything was done
differently because of it.

### Where It Misleads

A scan that confirms a decision already made has a low benefit and a real cost,
and it is the commonest scan requested. 5.1.2 covers that comparison.

A scan that changes the operation has a high benefit and is worth almost any
reasonable cost.

A scan requested for reassurance usually fails on its own terms, because an
incidental finding is the opposite of reassuring. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028,
and no cost figure appears either: prices and incidence rates are both the kind
of number this environment cannot check.

### Key Learning Points

- A scan's cost includes delay, incidental findings and the tests they generate.
- Its benefit is only the change in management.
- The test is whether anything was done differently.
- Confirming a decision already made is the commonest and least valuable use.
- A scan for reassurance often produces the opposite.

## MRCS Level

### How Good It Is

In a viva this is the resource question and the expected answer reaches the
change in management quickly.

The expected points are the full cost, the benefit as a change in management,
the incidental finding problem, and what a normal result would do.

Say that the benefit is the management change and not the diagnostic yield.
5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

Say that an incidental finding has its own cost and name it. 5.1.6 covers
imaging pitfalls.

Say that a scan whose normal result changes nothing was not indicated.

### Where It Misleads

The reasoning expected is that a test's value depends on the prior probability
and on whether either result changes what happens next.

In a knee where the examination has localised the problem the prior is high, the
scan confirms, and the value is in the detail rather than the diagnosis. 5.1.2
covers that comparison.

In an unlocalised knee the prior is low, so a positive finding is more likely to
be prevalent than causal and the scan has created a problem. 5.6.2 covers
meniscal signal grading.

Delay has a cost that is easy to ignore because nobody is billed for it, and in
an acute knee it is the dominant cost. 5.1.4 covers imaging pathways.

### Key Learning Points

- A test's value depends on the prior and on whether either result changes anything.
- A high prior makes the scan's value the detail rather than the diagnosis.
- A low prior makes a positive finding more likely prevalent than causal.
- Delay is a real cost that nobody is billed for.
- A scan can create the problem it then investigates.

## FRCS Level

### Where It Misleads

At decision level cost effectiveness is a service argument that gets made badly
in both directions.

Restricting access on cost produces delay, repeat consultations and operations
planned on worse information, and those costs land somewhere else in the system.
5.1.4 covers imaging pathways.

Opening access produces incidental findings at scale and a workload of
reassurance, which also lands somewhere else. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

The useful version of the argument is per presentation rather than per modality,
because the same scan is excellent for one question and wasteful for another.
5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

A cost effectiveness claim needs figures, and this page states none for the
reason the whole section states none.

### Key Learning Points

- Restricting access moves the cost rather than removing it.
- Opening access produces incidental findings at scale.
- The argument is per presentation and not per modality.
- The same scan can be excellent and wasteful for different questions.
- A claim here needs figures this environment cannot supply.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the economics are mostly outside the clinician's control and
the decisions are still theirs to make.

The marginal scan in a tertiary clinic is usually a repeat, and its value depends
entirely on what the first one could not answer. 5.5.1 covers the standard
protocol.

Advanced techniques have a cost structure of their own and a benefit that is
established in research populations rather than in clinics. 5.18.1 covers
quantitative imaging.

An incidental finding transfers cost to the patient as anxiety and to the system
as follow up, and neither appears in the price. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Published cost effectiveness work is bound to a health system and a price list,
so transferring its conclusions across systems is an extrapolation. 5.1.7
covers evidence based imaging.

The decision that saves the most is usually not to request, and it is the hardest
to defend afterwards if anything is later found. 4.23.7 covers the medico legal
setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The marginal scan is usually a repeat, valued by what the first could not answer.
- Advanced techniques carry benefits established in research populations.
- An incidental finding transfers cost to the patient and the system.
- Published work is bound to a health system and a price list.
- Not requesting saves the most and is the hardest to defend afterwards.
