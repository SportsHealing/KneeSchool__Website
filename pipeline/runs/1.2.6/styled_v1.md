# Muscles Around the Knee

Quadriceps in front, hamstrings behind, calf below and hip muscles above, all
controlling a joint none of them crosses alone.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

Four groups of muscles move and protect the knee.

At the front, the quadriceps. Four muscles that join into one tendon and
straighten the knee. This is the group that goes soft and thin surprisingly
fast if you stop using your leg.

At the back, the hamstrings. Three muscles that bend the knee and also help
control the shin.

Below, the calf. The big calf muscle actually starts above the knee, so it has
a small part to play there too.

Above, the hip muscles. They do not cross the knee at all, and they still
matter, because they control where your thigh points when you land and turn.

### Function

Muscles do two things here. They move the joint, and they protect it.

Protection is the part people forget. A strong muscle contracting absorbs
force that would otherwise land on the joint surface. Muscles also act faster
than you can think, steadying the knee when you step on something uneven.

That is why almost every knee rehabilitation programme is mostly strength
work, whatever the original problem was.

### Key Learning Points

- Quadriceps at the front straighten the knee; hamstrings behind bend it.
- The calf muscle starts above the knee and plays a small part there.
- Hip muscles do not cross the knee but control where the thigh points.
- Muscles both move the joint and absorb force that would reach the joint surface.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

The quadriceps group has four parts: rectus femoris, which crosses the hip as
well as the knee, and the three vasti, which arise from the femur itself. They
converge into the quadriceps tendon.

The hamstrings are biceps femoris on the outer side, and semitendinosus and
semimembranosus on the inner side. They cross both the hip and the knee.

Gastrocnemius, the bulky calf muscle, arises from the back of the femur above
the joint. Popliteus is a small muscle behind the knee that helps unlock it at
the start of bending.

The hip abductors and the gluteal muscles do not cross the knee but strongly
influence it.

### Function

The quadriceps straighten the knee and control it during bending, acting as a
brake as you sit down or walk downhill. The hamstrings bend the knee and
resist the shin sliding forwards, sharing work with the ACL.

Muscles also act as shock absorbers. A contracting muscle takes load that
would otherwise pass through the joint surface, and it responds faster than
conscious reaction, stabilising the knee on uneven ground.

### Clinical Relevance

Quadriceps weakness develops remarkably quickly after any knee problem,
sometimes within days, because the joint reflexively inhibits the muscle when
it is swollen or painful. That weakness then reduces protection of the joint,
which is why it is the first target of nearly every rehabilitation programme.

Hip strength matters more than most people expect. Weak hip muscles let the
thigh roll inwards when you land, which loads the kneecap and the inner side
of the joint, so hip work is standard in knee rehabilitation.

Specific muscle and tendon injuries are covered in the conditions section.

### Key Learning Points

- The quadriceps have four parts; only rectus femoris crosses the hip as well.
- The hamstrings bend the knee and share with the ACL in resisting forward shin slide.
- Gastrocnemius arises above the knee and popliteus helps unlock it.
- Quadriceps weakness appears within days of injury because of reflex inhibition.
- Hip strength controls thigh position on landing and is standard in knee rehabilitation.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why did my thigh get thin so quickly?
A swollen or painful knee reflexively switches off the quadriceps. Muscle is
lost fast and regained more slowly, which is why early strengthening matters.

**Q.** Why am I being given hip exercises for a knee problem?
Hip muscles control where your thigh points when you land and turn. Weakness
there loads the knee differently, so strengthening the hip often helps the
knee.

**Q.** Are hamstring or quadriceps exercises better?
Both. They do opposite jobs and the balance between them matters. A programme
should include both plus hip and calf work.

**Q.** How long does it take to rebuild strength?
Noticeable improvement usually takes six to twelve weeks of consistent work.
Full recovery after a significant injury takes considerably longer.

See a physiotherapist or doctor if your thigh muscle is visibly wasted, if
your knee gives way, or if weakness is not improving with exercise.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The quadriceps femoris comprises rectus femoris, arising from the anterior
inferior iliac spine and therefore biarticular, and vastus medialis, lateralis
and intermedius, arising from the femur. The distal oblique fibres of vastus
medialis are directed towards the patella and contribute to medial patellar
stability.

The hamstrings comprise biceps femoris laterally, inserting on the fibular
head, and semitendinosus and semimembranosus medially. Semitendinosus
contributes to the pes anserinus with sartorius and gracilis.

Gastrocnemius arises by two heads from the posterior femoral condyles.
Popliteus arises from the lateral femoral condyle and inserts on the posterior
tibia.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The quadriceps is innervated by the femoral nerve, from roots L2 to L4. The
hamstrings are supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, with the
short head of biceps femoris supplied by the common fibular division.
Gastrocnemius and popliteus are supplied by the tibial nerve.

This segregation is clinically useful: isolated quadriceps weakness points
towards the femoral nerve or the L3 and L4 roots.

### Function

The quadriceps extend the knee and act eccentrically during flexion under
load, controlling descent in sitting and in downhill walking. The hamstrings
flex the knee and extend the hip, and act as agonists to the anterior cruciate
ligament by resisting anterior tibial translation.

Popliteus unlocks the extended knee by internally rotating the tibia on the
femur at the initiation of flexion, and contributes to posterolateral
stability.

Muscle also attenuates load reaching the articular surface and provides reflex
stabilisation at latencies shorter than voluntary reaction.

### Clinical Relevance

Arthrogenic muscle inhibition causes rapid quadriceps weakness in the presence
of effusion or pain, and is reversible but requires deliberate retraining. It
is a principal target of rehabilitation across the whole range of knee
pathology.

Proximal control matters: hip abductor and external rotator weakness permits
femoral adduction and internal rotation in stance, increasing dynamic valgus
and patellofemoral load.

Muscle and tendon injuries themselves are covered in the conditions section.

### Key Learning Points

- Rectus femoris is the only biarticular quadriceps component.
- Quadriceps are femoral nerve supplied; hamstrings are sciatic nerve supplied.
- The hamstrings act as agonists to the ACL by resisting anterior tibial translation.
- Popliteus unlocks the extended knee by internally rotating the tibia.
- Arthrogenic muscle inhibition causes rapid, reversible quadriceps weakness.
