# Snapping Tendons

A tendon that moves abnormally, which is a diagnosis only a moving study can
make.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A snapping tendon moves abruptly over a bony prominence during knee movement,
producing a palpable or audible snap. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

The abnormality is in the movement rather than in the tendon, so a static study
of a normal looking tendon is the expected finding. 5.4.3 covers the lateral
knee.

It matters because this is the clearest case in knee imaging of a diagnosis that
no static modality can make at all. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct
investigation.

### How It Is Acquired

Place the probe over the symptomatic area and ask the patient to reproduce the
movement. 5.2.6 covers sound waves.

Watch the tendon through the range rather than imaging it at one position. 5.4.15
covers dynamic instability.

Ask the patient to say when the snap happens, and correlate it with what the
screen shows. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

Identify the tendon and the prominence it crosses, which names the lesion. 2.10.5
covers the biceps femoris.

Record a video clip where the equipment allows, because a still image cannot show
movement. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### What Normal Looks Like

A tendon gliding smoothly over bone through the range, with no abrupt
displacement.

A normal fibrillar tendon structure at every position. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

No fluid or thickening around the tendon. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

No correlation between the patient's symptom and anything on the screen.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

An abrupt displacement of the tendon over a bony prominence, coinciding with the
patient's symptom. 2.10.5 covers the biceps femoris.

Thickening of the tendon or its sheath at the point of contact. 5.5.12 covers the
bursa.

A bony prominence or an osteochondroma at the contact point. 5.13.7 covers bone
tumours.

An abnormal or accessory tendon slip taking an unusual course. 2.10.5 covers the
biceps femoris.

### Key Learning Points

- The abnormality is in the movement rather than in the tendon.
- A static study of a normal tendon is the expected finding.
- No static modality can make this diagnosis.
- The patient reproduces the movement during the scan.
- The tendon and the prominence it crosses name the lesion.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the diagnosis to name when asked what only ultrasound can do.

The expected points are the patient reproducing the movement, watching through
range, the symptom correlation, naming the structures, and recording a clip.

Say that the symptom and the screen must coincide. 5.4.15 covers dynamic
instability.

Say that a static magnetic resonance study will be normal. 5.1.3 covers choosing
the correct investigation.

Say that a bony prominence may be the cause and is visible. 5.13.7 covers bone
tumours.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the snap is a mechanical event in time, so an
instrument that samples one instant cannot capture it however good its
resolution. 5.2.6 covers sound waves.

Real time imaging samples continuously, which makes the temporal correlation
between the patient's symptom and the tendon's displacement the diagnostic
observation. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

The cause is usually a geometric one, an unusually prominent bone or an
abnormally coursing tendon, so finding the cause follows from finding the event.
2.10.5 covers the biceps femoris.

A snap without a visible displacement points elsewhere, which is a useful
negative because it redirects rather than reassures. 5.1.6 covers imaging
pitfalls.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound is the only modality that makes this diagnosis, and its performance
depends entirely on whether the symptom was reproduced.

Its best contribution is the temporal correlation, which is not available any
other way. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

It is limited by needing the patient to reproduce the symptom on demand, so a
negative study in a patient who could not reproduce it means nothing. 5.1.6
covers imaging pitfalls.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and the
comparison is the claim: this diagnosis is available to ultrasound and to no
static modality, which is a comparison rather than a number.

### Key Learning Points

- The snap is an event in time and one instant cannot capture it.
- Real time imaging makes the temporal correlation the diagnostic observation.
- The cause is usually geometric and follows from finding the event.
- A snap without visible displacement redirects rather than reassures.
- A negative study in a patient who could not reproduce it means nothing.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a confirmed snapping tendon has a specific answer and an
unconfirmed one does not.

A tendon displacing over a prominence, correlated with the symptom, identifies
the structure to address. 5.4.20 covers tendon procedures.

A bony prominence as the cause may be the thing to remove, which is a Section 7
decision informed by this study. 5.13.7 covers bone tumours.

An unreproduced symptom leaves the diagnosis open, and the study should say that
rather than reporting a normal tendon. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The examination and the scan are best done together, because the person scanning
needs to feel the snap as well as see it. 4.3.2 covers gait observation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting a normal tendon when the symptom was not
reproduced. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

A displacement is seen without being correlated with the symptom. 5.4.15 covers
dynamic instability.

A static study is requested for a dynamic question. 5.1.3 covers choosing the
correct investigation.

The finding is recorded as a still image, which cannot show it. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

The bony cause is not sought once the event is seen. 5.13.7 covers bone tumours.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal tendon is reported when the symptom was not reproduced.
- A displacement is seen without symptom correlation.
- A static study is requested for a dynamic question.
- A still image cannot record the finding.
- The bony cause goes unsought.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this is the purest demonstration of the chapter's argument,
that the examination is the study.

The diagnostic observation exists only while the probe is on the knee and the
patient is moving, so the entire diagnosis is held by the operator and
transmitted as a sentence. 5.2.9 covers refraction.

Video capture would make it transmissible and is not standard, which means a
genuinely unique diagnostic capability is recorded in the least reliable form
available. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The condition is uncommon enough that most operators have seen few, so the
examination that makes the diagnosis is also the one with the least accumulated
experience behind it. 5.4.3 covers the lateral knee.

Nothing in the imaging literature establishes performance here, because the
reference standard is the same examination. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging
literature.

The practical answer is for the surgeon to scan with the patient present, which
is the recommendation this chapter keeps arriving at from different directions.
5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

### Key Learning Points

- The observation exists only during the examination.
- Video capture would make it transmissible and is not standard.
- A unique capability is recorded in the least reliable form.
- The condition is uncommon, so experience is thin.
- The reference standard is the same examination, so performance is unestablished.
