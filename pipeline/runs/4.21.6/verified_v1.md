# Landing Error Scoring System

A scored observation of how someone lands, which turns a subjective judgement
into a list.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The landing error scoring system scores a jump landing against a list of
movement faults, so it converts an observation into a count. 4.4.9 covers single
leg landing.

The examination is watching a standardised landing from the front and the side
and recording which faults are present. 3.14.8 covers landing.

It matters because landing mechanics are associated with knee injury and an
unscored observation is unreliable between examiners. 3.7.14 covers injury
mechanisms.

### How It Is Performed

Set up a standardised jump, which is a drop and a forward jump to a marked
landing area.

Position yourself to view the landing from the front and arrange a second view
from the side, since the faults divide between the two planes.

Ask the patient to jump and land naturally, without coaching, because coaching
changes what is being measured. 4.2.32 covers previous physiotherapy.

Score the faults in the coronal plane, which include the knee moving inwards and
the trunk leaning. 3.12.7 covers dynamic valgus.

Score the faults in the sagittal plane, which include landing with a straight
knee and landing flat footed. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Score the overall impression as the list requires, and total the faults.

Repeat enough trials for a representative score rather than scoring one landing.

### What a Positive Means

A low fault count with a soft, flexed, symmetrical landing is the good result.

A high fault count indicates landing mechanics associated with injury at a
population level, which is a risk statement rather than a diagnosis. 3.7.14
covers injury mechanisms.

Knee valgus and a stiff landing together are the pattern that carries most of
the concern. 3.12.7 covers dynamic valgus.

An asymmetrical landing after an injury or a reconstruction indicates incomplete
rehabilitation. 4.20.1 covers the reconstruction assessment.

A good score does not establish readiness for sport, since the landing is
planned and unloaded by comparison with a match. 4.21.8 covers return to play
decision making.

### Key Learning Points

- The system converts an observation into a fault count.
- The faults divide between the coronal and the sagittal plane.
- Coaching changes what is being measured.
- A high count is a population level risk statement, not a diagnosis.
- A good score does not establish readiness for sport.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the answer to how landing mechanics are assessed objectively,
and the expected version names the two planes.

The expected points are the standardised jump, the two viewing planes, the
absence of coaching, the coronal faults, the sagittal faults, the total, and
multiple trials.

Say that you would not coach the landing, and give the reason. 4.2.32 covers
previous physiotherapy.

Say that you would score from two planes rather than one. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Say that the score describes risk at a population level rather than predicting an
individual injury. 3.7.14 covers injury mechanisms.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that landing forces are absorbed by knee and hip
flexion, so a stiff landing transfers more load to the joint and the ligaments.
3.14.8 covers landing.

Knee valgus during landing raises cruciate loading, which is the mechanism the
scoring system is built around. 3.7.12 covers loading during landing.

Trunk position shifts the centre of mass and so changes the knee moment, which is
why the trunk is scored. 3.14.7 covers jumping.

A scored list reduces examiner variability without removing it, which is the
system's purpose and its limit. 4.1.8 covers examination documentation.

### How Good It Is

The system is more reproducible than an unscored observation and less
reproducible than a measured distance.

Its best contribution is making landing mechanics recordable and comparable over
time, which supports rehabilitation. 4.1.8 covers examination documentation.

It is a risk description rather than a diagnostic test, so it does not identify a
structure.

Motion analysis measures landing mechanics properly, so this system is the
clinical approximation. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made. The scoring thresholds are a
literature convention and this repository does not state them.

### Key Learning Points

- Landing forces are absorbed by knee and hip flexion.
- Knee valgus during landing raises cruciate loading.
- Trunk position changes the knee moment.
- A scored list reduces examiner variability without removing it.
- The system is the clinical approximation of motion analysis.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the score supports injury prevention and rehabilitation
planning rather than a return decision.

A high fault count in an uninjured athlete identifies a modifiable risk pattern,
which is the main use. 3.7.14 covers injury mechanisms.

An asymmetrical landing after reconstruction identifies incomplete
rehabilitation when the strength tests have passed. 4.21.2 covers the single leg
hop.

A score that improves with training demonstrates the pattern is modifiable,
which is the basis of prevention programmes. 4.2.34 covers previous
rehabilitation.

The score contributes little to a return decision, because the landing is
planned and the risk is unplanned. 4.21.8 covers return to play decision making.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is scoring one landing, which is unrepresentative. 3.14.8
covers landing.

The landing is coached, which improves the score and changes nothing about the
athlete's habit. 4.2.32 covers previous physiotherapy.

A single plane view is used, so half the fault list is unscored. 3.12.7 covers
dynamic valgus.

A population level risk statement is applied to an individual as a prediction.
3.7.14 covers injury mechanisms.

A low score is read as readiness, which the system does not assess. 4.21.7
covers return to sport testing.

### Key Learning Points

- Scoring one landing is unrepresentative.
- Coaching improves the score without changing the habit.
- A single plane view leaves half the fault list unscored.
- A population risk statement is not an individual prediction.
- A low score does not assess readiness.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the scoring system's problem is that it is reliable about
something whose relationship to injury is a population association.

The faults it counts are associated with injury in groups and do not identify
which individual will be injured, and the clinical use often forgets that.
3.7.14 covers injury mechanisms.

The landing is planned, self initiated and unopposed, while injuries happen
unplanned and often with contact, so the test samples a different event. 3.14.6
covers cutting manoeuvres.

Scoring is observational, so inter examiner agreement depends on training, and a
score from one clinic is not comparable with another's. 4.1.8 covers examination
documentation.

The pattern is modifiable within minutes, which means a score measures the
current performance rather than the habit under fatigue. 4.21.3 covers the
triple hop.

Improving a score has not been shown by anything in this repository to reduce
injury in an individual, and that claim needs the literature rather than this
page. 4.21.8 covers return to play decision making.

### Key Learning Points

- The faults are associated with injury in groups, not in individuals.
- A planned self initiated landing is a different event from an injury.
- Observational scores are not comparable between clinics.
- The pattern is modifiable within minutes, so the score is performance not habit.
- Whether improving a score reduces injury is a literature question.
