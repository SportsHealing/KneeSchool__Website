# Established OA

The worn knee at the stage where the examination is about deformity, range and
function rather than about the diagnosis.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Established osteoarthritis presents as pain at rest and at night, a deformity
that is visible standing, a reduced range, and a walking distance the patient
can quote. 4.2.11 covers aggravating factors.

The examination at this stage is no longer deciding the diagnosis. It is
recording the deformity, the range, the stability and the function, because
those are what the treatment decision uses. 4.18.7 covers that assessment.

It is a systematic examination rather than a set of special tests, and the
findings are usually obvious.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history: the rest pain, the night pain, the walking distance, the
stairs, and what the patient has stopped doing. 4.18.6 covers functional
limitation.

Watch the gait for a thrust, a shortened stance and the use of a stick, since
all three grade the disease better than any single sign. 4.4.2 covers the
antalgic gait.

Assess the alignment standing from the front and from the side, recording
varus, valgus and any fixed flexion. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Measure the range, recording the fixed flexion deficit first and the flexion
second, since the deficit is the one that limits function. 4.5.4 covers the
flexion deformity.

Palpate for joint line tenderness, osteophyte and an effusion, which together
confirm the compartment and the activity. 4.6.4 covers joint line tenderness.

Assess the collateral ligaments, because the worn compartment shortens its own
side and the opposite ligament may have stretched. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress
testing.

Assess the patellofemoral joint, since it is involved in a large proportion of
worn knees and is often not examined. 4.18.5 covers that examination.

### What a Positive Means

A fixed deformity with a reduced range, joint line tenderness on the concave
side and a thrust on walking is the established picture.

A deformity that corrects passively means the bone loss is limited and the soft
tissues are not contracted, and that changes the options. 4.3.10 covers the
deformity.

A fixed flexion deficit indicates posterior capsular contracture and
osteophyte, and it is the finding patients notice most. 4.5.4 covers it.

Opposite side laxity in a worn compartment means the deformity has become
structural rather than positional. 4.12.5 covers the posterior oblique
ligament.

Night pain and rest pain mark a stage where activity modification has stopped
working.

### Key Learning Points

- The examination records deformity, range, stability and function, not the diagnosis.
- Fixed flexion is recorded before flexion, because it limits function more.
- A passively correctable deformity means limited bone loss and uncontracted tissues.
- Opposite side laxity means the deformity has become structural.
- The patellofemoral joint is involved often and examined rarely.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the arthritic knee station and the expected answer is a
systematic record rather than a diagnostic hunt.

The expected points are the gait, the standing alignment in both planes, the
range with the fixed flexion stated separately, the joint lines, the
collaterals, the patellofemoral joint, and the hip.

Say that you would examine the hip before concluding, because the hip and the
knee refer to each other and the hip is the cheaper miss. 4.2.15 covers
referred pain.

Say that you would record the walking distance and the stairs, since the
functional record is what the decision is made against. 4.18.6 covers it.

Say that you would assess the neurovascular status, which is part of the
preoperative record rather than the diagnosis. 4.3.9 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the worn compartment loses height, so the limb
angulates towards it and the opposite ligament lengthens. 3.5.2 covers contact
pressures.

Once the opposite ligament has lengthened, correcting the alignment alone
leaves the knee unbalanced, which is why the laxity is recorded. 4.12.5 covers
the posterior oblique ligament.

Posterior capsular contracture and posterior osteophyte both block extension,
so a fixed flexion deficit has a soft tissue and a bony component. 4.5.4 covers
it.

Rest and night pain reflect subchondral and synovial involvement rather than
load, which is why they do not respond to activity change.

### How Good It Is

The examination at this stage is reliable, because the findings are large and
reproducible.

Its contribution is the record of deformity, range and stability that the
treatment plan is built from, and no imaging substitutes for it. 4.18.7 covers
the assessment.

It is poor at predicting how much of the deformity will correct at operation,
which is a different question. 4.3.10 covers the deformity.

Weightbearing and long leg radiographs quantify the deformity, so the
examination establishes the clinical state and the films establish the
geometry. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The worn compartment loses height and the limb angulates towards it.
- Once the opposite ligament lengthens, correcting alignment alone leaves imbalance.
- Fixed flexion has a soft tissue and a bony component.
- Rest and night pain reflect subchondral involvement rather than load.
- The examination record, not the imaging, is what the plan is built from.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the examination sorts the worn knee into the groups that
different treatments serve, and the sorting is done by deformity,
correctability, range and compartment involvement.

A single worn compartment with a correctable deformity, intact ligaments and a
good range is one group, and the examination is what identifies it. 4.20.7
covers the partial replacement assessment.

A fixed deformity with opposite side laxity, lost range or multiple
compartments is another, and again the examination defines it. 4.20.8 covers
the total replacement assessment.

Patellofemoral involvement alongside compartment disease moves the knee between
those groups, so it is examined deliberately. 4.18.5 covers it.

A worn knee with a flexion range below what the patient needs for their daily
activity is a functional finding, not a structural one, and it is recorded
separately. 4.18.6 covers functional limitation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording the alignment supine, where the deformity
looks smaller than it is under load. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

A deformity that corrects under an examining hand can be read as correctable
when the contracture is simply being stretched, and the distinction is one of
degree. 4.3.10 covers the deformity.

Lateral joint line tenderness in a varus knee is often attributed to the medial
disease and can represent real lateral compartment involvement. 4.6.4 covers
the technique.

Hip disease reproduces the groin and thigh component of the pain closely, and
the knee examination will be abnormal anyway in a worn knee. 4.2.15 covers
referred pain.

Collateral laxity measured in a knee with a fixed flexion deficit is
unreliable, because the position changes what the ligament resists. 4.12.3 covers the assessment in flexion.

### Key Learning Points

- The alignment is recorded standing, because load reveals the real deformity.
- Correctability under an examining hand is a matter of degree, not a binary.
- Opposite joint line tenderness can be real involvement, not referred.
- A worn knee examines abnormally even when the hip is the source.
- Collateral laxity in a fixed flexion knee is unreliable.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the examination is a preoperative record, and its errors
become operative surprises.

The deformity recorded clinically and the deformity measured on a long leg film
can differ, and the film governs the geometry while the examination governs the
soft tissues. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

A varus knee with a correctable deformity and medial laxity is an unusual
combination that deserves rechecking, since it changes the balance the
operation has to achieve. 4.12.5 covers the posterior oblique ligament.

A fixed flexion deficit of the same size can come mostly from posterior
osteophyte or mostly from capsule, and the clinical end feel is the only clue
before operation. 4.7.5 covers the end feel.

Rotational deformity and torsional malalignment sit outside the varus and
valgus description and are not captured by it. 4.19.7 covers torsional
assessment.

The examination cannot tell how much of the pain is coming from the
patellofemoral joint when both joints are worn, and that uncertainty persists
into the decision. 4.18.5 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- The clinical and the radiographic deformity differ, and each governs a different question.
- Varus with medial laxity is an unusual combination and deserves rechecking.
- A fixed flexion deficit can be mostly bony or mostly capsular, and the end feel is the clue.
- Rotational deformity is not captured by a varus and valgus description.
- The share of pain from the patellofemoral joint is not available from the examination.
