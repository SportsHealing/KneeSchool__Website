# Blood Supply

One artery, a generous synovial sheath, and a set of nerve endings. Between
them they explain why this ligament behaves differently from the anterior
cruciate.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The middle genicular artery is the main supply. It arises from the popliteal
artery behind the knee, pierces the posterior capsule and enters the
intercondylar notch from behind to reach both cruciate ligaments.

Because it enters from behind, the posterior cruciate meets it first. The
anterior cruciate is supplied by the same vessel at the end of a longer route.

A second part of the supply runs in the synovial sheath that covers the
ligament. These periligamentous vessels run along its length and reach its
surface rather than its core.

At the tibial attachment, periosteal vessels from the back of the tibia add to
the supply. Branches of the inferior genicular arteries reach the same region
through the capsule.

Nerve supply comes from the posterior articular branches of the tibial nerve,
which travel with the vessels into the notch.

### Relations

The artery crosses the posterior capsule at about the level of the tibial
attachment, so it lies behind the part of the ligament a surgeon reaches last.

The synovial sheath separates the ligament from the joint fluid, and it is the
sheath that carries the surface vessels.

The nerve endings sit mostly near the two attachments rather than in the mid
substance.

### Function

The blood supply maintains the ligament's collagen and allows a partial injury
to repair.

The nerve endings report joint position and load. Free nerve endings carry
pain, and the encapsulated endings contribute to position sense.

A ligament that reports its own tension is part of a reflex loop as well as a
mechanical restraint, and 3.8.1 covers what that loop does.

### Clinical Relevance

The better supply, and the sheath that holds it, are why a partially torn
posterior cruciate often heals in continuity. The anterior cruciate, with a
longer route to the same artery, usually does not.

Healing in continuity is not the same as healing to length. A ligament can
look intact and still be lax, which is why imaging and examination can
disagree.

The middle genicular artery lies in the path of anything that passes through
the posterior capsule near the tibial attachment, and 2.8.6 covers that.

Loss of the nerve supply after injury contributes to the altered position
sense patients describe, which is separate from the laxity.

### Key Learning Points

- The middle genicular artery is the main supply and reaches the notch from behind.
- The posterior cruciate meets that vessel before the anterior cruciate does.
- Periligamentous vessels in the synovial sheath supply the surface along its whole length.
- Nerve supply is from the posterior articular branches of the tibial nerve.
- The better supply is why a partial tear can heal in continuity, though often lengthened.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe the supply in two parts: an intrinsic supply entering at the
attachments and through the mid substance from the artery, and an extrinsic
supply running in the sheath.

The femoral attachment is the least well supplied region, and within it the
anterior part of the footprint is furthest from the vessel's entry point.

Conventional teaching describes the ligament as better vascularised than the
anterior cruciate at every level, with the difference greatest in the mid
substance.

### Relations

The middle genicular artery arises from the popliteal artery between the two
heads of gastrocnemius and runs forwards through the capsule. It is short,
which is why it tethers rather than loops.

The vein accompanies it. Both lie in fat behind the capsule, and the fat plane
is what a surgeon works in rather than the vessels themselves.

The posterior articular nerve branches enter alongside, so the structures at
risk behind the tibial attachment are vascular and neural together.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Mechanoreceptors include Ruffini endings, which report position, Golgi type
endings, which report tension, and Pacinian corpuscles, which report rapid
change. Free nerve endings carry pain.

Conventional teaching puts the receptor population at roughly 1 per cent of
the ligament's volume, concentrated within a few millimetres of each
attachment.

The receptor population is smaller than the anterior cruciate's, so the
proprioceptive loss after injury is correspondingly less prominent.

The periligamentous vessels in the sheath are what a repair relies on, because
they survive an injury that disrupts the intrinsic supply.

### Function

Vascular supply sets the healing capacity, and it is the reason a partial
posterior cruciate injury is managed differently from a partial anterior
cruciate one.

The receptor population contributes to the hamstring and quadriceps reflex
response to load, which 3.8.1 covers.

Nerve supply also explains the pain pattern: a ligament injury hurts most at
its ends, where the free nerve endings are.

### Clinical Relevance

Expect a knee with a partial posterior cruciate injury and an intact sheath to
recover some restraint. The anatomy supports a trial of non operative
management, and 6.9 covers it.

Treat healing in continuity and restored length as separate questions. Imaging
answers the first and examination answers the second.

Protect the fat plane behind the capsule when working near the tibial
attachment. The artery, the vein and the nerve branches are in it together.

Avoid attributing altered position sense to laxity alone. The receptor loss is
real and it is a separate mechanism.

### Key Learning Points

- The supply is intrinsic from the artery and extrinsic through the synovial sheath.
- The anterior part of the femoral footprint is the least well supplied region.
- Receptors are Ruffini, Golgi type and Pacinian, with free nerve endings for pain.
- Conventional teaching puts receptors at roughly 1 per cent of ligament volume, near the attachments.
- The sheath vessels are what a partial repair relies on.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The structures behind the tibial attachment are the artery, the vein and the
tibial nerve branches, lying in a fat plane behind the capsule. They are a
group rather than a single hazard.

The middle genicular artery is short and tethered, so it does not move out of
the way when the capsule is displaced. That is what makes it different from
the larger vessels it arises from.

The sheath is continuous with the synovium of the notch, so a procedure that
strips the sheath removes the extrinsic supply along with it.

### Function

Treat the vascular anatomy as the determinant of healing capacity. A ligament
with an intact sheath and a disrupted mid substance has a route to repair; one
stripped of its sheath does not.

The receptor population is smaller than the anterior cruciate's, so
proprioceptive arguments carry less weight here than they do in Section 7's
anterior cruciate chapters.

Healing in continuity at a greater length restores the ligament's appearance
and not its function, and the anatomy predicts that outcome rather than
explaining it after the event.

### Clinical Relevance

Preserve the synovial sheath wherever the plan allows. It carries the supply a
partial injury depends on.

Consider a trial of non operative treatment where the sheath is intact and the
injury is partial, and read the anatomy as the reason rather than as a
preference. 6.9 carries the management.

Assess the fat plane behind the capsule before any posterior work. In a knee
with capsular injury that plane is the thing that has been lost.

Document position sense separately from laxity. The two have different
anatomical causes and they recover on different timescales.

### Key Learning Points

- The sheath carries the supply a partial injury depends on, and stripping it removes that route.
- The middle genicular artery is short and tethered, so it does not move with the capsule.
- Receptor population is smaller than the anterior cruciate's, so proprioceptive arguments carry less weight.
- Healing in continuity at greater length restores appearance, not function.
- The fat plane behind the capsule is what a capsular injury takes away.
