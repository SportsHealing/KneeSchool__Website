# Patellofemoral Pain

Mechanical contributors to anterior knee pain, and why the mechanics explain
the pattern of the pain better than they explain its presence.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A loaded joint can hurt without anything being broken, in the way a hand can
ache from gripping too long.

Load that is too high, held too long or applied in the wrong place can all
produce pain.

So the question is rarely whether the joint is damaged. It is usually what the
joint is being asked to do.

### At the Knee

Pain at the front of the knee is commonest in the activities that load this
joint most: stairs, squatting, kneeling and sitting for long periods. 3.12.3
covers the pressures.

The cartilage on the back of the patella has no nerve supply, so the pain
comes from elsewhere.

The bone under the cartilage, the synovium and the fat pad all have nerve
endings and all respond to load.

That is why a scan can look normal in a painful knee, and worn in a
comfortable one. 6.60 covers the condition.

### What Changes It

The activities being done, and how much of them.

Body weight, which scales the force. 3.5.7 covers obesity.

Quadriceps strength and control, which change how the load is managed. Section
8 covers the training.

Hip and foot control, which change where the load goes. 3.12.7 covers dynamic
valgus.

### Clinical Relevance

The advice is about load rather than about damage, which is often reassuring
to hear.

Most people improve with a change in loading and in strength, without any
operation. Section 8 covers it.

A normal scan does not mean the pain is imagined. It means the pain is not
coming from something a scan sees. 5.12 covers the imaging.

Section 4 covers how the joint is examined.

### Key Learning Points

- A loaded joint can hurt without being damaged.
- Pain is commonest in stairs, squatting, kneeling and prolonged sitting.
- Patellar cartilage has no nerve supply, so the pain comes from elsewhere.
- Subchondral bone, synovium and fat pad are load sensitive and innervated.
- A normal scan does not mean the pain is imagined.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Mechanics predicts where load is highest and when. It does not predict who
will feel pain at a given load.

That mismatch is the central difficulty of this condition and the reason
purely mechanical models of it have disappointed.

The useful role of mechanics here is to explain the pattern of the symptoms,
which it does well, rather than their presence.

### At the Knee

The provocative activities map onto the arc of highest pressure, which is
early to mid flexion under load. 3.12.3 covers the pressures.

Prolonged sitting loads the joint at a modest pressure for a long time, which
is a different mechanical insult from a single squat.

Open chain extension near terminal extension produces peak pressure over a
small contact area, which is why it is often provocative. 3.12.2 covers the
areas.

Subchondral bone is the most plausible nociceptive source, and bone marrow
signal change correlates with symptoms imperfectly. 5.12.7 covers imaging
findings.

Chondromalacia is a cartilage finding rather than a diagnosis, and its
relationship to pain is weak. 6.61 covers it.

### What Changes It

Load magnitude, duration and rate, which are three separate variables often
collapsed into one.

Contact area at the loaded angle, which decides the pressure. 3.12.2 covers
it.

Quadriceps function, which changes both the force and its timing.

Proximal and distal control, which change the vector. 3.12.7 covers dynamic
valgus.

Factors outside mechanics altogether, including sleep, mood and sensitisation,
which change the threshold rather than the load.

### How It Is Measured

Symptom provocation by activity, which is the clinical measurement and is
subjective. 4.15.9 covers the assessment.

Estimated joint pressure from modelling, which gives a load and not a symptom.
3.16.4 covers modelling.

Magnetic resonance imaging of cartilage and bone marrow, which shows findings
with a weak association to pain. 5.12 covers it.

Instrumented strength and control testing, which measures a contributor rather
than the condition.

No measurement identifies the painful structure in an individual, which is why
the diagnosis remains clinical.

### Clinical Relevance

Use mechanics to explain the pattern of the symptoms and to guide loading, not
to explain why the patient has them.

Separate load magnitude, duration and rate when taking the history, because
the management differs.

Avoid attributing pain to a cartilage appearance, since the association is
weak. 6.61 covers chondromalacia.

Explain a normal scan as evidence about structures rather than about the
symptom. 5.12 covers the imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- Mechanics predicts where load is highest, not who will feel pain.
- It explains the pattern of symptoms well and their presence poorly.
- Prolonged sitting is a different mechanical insult from a single squat.
- Subchondral bone is the most plausible nociceptive source.
- No measurement identifies the painful structure in an individual.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The defensible position is that patellofemoral pain has mechanical
contributors and is not a mechanical diagnosis, and the literature has moved
steadily in that direction.

Pressure based explanations predicted that correcting malalignment would
relieve pain, and the results of realignment surgery for pain alone have been
poor. 7.56 covers the operation.

Loading programmes outperform mechanical corrections for pain, which is itself
a mechanical finding: it suggests tolerance rather than geometry is the
modifiable variable. 8.29 covers them.

Tissue level mechanics has moved to the subchondral bone and to the rate and
duration of loading rather than to peak pressure alone.

Conventional teaching has abandoned the single malalignment model, and the
alternative is a multifactorial account rather than a competing single cause.
6.153 covers the controversies.

### What Changes It

Cumulative load, which is magnitude multiplied by exposure and is the variable
most often unmeasured.

Loading rate, which modelling suggests matters independently of peak pressure.

Tissue tolerance, which training changes and geometry does not.

Skeletal geometry, which sets the baseline load distribution. 3.12.11 covers
it.

Central and psychosocial factors, which change the threshold and are not a
diagnosis of exclusion.

### How It Is Measured

Patient reported outcome measures, which are the primary endpoints in every
trial and are not mechanical quantities.

Modelled joint stress from motion capture and inverse dynamics, which has the
error chain described in 3.12.3.

Quantitative magnetic resonance imaging of cartilage composition, which is a
research tool with no clinical threshold. 5.12 covers the imaging.

Load monitoring by wearables, which measures exposure rather than stress and
is the most practical of these. 3.16.8 covers wearables.

The absence of a measurable link between a mechanical quantity and this
symptom is the reason the condition resists mechanical solutions.

### Clinical Relevance

Offer loading based management before considering any mechanical correction
for pain alone. 8.29 covers the programmes.

Reserve realignment for instability rather than for pain, because that is
where the evidence sits. 3.12.8 covers instability.

Quantify exposure as well as intensity when taking a history, since cumulative
load is the likelier driver.

Explain the multifactorial account honestly rather than substituting a single
mechanical cause the patient can act on.

Weigh the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, because tolerance and
adherence sit on that side of the balance rather than in the imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- The condition has mechanical contributors and is not a mechanical diagnosis.
- Realignment surgery for pain alone has performed poorly.
- Loading programmes outperform mechanical corrections, suggesting tolerance is the modifiable variable.
- Cumulative load and loading rate matter beyond peak pressure.
- Reserve realignment for instability rather than for pain.
