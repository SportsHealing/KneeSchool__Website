# Stance Phase

The foot is down and the knee is carrying the body. Sixty per cent of the
cycle, and where almost all the load is.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A limb carrying weight has to do two opposite things: absorb a landing and
then push the body past.

Absorbing means giving way in a controlled manner. Pushing means becoming
rigid.

A joint that must do both within half a second is doing the hardest thing in
walking.

### At the Knee

Stance begins at heel strike and ends at toe off, and it occupies about 60 per
cent of the cycle. 3.14.1 covers the whole cycle.

In loading response the knee flexes to around 15 to 20 degrees while the
quadriceps lengthen under tension, braking the collapse.

Through midstance the knee extends again as the body passes over the foot, and
loading is at its most efficient.

In terminal stance the knee begins to flex again in preparation for swing,
while the ankle is still pushing. 3.14.3 covers swing.

Ground reaction force has two peaks across stance, one after landing and one
before push off. 3.4.2 covers it.

### What Changes It

Walking speed, which raises both force peaks.

Quadriceps strength, which decides whether the loading response flexion
happens at all.

Pain, which shortens the time spent on that limb. 3.14.11 covers the arthritic
pattern.

Footwear and surface, which change how quickly the force rises at heel strike.
3.4.10 covers shock absorption.

### Clinical Relevance

A knee that stays straight through loading response has usually lost
quadriceps function rather than movement. 4.4.3 covers the gait.

Shortened stance time on one side is the commonest sign of pain in that limb.
4.4.2 covers the antalgic pattern.

Most knee loading in daily life happens in this phase, repeated thousands of
times a day.

Section 8 covers the rehabilitation that targets it.

### Key Learning Points

- Stance must absorb a landing and then push the body past.
- It occupies about 60 per cent of the cycle.
- Loading response flexes the knee 15 to 20 degrees under eccentric quadriceps control.
- Ground reaction force has two peaks across stance.
- A knee held straight through loading has usually lost quadriceps function.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Stance subdivides into loading response, midstance, terminal stance and
preswing, and each has a different mechanical demand.

Naming the subphase an abnormality occupies is more useful than naming the
limp, because the subphase identifies the structure under load at that moment.

The knee's moments reverse within stance, so a single average figure for the
phase conceals the thing worth knowing.

### At the Knee

Loading response: external flexion moment peaks, quadriceps work
eccentrically, and the stance flexion wave reaches 15 to 20 degrees.

Midstance: the knee approaches extension, the external moment swings towards
extension, and quadriceps demand falls.

Terminal stance: the moment arm is anterior, the knee is near full extension
and the posterior structures are tensioned.

Preswing: the knee flexes rapidly while still loaded, which is the transition
most often disrupted by stiffness.

The external adduction moment is double peaked across stance and tracks medial
compartment load throughout. 3.13.7 covers it.

### What Changes It

Speed and cadence, which scale the moments and compress the timing.

Quadriceps inhibition from pain or effusion, which flattens the stance flexion
wave.

Coronal alignment, which sets the adduction moment arm. 3.13.3 covers varus.

Trunk lean, which reduces the adduction moment without altering the knee.
3.13.7 covers it.

Ankle and hip function, since a stiff ankle transfers demand upward.

### How It Is Measured

Force plate and motion capture together, which is the only way to obtain the
moments. 3.16.7 covers motion capture.

Temporal parameters alone, from a pressure mat or instrumented walkway, which
give stance time without kinetics.

Electromyography for the timing of quadriceps activity, which distinguishes
weakness from inhibition.

Observation, which reliably identifies a missing stance flexion wave and
little else. 4.3.2 covers it.

Phase boundaries are defined by events that themselves must be detected, so
subphase timings differ between laboratories.

### Clinical Relevance

Name the subphase rather than the limp, because the subphase names the
structure under load.

Read a flattened stance flexion wave as quadriceps inhibition until proved
otherwise. 6.71 covers the arthritic knee.

Measure stance time symmetry, which is the cheapest reliable asymmetry marker
available.

Avoid averaging a moment across stance, since the knee's moments reverse
within it.

### Key Learning Points

- Stance subdivides into loading response, midstance, terminal stance and preswing.
- Naming the subphase identifies the structure under load at that moment.
- The knee's moments reverse within stance, so an averaged figure conceals the point.
- The adduction moment is double peaked and tracks medial compartment load.
- Preswing is the transition most often disrupted by stiffness.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Stance is where the clinically relevant mechanics almost entirely live, and
the stance flexion wave is its most informative single feature.

Its amplitude falls with pain, with effusion, with quadriceps weakness and
with arthrofibrosis, and the four are distinguishable by what else accompanies
them rather than by the wave itself.

Loss of the wave raises peak loading rate, because the limb no longer has a
compliant phase to spread the impact. 3.4.10 covers shock absorption.

The adduction moment's first peak is the parameter with the strongest reported
link to medial compartment progression, and it is a stance parameter. 3.13.8
covers the link.

Conventional teaching discusses gait abnormality in terms of named limps; the
measurable literature is in terms of subphase parameters, and the two map onto
each other poorly.

### What Changes It

Quadriceps capacity and its inhibition, which are different problems with the
same appearance.

Joint range, since a knee that cannot reach extension cannot complete
midstance.

Alignment and the adduction moment arm. 3.13.3 covers varus.

Speed, which must be matched before any two stance measurements are compared.

Compensations at the hip and ankle, which can conceal a knee abnormality
entirely.

### How It Is Measured

Three dimensional capture with force plates, reported as a moment curve rather
than a peak, because the shape carries the information.

Loading rate from the force trace, which is sensitive to heel strike
detection.

Electromyography to separate weakness from inhibition, which imaging cannot
do.

Stance time symmetry from a simple instrumented walkway, which is the most
deployable measurement here.

There is no clinical measurement of the stance flexion wave, which is the
thing the literature says matters most.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess the stance flexion wave by observation and treat its absence as
significant, since nothing in clinic measures it. 4.4.1 covers the
examination.

Separate quadriceps weakness from inhibition before prescribing, because the
treatments differ. Section 8 covers them.

Match walking speed before comparing any two stance measurements.

Report a moment curve rather than a peak when one is available.

Expect a lost flexion wave to raise loading rate, which is a mechanical
argument for restoring it rather than working around it.

### Key Learning Points

- The stance flexion wave is the most informative single feature of gait at the knee.
- Pain, effusion, weakness and arthrofibrosis all flatten it and look alike.
- Losing it raises peak loading rate, because the compliant phase is gone.
- The adduction moment's first peak is the strongest reported link to medial progression.
- Named limps and measurable subphase parameters map onto each other poorly.
