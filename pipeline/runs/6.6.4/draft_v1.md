# Prevention

The one part of this chapter with intervention evidence behind it, and the part
least often delivered.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

Prevention here means training that makes this injury less likely, done before
anything has gone wrong. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

It is a warm-up that has been designed rather than improvised, and it is done at
every session rather than once. 3.1.5 covers biomechanics and injury
prevention.

### Causes and Risk Factors

It works on the part of the risk that can be changed: how the body lands, turns
and stops. It cannot change the shape of a knee. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Most of these injuries happen with nobody touching the player, which is the
reason prevention is possible at all. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

### Key Learning Points

- Prevention means training done before anything goes wrong.
- It is a designed warm-up rather than an improvised one.
- It is done at every session rather than once.
- It changes how the body lands, turns and stops.
- It is possible because most of these injuries involve no contact.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

Prevention programmes are structured neuromuscular training sessions, usually
built into a warm-up, combining jumping and landing drills, strength work,
balance work and feedback on technique. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

They are the only part of this chapter where an intervention has been tested
rather than a mechanism proposed. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Management Overview

Two things limit them, and both are practical rather than scientific. 3.1.5
covers biomechanics and injury prevention.

The first is dose: they work when done consistently through a season and the
effect falls away when sessions are missed. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

The second is adherence: a programme that a squad finds dull, long or
unsupervised stops being done, and a programme not done prevents nothing. 6.6.1
covers the epidemiology.

### Rehabilitation Outline

The same content reappears after a reconstruction, where it is rehabilitation
rather than prevention, because a reconstructed knee carries a higher risk of
further injury than an uninjured one. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

That makes the second half of rehabilitation and the prevention programme
substantially the same work with a different name. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular
factors.

### Key Learning Points

- Programmes combine jumping, landing, strength, balance and feedback.
- They are the tested part of this chapter.
- Dose matters and the effect falls away when sessions are missed.
- A programme not done prevents nothing.
- The same content is rehabilitation after a reconstruction.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Does this actually work?
At squad level the evidence supports a reduction in injuries. What it means for
one player is a weaker claim, and this page does not convert a squad result into
a personal promise.

**Q.** How long does it take at each session?
The published programmes are built to fit a normal warm-up rather than to be
added to it, which is the design feature that makes them survive a season.

**Q.** Who should run it?
Somebody who watches the technique and corrects it. Unsupervised repetition
drifts, and the correction is the part with the effect in it.

**Q.** My club does not do anything like this. What now?
That is the common position and it is worth raising with the coach. The
programmes are published, free and designed for squads rather than clinics.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

Primary prevention targets uninjured athletes. Secondary prevention targets the
reconstructed knee and is usually called rehabilitation. The content overlaps
almost entirely. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

Programmes are multi-component by design, which is a strength clinically and a
weakness scientifically, since the active ingredient cannot be isolated. 3.1.5
covers biomechanics and injury prevention.

### Causes and Risk Factors

The target is the modifiable group: landing mechanics, trunk control, strength
balance and movement strategy under fatigue. 3.3.13 covers kinematics during
landing.

Fatigue belongs on that list because injuries cluster late in halves and late in
seasons, and a drill performed fresh does not test the state in which the injury
happens. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

### Management Overview

The components usually present are plyometrics, strength work for the posterior
chain, balance and agility drills, and explicit technique feedback. 6.6.3 covers
neuromuscular factors.

The technique feedback is the component most often dropped when a programme is
adopted locally, and it is the component most plausibly carrying the effect.
4.4.9 covers single leg landing assessment.

### Rehabilitation Outline

Return to sport after reconstruction uses the same drills under criteria, and
the criteria are strength symmetry, hop symmetry, movement quality, completed
sport specific exposure and the athlete's confidence. 6.8.1 covers graft
failure.

Time alone is the weakest criterion and the most commonly used one. 6.6.1 covers
the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Primary and secondary prevention share almost all their content.
- Multi-component design helps clinically and obscures the active ingredient.
- The target includes movement strategy under fatigue.
- Technique feedback is dropped most often and probably matters most.
- Time alone is the weakest and commonest release criterion.

## MRCS Level

### Management Overview

In a viva the expected answer names the components, states that the evidence is
at squad level, and identifies adherence as the limiting factor.

Say plyometrics, posterior chain strength, balance and agility, technique
feedback, delivered in the warm-up through the season. 3.1.5 covers biomechanics
and injury prevention.

Say that effect depends on dose and that implementation rather than efficacy is
the current problem. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Rehabilitation Outline

For the reconstructed athlete say criteria based release and name the criteria.
6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Say that the second injury risk in young pivoting athletes is the reason this
matters more in that group than in any other. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Complications and Prognosis

The failure mode is a programme adopted in name: shortened, unsupervised, done in
pre-season and abandoned by October. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

A programme in that state is reported as being in place and prevents nothing,
which is worth saying because the audit question asks whether it exists rather
than whether it is being done. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Name the components and the delivery setting.
- The evidence is at squad level and depends on dose.
- Implementation rather than efficacy is the current problem.
- For a reconstructed athlete, criteria based release and name the criteria.
- A programme adopted in name is reported as present and prevents nothing.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is asked what they would put in place for a
club or a school, which is a service question rather than a surgical one.

The answer that holds up names a published programme, a delivery slot inside
existing training, a person responsible for supervision, and a measure of whether
it is still happening in the second half of the season. 3.1.5 covers
biomechanics and injury prevention.

Omitting the fourth item is the usual failure, since a programme without a
monitoring step reliably stops. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

For the individual athlete returning after reconstruction the same answer becomes
criteria based release with the criteria stated. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

### Complications and Prognosis

The prognosis at squad level is better than the prognosis for implementation,
and that is the uncomfortable comparison. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

An intervention with evidence behind it that is not delivered produces the same
outcome as one without evidence, and the difference is invisible in an audit
that counts policies. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is which component carries the effect, which the
multi-component design prevents the existing trials from answering. 3.1.5 covers
biomechanics and injury prevention.

The second is what dose is needed to sustain the benefit through a season. 6.6.3
covers neuromuscular factors.

The third is whether these programmes reduce second injury after reconstruction
to the degree they reduce first injury in uninjured athletes. 6.8.1 covers graft
failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Name a programme, a slot, a responsible person and a monitoring step.
- Omitting the monitoring step is the usual failure.
- An undelivered intervention performs like one without evidence.
- An audit that counts policies cannot see that difference.
- Which component carries the effect is unresolved by design.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the athletes who arrive are the ones prevention did not
reach, and almost none of their records say whether a programme was available to
them.

That absence makes the local question unanswerable. Nobody can say whether this
clinic's second injuries are a training failure or a delivery failure. 6.6.3
covers neuromuscular factors.

The habit worth keeping is to record what the athlete's squad actually does:
whether a structured warm-up exists, who supervises it, and whether she was
doing it. Three questions, asked once. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

The second habit is to record what she returned to and when, so that the
denominator for a second injury exists at all. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Neither question is in a clinic template, and both are the only route from
individual cases to a local answer. 3.1.5 covers biomechanics and injury
prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- The arriving athletes are the ones prevention did not reach.
- Records rarely say whether a programme was available.
- That absence makes the local question unanswerable.
- Record whether a structured warm-up exists, who supervises, and whether she did it.
- Record what she returned to and when.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that the most effective thing in this chapter sits
outside the consultation entirely, and influence over it is indirect. 6.6.3
covers neuromuscular factors.

A surgeon can describe a programme, write to a club and raise it in a school, and
none of that is treatment, which is why it is usually left undone in a clinic
measured by operations. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which decide how many
seasons of exposure are ahead of her. Their medical comorbidities. The demands
they put on the knee, set by a squad that may or may not run anything. Their
psychological state, because an athlete who believes nothing can be done will do
nothing. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which rarely include
prevention at all, since it is the part of the subject nobody has told them
about.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and tell her what exists.
3.1.5 covers biomechanics and injury prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- The most effective thing here sits outside the consultation.
- Describing a programme is not treatment and is usually left undone.
- A clinic measured by operations does not count this work.
- An athlete who believes nothing can be done will do nothing.
- Tell her what exists.
