# Dial Test Biomechanics

Why comparing two flexion angles tells you what is injured. The test works by
subtraction, and the subtraction is the mechanics.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

If two structures resist the same motion at different joint angles, measuring
at both angles separates them.

The comparison does the work. A finding at one angle and not the other
implicates the structure tight at that angle.

That is a subtraction rather than a direct measurement, which is why the test
is read as a difference.

### At the Knee

External rotation of the tibia is resisted mainly by the posterolateral corner
at 30 degrees of flexion and increasingly by the posterior cruciate as flexion
increases. 3.10.2 covers external rotation.

Measuring external rotation at 30 and at 90 degrees therefore separates a
corner injury from a combined corner and posterior cruciate injury.

Increased rotation at 30 degrees alone indicates an isolated corner injury.
Increased rotation at both, worse at 90, indicates the posterior cruciate as
well. 3.8.1 covers that ligament.

The test is performed on both legs and read as a side to side difference,
because individual variation is large. Section 4 covers the technique.

### What Changes It

Which structures are injured.

The flexion angles used, since the comparison depends on them.

Muscle guarding, which reduces the measured rotation.

Whether both sides were compared.

### Clinical Relevance

The test's value is in the comparison, so performing it at one angle wastes
it. 4.13 covers the lateral examination.

A positive test at 30 degrees changes the management, because an isolated
corner injury is still an operative one. Section 6 covers management.

It is the clinical test that most directly reflects the corner's function,
which is why it appears in every account of the corner. 3.10.6 covers the
corner.

Under anaesthetic the findings are clearer, because guarding is removed.

### Key Learning Points

- Two structures resisting one motion at different angles can be separated by measuring both.
- The comparison does the work; the test is read as a difference.
- The corner dominates at 30 degrees and the posterior cruciate increasingly at 90.
- Increased rotation at 30 alone indicates an isolated corner injury.
- Worse at 90 indicates the posterior cruciate as well.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The test is an applied example of angle dependent restraint, which is the
organising idea of the whole section. 3.2.10 covers stability through range.

Its logic assumes the two structures' contributions change in opposite
directions with flexion, which they do: the corner slackens and the posterior
cruciate tightens. 3.8.3 covers the cruciate's bundles.

Where that assumption fails, the test fails. A knee with both injured and a
large laxity at both angles is harder to interpret than the clean two case
model suggests.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching reads an increase of more than 10 degrees of external
rotation compared with the uninjured side as significant.

An increase at 30 degrees that does not worsen at 90 is read as an isolated
posterolateral corner injury.

An increase at both angles that is greater at 90 is read as a combined corner
and posterior cruciate injury. 3.10.9 covers combined injury.

The test can be performed prone or supine, and the position affects
reproducibility more than the mechanics. 4.13 covers the technique.

### What Changes It

Which structures are injured and to what degree.

Guarding, which reduces the measurement.

Examiner technique and patient position.

Whether the comparison with the other side was made.

### How It Is Measured

Clinical measurement of the thigh foot angle or the tibial tubercle position
gives the rotation, with modest reproducibility.

Computed tomography and radiographic measurement under applied rotation
quantify it in research settings.

Sectioning studies establish which structures the test is sampling at each
angle.

No instrument in routine clinical use quantifies rotatory laxity, which is the
standing limitation. 3.10.2 covers that point.

### Clinical Relevance

Perform it at both angles and record both, since the comparison is the
finding.

Compare with the uninjured side rather than against an absolute figure.

Repeat under anaesthetic where the finding will change the operation.

Expect interpretation to be harder when both structures are badly injured.

### Key Learning Points

- The test applies angle dependent restraint, the section's organising idea.
- It assumes the corner slackens and the posterior cruciate tightens with flexion.
- Conventional teaching reads more than 10 degrees of side to side difference as significant.
- The two case model is harder to apply when both structures are badly injured.
- No routine instrument quantifies the rotation.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the dial test as the intraoperative check as well as the clinical one.
Performed under anaesthetic before and after fixation, it is the measurement
that says whether the corner reconstruction worked. 3.10.8 covers the
construct.

Its limitation is that it has no instrument, so a comparison between two
examinations by different people is weak. That limits its use as an outcome
measure and not its use as an intraoperative check.

Conventional teaching holds that a residual positive dial test after corner
reconstruction predicts a poorer result, which makes the intraoperative check
the one worth doing.

### What Changes It

Which components were reconstructed and their orientation.

Graft tension and the flexion angle at fixation.

Alignment, which loads the construct. 3.13 covers alignment.

Posterior cruciate status, where it is also injured.

### How It Is Measured

Examination under anaesthetic before and after fixation, at both angles, is
the practical check.

Varus stress radiography measures the coronal component reproducibly and not
the rotational one.

Research instrumentation quantifies rotation and is not available clinically.

Inter observer comparison is weak, which limits its use as an outcome measure.

### Clinical Relevance

Perform the dial test under anaesthetic before and after corner fixation.

Use it as an intraoperative check rather than as a comparative outcome
measure.

Record both angles and the side to side difference.

Investigate a residual positive test before leaving theatre.

### Key Learning Points

- The dial test is an intraoperative check as well as a clinical one.
- It says whether the corner reconstruction worked.
- Having no instrument limits its use as an outcome measure, not as a check.
- A residual positive test after reconstruction predicts a poorer result.
- Investigate a residual positive test before leaving theatre.
