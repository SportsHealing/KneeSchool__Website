# Acute Haemarthrosis

Blood in a knee within hours of an injury, which is a short differential and an
urgent one.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

An acute haemarthrosis is a tense effusion appearing within hours of an injury,
and its significance is that blood comes from a vascular structure.

The differential is short. A cruciate rupture, an osteochondral fracture, a
peripheral meniscal tear, a patellar dislocation and an intra-articular fracture
account for most of them. 4.2.22 covers the dislocation event.

The timing is what makes the diagnosis. A tense effusion within hours is blood;
an effusion appearing over a day or two is synovial fluid and a different
differential. 4.2.3 covers swelling as a symptom.

### How It Is Performed

Establish the timing precisely: how long after the injury the knee swelled, and
how quickly.

Assess the effusion's size and tension, since a tense effusion is the
characteristic finding. 4.8.6 covers ballottement.

Assess the range, which will be limited by the tension, and record that the
effusion was present when it was measured. 4.8.2 covers the significance.

Examine the ligaments, accepting that the examination is less reliable in this
setting and that guarding produces false reassurance. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman
test.

Examine for a patellar dislocation: medial retinacular tenderness, apprehension
and a palpable defect. 4.6.5 covers patellar tenderness.

### What a Positive Means

A tense effusion within hours of an injury indicates blood and therefore a
structural injury, even if the ligament examination appears normal.

A normal ligament examination in an acute haemarthrosis is not reassurance,
because guarding reduces every translational finding. 4.14.6 covers the setting
that removes it.

Medial retinacular tenderness with a haemarthrosis after a twisting injury
indicates a patellar dislocation, which is the diagnosis most often missed in
this group. 4.2.22 covers the event.

A haemarthrosis with a mechanical block to extension indicates a displaced
fragment or a displaced meniscal tear, which is urgent. 4.9.8 covers mechanical
locking.

A haemarthrosis with a palpable fat globule on aspiration indicates an
intra-articular fracture, which changes the management entirely.

### Key Learning Points

- A tense effusion within hours of an injury is blood and indicates a structural injury.
- The differential is short and includes a cruciate rupture, a fracture and a patellar dislocation.
- A normal ligament examination in this setting is not reassurance.
- Medial retinacular tenderness with a haemarthrosis suggests a patellar dislocation.
- A mechanical block with a haemarthrosis is urgent.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the acute haemarthrosis is a standard station and the differential is
the answer, with the timing as the reasoning.

The expected points are the timing, the tension, the limited range, the ligament
examination with its caveat, and the patellar assessment.

Say the differential list without being prompted, because that is the content of
the question.

Say that a normal ligament examination here is unreliable, and say what you
would do about it: a repeat examination when the effusion settles, or an
examination under anaesthesia. 4.14.6 covers it.

Say that you would image the knee, since a fracture and an osteochondral injury
are both in the differential and neither is a clinical diagnosis. 4.1.6 covers
that relationship.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the structures capable of producing a rapid
haemarthrosis are vascular, so the differential is anatomical rather than
statistical. 4.8.1 covers normal fluid physiology.

The anterior cruciate has a blood supply and a synovial covering, which is why
its rupture bleeds, and it is the commonest cause in a young patient. 2.7.1
covers its anatomy where that chapter exists.

The peripheral third of the meniscus is vascular and the inner part is not,
which is why a peripheral tear bleeds and a central one does not. 2.5.3 covers
the vascular supply.

A patellar dislocation tears the medial retinaculum and often causes an
osteochondral injury, which is why it produces a haemarthrosis and why it is
missed when the patella has reduced spontaneously. 4.2.22 covers the event.

### How Good It Is

The clinical diagnosis of a haemarthrosis from the timing and the tension is
reliable, and the diagnosis of its cause from the examination is not.

The examination in this setting fails towards false reassurance, because
guarding reduces the translational findings, which is the most important
limitation on the page. 4.13.4 covers the assessment that suffers most.

Imaging and, where appropriate, aspiration add what the examination cannot,
which makes this a presentation where the examination's job is to establish
urgency rather than diagnosis. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

The timing is obtained from the history, so the history is the diagnostic
instrument here rather than the hands. 4.2.3 covers it.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The structures capable of a rapid haemarthrosis are vascular, so the differential is anatomical.
- The peripheral third of the meniscus is vascular and the inner part is not.
- A patellar dislocation tears the retinaculum and often causes an osteochondral injury.
- The examination here fails towards false reassurance.
- The timing comes from the history, which is the diagnostic instrument.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the haemarthrosis establishes urgency, and the urgency is what
protects the treatable diagnoses in the differential.

An osteochondral fragment is repairable acutely and not later, which is the
clearest time dependent diagnosis in this group.

A displaced meniscal tear with a block is repairable acutely, and the window
matters. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

A patellar dislocation with an osteochondral injury needs identifying before the
effusion settles and the diagnosis is attributed to something else. 4.2.22
covers the event.

A cruciate rupture is not urgent, which makes it the one diagnosis in the
differential that can wait, and recognising that prevents an unnecessary rush.
4.10.2 covers the acute knee.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is concluding a normal examination from a guarding effused
knee.

The second is attributing a haemarthrosis to a cruciate rupture by default and
missing the time dependent diagnoses.

The third is not imaging the knee.

The fourth is missing a patellar dislocation that reduced spontaneously, since
the history may not mention it. 4.2.22 covers it.

The fifth is recording a range and a laxity grade without noting the
haemarthrosis. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### Key Learning Points

- The urgency protects the treatable diagnoses in the differential.
- An osteochondral fragment is repairable acutely and not later.
- A patellar dislocation with an osteochondral injury needs identifying before the effusion settles.
- A cruciate rupture is the one diagnosis that can wait.
- Attributing a haemarthrosis to a cruciate rupture by default misses the time dependent diagnoses.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the acute haemarthrosis has usually already been managed,
and the referral concerns what was missed in it.

A patient with a haemarthrosis attributed to a cruciate rupture who turns out to
have had a patellar dislocation has a different problem and a different
operation. 4.2.22 covers the event.

A patient with an osteochondral fragment identified late has lost the repair
window, and that loss is usually traceable to the imaging rather than to the
examination.

A patient whose ligament examination was normal acutely and abnormal at six
weeks did not change, and the first examination was unreliable. 4.14.6 covers
the alternative.

A patient with a recurrent haemarthrosis without injury has a vascular or
haematological cause, and that is a different pathway from anything in this
section. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

In medicolegal work the recorded timing of an acute effusion is the single most
useful fact in these cases, and a vague entry is one of the commonest
weaknesses in a record. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A haemarthrosis attributed to a cruciate rupture may have been a patellar dislocation.
- An osteochondral fragment identified late has lost its repair window.
- A ligament examination normal acutely and abnormal at six weeks was unreliable the first time.
- A recurrent haemarthrosis without injury has a vascular or haematological cause.
- A vague recorded timing is one of the commonest weaknesses in a record.
