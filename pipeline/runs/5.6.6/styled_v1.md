# Bucket Handle Tears

A long vertical tear whose inner fragment has displaced into the joint, which is
the tear that locks a knee.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A bucket handle tear is a long longitudinal vertical tear whose inner fragment
has flipped into the notch, leaving the outer rim in place. 5.6.4 covers
vertical tears.

The displaced fragment is the handle and the remaining rim is the bucket, which
is where the name comes from.

It matters because this is the pattern that blocks movement, so it is one of the
few meniscal findings that is urgent. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

### How It Is Acquired

Look for a meniscus that appears too small, because part of it is no longer in
its parent position. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Look in the intercondylar notch on the coronal images for the displaced fragment,
which lies between the condyles. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Look on the sagittal images for the double posterior cruciate sign, where the
fragment lies beneath the cruciate and mimics a second one. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

Count the bow tie appearances on the peripheral sagittal images, since an absent
bow tie indicates the body of the meniscus has gone. 5.6.1 covers the normal
meniscus.

Establish which meniscus has displaced, because the medial side is far more
often involved. 2.5.1 covers the gross anatomy.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A fragment of meniscal tissue lying in the intercondylar notch, dark like the
meniscus it came from.

The double posterior cruciate appearance, which is the displaced fragment lying
parallel and inferior to the cruciate on a sagittal image. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

An absent bow tie on the peripheral sagittal images, meaning the meniscal body is
not where it should be. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

A parent meniscus that is truncated, with the remaining rim narrower than the
other side. 5.6.12 covers the post operative meniscus.

### Key Learning Points

- The inner fragment has displaced into the notch and the outer rim remains.
- It is one of the few meniscal findings that is urgent.
- A meniscus that looks too small is the first clue.
- The double cruciate appearance is the fragment under the cruciate.
- An absent bow tie means the body has gone.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the locked knee and the expected answer names the signs.

The expected points are the small meniscus, the fragment in the notch, the
double cruciate appearance, the absent bow tie, and which side.

Say that a displaced fragment can block extension and that this is urgent.
4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

Say that you would look specifically in the notch rather than only at the
meniscus. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Say that the medial meniscus is more often involved. 2.5.1 covers the gross
anatomy.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a long longitudinal tear separates an inner
fragment that remains attached at its ends, so it can hinge into the notch and
back again. 3.6.3 covers the circumferential fibres.

Because it stays attached, the fragment is viable tissue and the tear is often
repairable once reduced. 3.6.13 covers meniscal repair biomechanics.

The block to movement is mechanical rather than painful, which is why a locked
knee has a firm end point rather than a guarded one. 4.9.8 covers mechanical
locking.

The fragment's position explains the signs: in the notch it lies next to the
cruciate, and its absence from the parent site removes the bow tie. 5.6.1 covers
the normal meniscus.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging detects a displaced fragment reliably when the signs
are looked for.

Its best contribution is locating the fragment, which the examination cannot do
and which the operation needs. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

It is missed when the meniscus alone is assessed and the notch is not, because
the parent site then reads as a previous meniscectomy. 5.6.12 covers the post
operative meniscus.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The fragment stays attached at its ends, so it can hinge in and out.
- Because it stays attached it is viable and often repairable once reduced.
- The block is mechanical, which is why the end point is firm.
- The fragment's position explains every sign.
- It is missed when the notch is not assessed.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this is a finding that changes the timetable rather than only
the plan.

A displaced fragment with a locked knee is operated sooner than an undisplaced
tear, and the imaging confirms what the examination suspected. 4.9.8 covers
mechanical locking.

The fragment's viability and the tear's position decide between reduction with
repair and resection, and the imaging contributes the position. 3.6.13 covers
meniscal repair biomechanics.

A displaced fragment in an older degenerate meniscus is a different proposition
from the same displacement in a young knee, and the tissue quality is the
difference the imaging cannot show. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

An intermittently reducing fragment may be absent from the scan and present
clinically, so a normal looking meniscus does not settle it. 4.9.8 covers
mechanical locking.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting a small meniscus as a previous meniscectomy
without looking in the notch. 5.6.12 covers the post operative meniscus.

The double cruciate appearance is missed because only the meniscus was being
assessed. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

A reduced fragment at the time of scanning gives a near normal study in a knee
that locks. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

The meniscofemoral ligament is mistaken for a displaced fragment next to the
cruciate. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Repairability is judged from the displacement alone, when the tissue quality
matters as much. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

### Key Learning Points

- Reporting a small meniscus as a meniscectomy without checking the notch is the commonest error.
- The double cruciate sign is missed when only the meniscus is assessed.
- A reduced fragment gives a near normal study in a knee that locks.
- The meniscofemoral ligament mimics a displaced fragment.
- Displacement alone does not settle repairability.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this is a finding where the imaging and the clinical picture
most often disagree, and the disagreement is informative.

A knee that locks intermittently has a fragment that reduces, so the scan
catches it in one state and the patient describes the other. 4.9.8 covers
mechanical locking.

A chronically displaced fragment remodels and becomes harder to recognise as
meniscal tissue, and its parent rim atrophies. 5.6.12 covers the post operative
meniscus.

Repair after chronic displacement depends on tissue that has been out of
position for months, which the imaging cannot assess. 3.6.13 covers meniscal
repair biomechanics.

A previous partial meniscectomy and a chronically displaced fragment produce
similar parent appearances, and the surgical history is what separates them.
5.6.12 covers the post operative meniscus.

The fragment's position at scanning is not where it will be at operation, which
makes the imaging a statement about the tear rather than a map. 5.5.1 covers the
standard protocol.

### Key Learning Points

- An intermittently locking knee is caught in one state by the scan.
- A chronically displaced fragment remodels and is harder to recognise.
- Repair depends on tissue quality the imaging cannot assess.
- A previous meniscectomy and chronic displacement look alike.
- The fragment's position at scanning is not where it will be at operation.
