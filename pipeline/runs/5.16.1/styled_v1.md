# Fracture Assessment

What computed tomography adds to a knee fracture, which is a classification
rather than a diagnosis.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Computed tomography shows the cortical outline and the articular surface of a
knee fracture in any plane, from one acquisition. 5.2.22 covers the CT
acquisition.

It shows the number of fragments, where each one sits, and how far the articular
surface has been depressed. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

It matters because the fracture is usually already visible on the radiograph,
and what changes with the scan is how the injury is classified. 5.3.1 covers the
AP view.

### How It Is Acquired

Request it after the radiographs rather than instead of them, because the
radiograph decides whether the scan is needed. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct
investigation.

Acquire thin slices through the joint, since a reformat can only be as good as
the slice it was built from. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

Review the axial images alongside coronal and sagittal reformats rather than one
plane alone. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

Assess the articular surface separately from the metaphysis, because they drive
different decisions. 2.2.2 covers the tibial plateau.

Record the dose, since this is a higher dose study than the radiographs it
follows. 5.2.24 covers the radiation dose.

### What Normal Looks Like

A continuous cortical outline on every reformat. 2.1.8 covers the imaging
anatomy.

An articular surface at an even level across the plateau and the condyles. 2.2.2
covers the tibial plateau.

A trabecular pattern without the impaction lines of a compression injury. 5.13.3
covers stress fractures.

No fragment separate from the bone it belongs to. 5.3.14 covers avulsion
injuries.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A cortical break traced across consecutive slices rather than seen on one.
5.3.13 covers femoral condyle fractures.

Articular depression measured from the surrounding intact surface. 2.2.2 covers
the tibial plateau.

The number and position of the fragments, including those hidden on the
radiograph. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

An avulsion at a ligament attachment, which points at the ligament rather than
the bone. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Impaction with no cortical break, which a radiograph can miss entirely. 5.13.3
covers stress fractures.

### Key Learning Points

- The fracture is usually visible on the radiograph first.
- The scan changes the classification rather than the diagnosis.
- A reformat is limited by the slice thickness acquired.
- The articular surface and the metaphysis drive different decisions.
- This is a higher dose study than the radiographs it follows.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer requests the radiographs first and says why.

The expected points are the radiographs first, the thin slices, all three
planes, the articular surface, and the dose.

Say that the radiograph establishes the injury and the scan characterises it.
5.3.1 covers the AP view.

Say that a reformat cannot recover detail the acquisition did not capture.
5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

Say that the scan shows bone and says nothing about the ligaments. 5.5.9 covers
the ligaments.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that an operation on an articular fracture is planned
around fragment position and surface depression, neither of which a single
projection can give. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

Depression is measured against the intact surface nearby, so the reference
matters as much as the figure. 2.2.2 covers the tibial plateau.

A posterior fragment is the one a lateral radiograph understates and the one
that changes the approach. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

An avulsion fracture is a ligament injury carrying a piece of bone, so the
report should name the ligament. 5.3.15 covers Segond fractures.

### How Good It Is

Computed tomography is the best available test of fracture architecture in the
knee and adds nothing about the soft tissues.

Its best contribution is fragment position and articular depression, which
decide the approach. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

It is limited by soft tissue contrast, by the dose, and by a normal scan not
meaning an uninjured knee. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no slice
thicknesses, dose values or depression thresholds appear either: those are local
protocol and published quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- Fragment position and surface depression decide the approach.
- Depression is measured against a reference that must be stated.
- A posterior fragment changes the approach and hides on a radiograph.
- An avulsion is a ligament injury carrying bone.
- A normal scan is not an uninjured knee.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the scan is requested to choose an approach and a fixation, so
a report listing fragments without their positions has not helped. 5.20.8 covers
common reporting errors.

The articular surface decides whether the operation is a reduction or an
augmentation. 2.2.2 covers the tibial plateau.

The posterior structures decide whether the approach is anterior at all. 5.3.2
covers the lateral view.

A magnetic resonance study is added when the ligaments and menisci change the
plan, which in these injuries is often. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

The dose is justified by the plan that follows it rather than by completeness.
5.1.5 covers the cost-effectiveness of imaging.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading a bone scan as a knee assessment. 5.5.9 covers
the ligaments.

Thick slices produce a stair artefact at an oblique surface, which is read as a
step in the articular surface. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

Metal or motion degrades the study where the question sits. 5.2.19 covers the
artefacts.

A fracture reduced by the position on the table is reported as a reduced
fracture. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

An old healed injury is reported as acute, because the scan carries no interval.
5.13.3 covers stress fractures.

### Key Learning Points

- A bone scan is not a knee assessment.
- Thick slices mimic an articular step.
- Artefact degrades the study where the question sits.
- Positioning on the table can reduce a fracture.
- The scan carries no interval, so age is inferred.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the scan arrives with the patient and the first fixation has
already been done elsewhere. 5.20.7 covers reporting the post-operative knee.

The pre-operative anatomy cannot be recovered from a scan taken after fixation,
which makes the original study the more valuable one to obtain. 5.12.8 covers
the post-operative imaging.

Classification systems were built on radiographs and on scans from earlier eras
of resolution, so a modern scan reclassifies the injuries those systems were
calibrated on. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

Automated fracture detection performs on the obvious injuries and is weakest on
the impaction that changes the plan. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in
imaging.

Repeat scanning through a treatment course accumulates dose in a population that
is often young. 5.2.24 covers the radiation dose.

The useful report gives the fragments with their positions, the surface with its
reference, the dose, and the soft tissue question stated as open. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- A scan after fixation cannot recover the original anatomy.
- Classification systems were calibrated on lower resolution.
- Automated detection is weakest on the impaction.
- Repeat scanning accumulates dose in young patients.
- The report should state the soft tissue question as open.
