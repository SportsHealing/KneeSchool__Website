# Structured Reporting

The template that decides what a report contains, and the reason a described
scan is not the same thing as an answered question.

## For Medical Students

### What the Report Is For

A structured report follows a fixed set of headings rather than free prose, so
the same facts appear in the same order for every study. 5.1.1 covers why
imaging matters.

The pages of this chapter are built on the report rather than on an image, so
they ask what the report is for, how it is built, what a good one and a poor one
look like, how good reporting is, and where reports mislead. 5.1.7 covers
evidence-based imaging.

It matters because the report is the only part of the study most referrers
read, so what the template omits is effectively absent. 5.1.2 covers clinical
examination versus imaging.

### How the Report Is Built

Start from the clinical question in the request, because that is what the report
has to answer. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

State the technique: the sequences acquired, the planes, and anything that
limited them. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Work through the structures in a fixed order, so an omission shows up as a
missing heading rather than hiding in prose. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Record the relevant negatives, since a question is answered only when the
alternative has been excluded. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Finish with a conclusion that answers the question in the words the referrer
used. 4.1.8 covers the examination documentation.

### What a Good One Looks Like

A technique paragraph that says what was acquired and what was not. 5.5.1
covers the standard protocol.

A findings section organised by structure, each one stated as present, absent or
not assessable. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

Measurements given with the convention used to make them. 5.16.4 covers the
TT-TG measurement.

A conclusion short enough to be read in full and specific enough to act on.
5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

### What a Poor One Looks Like

A report that describes the images and never returns to the question. 5.1.3
covers choosing the correct investigation.

A findings list with no negatives, so a structure's absence from the text could
mean normal or unexamined. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

A conclusion that repeats the findings rather than interpreting them. 5.20.8
covers common reporting errors.

A measurement with no convention attached to it. 5.16.4 covers the TT-TG
measurement.

Hedging language that hands the decision back to the referrer. 4.1.6 covers the
examination in the era of MRI.

### Key Learning Points

- The template fixes what a report must contain.
- The referrer reads the report rather than the images.
- Without negatives, an omitted structure looks like a normal one.
- A measurement without its convention cannot be used.
- The conclusion answers the question that was asked.

## MRCS Level

### How the Report Is Built

In a viva the expected answer treats the report as an instrument with a user.

The expected points are the clinical question, the technique, the fixed order,
the relevant negatives, and a conclusion that decides something.

Say that the quality of the request sets a ceiling on the quality of the report.
5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

Say that a structured report can be audited where free prose cannot. 5.1.7
covers evidence-based imaging.

Say that the same template is what makes two studies comparable over time.
5.20.7 covers reporting the post-operative knee.

### What a Good One Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a report serves a reader who will not look at the
images, so completeness and ordering do work that prose cannot. 4.1.8 covers the
examination documentation.

Relevant negatives are the part most often dropped and the part a surgeon leans
on hardest, because an operation is planned around what is not there. 5.17.2
covers the meniscal repair.

A fixed order lets a reader find one structure without reading the whole report,
which is how reports are used in a clinic. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Naming the convention behind a measurement is what makes the number portable
between departments. 5.16.4 covers the TT-TG measurement.

### How Good It Is

Structured reporting is the best available way to make reports complete and does
nothing for the judgement inside them.

Its best contribution is completeness and comparability, both of which free text
achieves by luck. 5.1.7 covers evidence-based imaging.

It is limited by the fact that a template can be filled in without thought, so a
complete report can still be wrong. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no reader
agreement statistics appear either: those are published quantities from
particular reader panels and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- A report serves a reader who will not see the images.
- Negatives are dropped most often and relied on most heavily.
- A fixed order is how reports are used in clinic.
- The convention is what makes a measurement portable.
- A template improves completeness rather than judgement.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a report either constrains the operative plan or it does not,
and most reports do not. 5.1.2 covers clinical examination versus imaging.

A report naming the pattern, the location and the extent constrains the plan.
5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

A report naming the structure alone leaves the plan where it was. 5.20.8 covers
common reporting errors.

The surgeon reviews the images regardless, so the report's value lies in
directing that review rather than replacing it. 4.1.6 covers the examination in
the era of MRI.

A conclusion that commits is what allows a disagreement between the report and
the images to be noticed at all. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading completeness as accuracy. 5.20.8 covers common
reporting errors.

A populated template reads as a thorough study even when the sequences acquired
could not support the findings. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Text copied from a previous report carries forward a finding that has since
changed. 5.20.7 covers reporting the post-operative knee.

Standard phrases acquire local meanings, so one department's wording is read as
the national one. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

A long findings section with a short conclusion reads as a careful report when
it may be an undecided one. 4.1.6 covers the examination in the era of MRI.

### Key Learning Points

- Completeness is read as accuracy.
- A populated template can outrun the sequences acquired.
- Copied text carries a stale finding forward.
- Standard phrases carry local meanings.
- Length is read as care.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the report in front of you was written in another
department, to another template, for another question. 5.1.3 covers choosing the
correct investigation.

The words have no shared definition between departments, so the same appearance
yields different reports and the referrer cannot tell which. 5.21.1 covers the
meniscal imaging literature.

A report generated automatically is complete by construction and carries no
record of what the model could not assess. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence
in imaging.

Serial comparison across departments compares templates as much as it compares
knees. 5.20.6 covers reporting osteoarthritis.

Re-reporting a study from elsewhere is a clinical act and belongs in the notes
as one. 4.1.8 covers the examination documentation.

The useful report states the question, the technique and its limits, the
findings with their negatives, the measurements with their conventions, and a
conclusion that commits. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

### Key Learning Points

- Report words have no shared definition between departments.
- An automated report is complete by construction.
- Serial comparison across departments compares templates.
- Re-reporting is a clinical act and belongs in the notes.
- A conclusion that commits is the point of the exercise.
