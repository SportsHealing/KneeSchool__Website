# Childress Squat Test

Deep flexion under load. The test that reaches the posterior horn, and the one
most patients cannot do.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The Childress test asks the patient to squat fully and then walk or rock in
that position, which compresses the posterior horns of both menisci under
load.

It tests the part of the meniscus the other tests reach least well, because
full flexion is where the posterior horn is loaded.

It also tests function directly, since a patient who cannot squat has a
finding whatever the cause.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the patient to squat down as far as they comfortably can, with support
available.

Ask them to hold the position and then to rock or waddle forwards a short
distance in the squat.

Note where the pain is, whether it is at the joint line, and whether the knee
catches or blocks.

Note how far they could squat before symptoms began, which is a recordable
measure. 4.7.4 covers flexion assessment.

Do not persist if the patient cannot squat safely, and record the inability
rather than forcing it.

### What a Positive Means

Posterior joint line pain in the squat, or a catch or block, is a positive for
a posterior horn problem.

Anterior pain in the squat is patellofemoral, because a deep squat loads the
patellofemoral joint heavily. 4.15.9 covers it.

An inability to squat at all is a finding and needs attributing: pain, a
block, stiffness or weakness are different causes with different implications.

A patient who squats fully and comfortably has made a posterior horn tear less
likely, which is one of the more useful negatives in this chapter.

### Key Learning Points

- The test loads the posterior horns in full flexion, which other tests reach least well.
- Anterior pain in a deep squat is patellofemoral rather than meniscal.
- An inability to squat is a finding that needs attributing to pain, block, stiffness or weakness.
- A full comfortable squat makes a posterior horn tear less likely.
- Record how far they squatted before symptoms began.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected points are the full squat, the loaded rocking, the site
of the pain, and the recorded depth.

The candidate should say why full flexion is used, which is that the posterior
horn is loaded there and is reached poorly in a supine test. 3.6.1 covers
meniscal function.

Say that the test is a functional as well as a provocative test, which is
unusual in this chapter.

Say that you would not force it and would record an inability rather than a
negative.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that deep flexion shifts tibiofemoral contact
posteriorly and concentrates load on the posterior horn. 3.5.4 covers the
effects of flexion.

That makes the squat the natural provocation for a posterior horn or root
lesion, and a squatting injury is a classic mechanism for one. 4.9.9 covers
roots.

It also loads the patellofemoral joint heavily, which is why attributing the
pain by site is essential rather than optional.

And it loads the cartilage of the posterior condyles, which gives a third
possible source in the same position. 4.17.1 covers the cartilage
presentation.

### How Good It Is

The Childress test is less studied than the McMurray and Thessaly tests and
its reported performance is correspondingly less certain.

The honest position is that it shares the group's pattern and that its
particular value is access to the posterior horn rather than superior
accuracy.

Its main practical limitation is that many patients, particularly older ones
and those with stiff or painful knees, cannot perform it, so it is unavailable
in much of the population it would help.

That selective availability is a real limitation and is rarely stated.

No figures appear, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Deep flexion shifts contact posteriorly and loads the posterior horn.
- A squatting injury is a classic posterior horn and root mechanism.
- The position also loads the patellofemoral joint and the posterior condylar cartilage.
- Its value is access to the posterior horn rather than superior accuracy.
- Many patients cannot perform it, which is a real and rarely stated limitation.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the squat test is useful for two things: posterior horn
localisation and a functional baseline.

The posterior horn is where root tears sit, and a root tear behaves
differently from a body tear because it releases hoop tension. 4.9.9 covers
it.

The functional baseline matters in counselling, because a patient who cannot
squat before an operation is unlikely to squat after it, and saying so early
is better than discovering it late.

It is also the test most directly relevant to the activities patients with
posterior horn problems complain about, which is squatting, kneeling and
getting up from low seats.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is interpreting any pain in a deep squat as meniscal, when
the position loads three structures.

The second is recording a negative when the patient could not perform the
test, which is a different result entirely.

The third is forcing the squat in a knee with a suspected displaced fragment
or a recent repair.

And using a patient's inability to squat as evidence of a meniscal problem
when it is frequently stiffness or quadriceps weakness. 4.7.7 covers
contractures.

### Key Learning Points

- Its decision value is posterior horn localisation and a functional baseline.
- A patient who cannot squat before an operation is unlikely to squat after it.
- The position loads meniscus, patellofemoral joint and posterior cartilage together.
- An inability to perform the test is not a negative result.
- Inability to squat is frequently stiffness or weakness rather than a meniscal problem.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

After surgery the squat is more useful as a functional measure than as a
provocative test, which is the same conclusion 4.9.4 reaches for the weight
bearing rotation.

A patient who regains a full painless squat after meniscal surgery has a good
functional result whatever the imaging shows.

A patient who does not needs the cause separated: a new tear, a deficient
meniscus, posterior cartilage damage, stiffness, or fear. 4.9.10 covers
deficiency.

After a root repair the squat is specifically restricted during the protected
period, because deep flexion loads the repair, so performing the test is a
protocol decision. 4.20.3 covers root repair assessment.

In the degenerate knee an inability to squat is close to universal and the
test therefore contributes nothing diagnostically, though it remains a useful
functional record. 4.18.6 covers functional limitation.

No figure here supports these claims, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- After surgery the squat is a functional measure rather than a provocative test.
- A regained full painless squat is a good functional result whatever the imaging shows.
- After a root repair deep flexion is restricted, so the test is a protocol decision.
- In the degenerate knee an inability to squat is near universal and contributes nothing diagnostically.
