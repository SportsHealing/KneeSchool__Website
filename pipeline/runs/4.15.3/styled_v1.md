# Patellar Apprehension Test

The test where the finding is the patient's reaction rather than the examiner's
measurement.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The patellar apprehension test displaces the patella laterally and looks for the
patient's apprehension: a reflex guarding, a grab at the examiner's hand, or a
statement that this is how it feels when it goes.

It is the only test in this section whose positive finding is a behaviour rather
than a laxity, and that is both its strength and its weakness. 4.2.2 covers
instability as a symptom.

What it establishes is that the patient recognises the direction of their
instability, which is more specific for patellar instability than any
measurement of patellar mobility. 4.15.4 covers the measurement.

### How It Is Performed

Lay the patient supine with the knee extended and the quadriceps relaxed, and
explain what you are about to do.

Place both thumbs on the medial border of the patella and displace it gently
laterally.

Watch the patient's face and the quadriceps, and stop as soon as apprehension
appears.

Flex the knee gently while maintaining the lateral pressure, since apprehension
often appears in the first part of flexion as the patella is carried towards the
groove.

Record what the patient did and said, rather than recording the test as
positive. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

Apprehension, guarding, or the patient stopping you indicates that the lateral
displacement reproduces their symptom, which supports patellar instability.

A patient who says this is exactly how it feels when the knee goes has given the
most useful version of the finding.

Discomfort without apprehension is not a positive test, and recording it as one
converts a painful knee into an unstable one. 4.15.9 covers patellofemoral pain.

A negative test in a patient with documented dislocations does not exclude
instability, because apprehension depends on the patient's confidence as much as
on the anatomy. 4.2.22 covers the dislocation event.

Apprehension on medial displacement is unusual and suggests a previous
reconstruction that is over constrained or a medial instability. 4.20.6 covers
that assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- The finding is a behaviour rather than a laxity.
- It establishes that the patient recognises the direction of their instability.
- Discomfort without apprehension is not a positive test.
- A negative test does not exclude instability, since confidence contributes.
- Apprehension on medial displacement suggests over constraint or medial instability.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this test rewards a candidate who explains it to the patient first and
who stops at apprehension rather than pushing through it.

The expected points are the relaxed extended knee, the explanation, the thumbs
on the medial border, watching the patient rather than the patella, and the
gentle flexion.

Say that you would stop at apprehension, both because continuing is unkind and
because the finding is already obtained.

Say that you would record the patient's words, since the specific statement is
worth more than the label. 4.1.8 covers the record.

Say that you would perform the glide and tilt tests separately, because those
measure the restraints and this one reads the symptom. 4.15.4 covers the glide.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the medial patellofemoral ligament is the
principal restraint to lateral patellar displacement near extension, so
displacing the patella laterally loads it and reproduces the mechanism of
dislocation. 2.12.2 covers the ligament.

Apprehension is a protective response to that mechanism being reproduced, which
is why the finding is specific to instability rather than to pain. 3.12.8 covers
patellar instability.

The response often appears in early flexion rather than in full extension,
because that is where the patella is being carried across the lateral trochlear
facet. 3.12.4 covers trochlear function.

A patient with a deficient ligament and an intact confidence can have a lax
patella and no apprehension, which is why the test and the glide measure
different things. 4.15.4 covers it.

### How Good It Is

The apprehension test is the most specific clinical test for patellar
instability, because the response is hard to produce in a patient who has never
dislocated.

It is moderately sensitive and depends on the patient, so a negative result in a
guarded or stoical patient is uninformative rather than reassuring.

It is more useful than any measurement of patellar mobility for the question of
whether the instability is patellar, which is the question most often at stake.
4.15.4 covers the measurements.

It cannot grade the instability, which the number of episodes and the imaging do
between them. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The medial patellofemoral ligament is the principal restraint to lateral displacement near extension.
- Apprehension is a protective response to the dislocation mechanism being reproduced.
- The response often appears in early flexion rather than full extension.
- A lax patella with no apprehension is why this test and the glide differ.
- A negative result in a stoical patient is uninformative rather than reassuring.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level apprehension contributes to whether an instability complaint
is patellar, and that is a diagnosis rather than a grade.

A patient with apprehension, a history of dislocation and a maltracking patella
has patellar instability, and the operative decision rests on the imaging and
the number of episodes rather than on this test. 4.15.2 covers the sign.

A patient with a complaint of giving way, no apprehension and a normal patellar
examination is describing something else, and the cruciate and rotational
assessments are where it will be found. 4.14.3 covers one of them.

A patient with apprehension and no documented dislocation has a subluxation
pattern, which is a real diagnosis and a harder operative decision.

After surgery the loss of apprehension is a meaningful outcome and is more
closely related to the patient's experience than any laxity measurement.
4.20.6 covers that assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording discomfort as apprehension.

The second is pushing through apprehension, which hurts the patient and adds
nothing.

The third is reading a negative test as excluding instability.

The fourth is performing it without explaining it, which produces guarding that
is not apprehension.

The fifth is recording the label rather than what the patient did and said.

### Key Learning Points

- Apprehension contributes to whether an instability complaint is patellar.
- Giving way with no apprehension and a normal patellar examination is something else.
- Apprehension without documented dislocation is a subluxation pattern.
- The loss of apprehension after surgery is a meaningful outcome.
- Performing it without an explanation produces guarding that is not apprehension.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the apprehension test separates the two groups who present
with patellar complaints and need opposite treatments.

A patient with apprehension and recurrent dislocations has a structural problem,
and the assessment moves to the imaging and the bony factors. 4.15.8 covers
dysplasia.

A patient with anterior pain, no apprehension and a normal glide has a load
problem, and operating on the patella makes it worse. 4.15.9 covers
patellofemoral pain.

A patient who remains apprehensive after a successful reconstruction, with a
stable patella on examination, has a confidence problem, and that responds to
rehabilitation rather than to revision. 4.22.6 covers the score built for that
question.

A patient with apprehension on medial displacement after a reconstruction is
over constrained, which is under recognised and is the complication most often
attributed to something else. 4.20.6 covers the assessment.

In medicolegal work the recorded apprehension response is weak evidence, since
it is a behaviour, and the recorded dislocation history is what carries an
opinion. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The test separates two groups with patellar complaints who need opposite treatments.
- Anterior pain with no apprehension and a normal glide is a load problem.
- Apprehension after a successful reconstruction with a stable patella is a confidence problem.
- Apprehension on medial displacement means over constraint.
- Being a behaviour, it is weak medicolegal evidence; the dislocation history is not.
