# Popliteus Tendon

The hardest knee tendon to image completely, and a structure whose sheath is
easier to assess than the tendon.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The popliteus tendon runs from the lateral femoral condyle, through the joint, to
the popliteus muscle behind the tibia. 2.10.2 covers the popliteus tendon
anatomy.

Part of its course is intra-articular, which puts that part beyond a probe's
reach. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

It matters because the accessible part is the musculotendinous portion and the
sheath, and the part that matters to the corner is the part that is not
accessible. 2.10.4 covers the posterolateral corner.

### How It Is Acquired

Approach posterolaterally with the patient prone or in a lateral decubitus
position. 5.4.4 covers the posterior knee.

Find the tendon in its groove on the lateral condyle, which requires the beam to
angle under the lateral structures. 2.10.2 covers the popliteus tendon anatomy.

Follow it to the musculotendinous junction and the muscle behind the tibia. 2.10.2
covers the popliteus tendon anatomy.

Assess the sheath for fluid, which is often the only abnormality visible. 5.5.12
covers the bursa.

Accept that the intra-articular portion is not fully assessable and say so. 5.1.6
covers imaging pitfalls.

### What Normal Looks Like

A tendon of fibrillar texture within its groove on the lateral condyle. 2.10.2
covers the popliteus tendon anatomy.

A clean musculotendinous junction continuing into the muscle.

A sheath without fluid distension. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

No colour Doppler signal in the tendon. 5.2.11 covers Doppler imaging.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Fluid distending the popliteus sheath, which is often the first visible change.
5.5.12 covers the bursa.

Thickening or loss of the fibrillar pattern in the accessible portion. 5.5.10
covers the tendons.

An absent tendon in its groove, indicating avulsion or retraction. 5.10.2 covers
popliteus injuries.

A fragment at the femoral attachment, indicating a bony avulsion. 5.3.14 covers
avulsion injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Part of the tendon's course is intra-articular and beyond reach.
- The accessible part is the musculotendinous portion and the sheath.
- Sheath fluid is often the only visible abnormality.
- The part that matters to the corner is the part not accessible.
- An incomplete assessment should be reported as incomplete.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the tendon to name when asked which knee tendon ultrasound
cannot assess completely.

The expected points are the posterolateral approach, the groove, the
musculotendinous junction, the sheath, and the admission of incompleteness.

Say that the intra-articular portion is out of reach. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

Say that sheath fluid is a sensitive but non-specific sign. 5.5.12 covers the
bursa.

Say that the corner is assessed on magnetic resonance imaging. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that an intra-articular tendon is surrounded by bone and
joint, so the beam cannot reach it without crossing a cortical surface that
reflects nearly everything. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

That makes the sheath, which lies more superficially, the accessible proxy, and a
proxy is what sheath fluid is. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

The popliteus contributes to rotational restraint, so its injury matters
functionally more than its accessibility suggests. 3.10.6 covers the
posterolateral corner function.

A bony avulsion at the femoral attachment is visible and is the one direct finding
ultrasound gets here. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound assesses the popliteus least completely of the knee tendons and is
useful for its sheath.

Its best contribution is sheath fluid and a femoral bony avulsion, both of which
raise a question worth pursuing. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

It is limited absolutely by the intra-articular course, so a normal study does
not exclude a tendon injury. 5.10.2 covers popliteus injuries.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and the
comparison is the claim: this tendon is assessed less completely than any other
knee tendon, which is a comparison rather than a number.

### Key Learning Points

- An intra-articular tendon is unreachable without crossing a cortical surface.
- The sheath is the accessible proxy and sheath fluid is a proxy finding.
- The popliteus contributes to rotational restraint.
- A femoral bony avulsion is the one direct finding available.
- A normal study does not exclude a tendon injury.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this tendon is a magnetic resonance question and ultrasound
contributes only at the margin.

Sheath fluid in a patient with lateral symptoms raises a corner question worth
pursuing. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

A bony avulsion at the femoral attachment is a direct finding with a surgical
consequence. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

A corner injury assessment needs magnetic resonance imaging, because the
operative decision rests on structures ultrasound cannot see. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

A normal ultrasound here should be reported as an incomplete assessment rather
than as a normal tendon. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the tendon as normal when only part of it was
seen. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Sheath fluid is reported as a tendon tear. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

Anisotropy in the obliquely running tendon is read as pathology. 5.2.10 covers
anisotropy.

The approach is not attempted, so the structure is omitted from a lateral
examination. 5.4.3 covers the lateral knee.

A normal study is taken to exclude a corner injury. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral
corner injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- The tendon is reported normal when only part was seen.
- Sheath fluid is reported as a tear.
- Anisotropy in the oblique tendon is read as pathology.
- The approach is not attempted and the structure is omitted.
- A normal study is taken to exclude a corner injury.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the popliteus is the structure that shows most clearly why
knee ultrasound's limits should be stated in the report.

The tendon's intra-articular course is a physical barrier rather than a technique
problem, so no operator skill removes it, and a reader cannot tell from a normal
report how much was seen. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

A report that says the tendon was normal and a report that says the accessible
portion was normal describe the same examination and license different
conclusions. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The popliteus complex is central to posterolateral restraint and is one of the
structures whose injury most changes an operation, which makes the gap
consequential rather than academic. 3.10.6 covers the posterolateral corner
function.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows the whole course and still assesses the corner
inconsistently, so the structure is difficult for both modalities rather than
only for this one. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

The practice worth keeping is to report what was accessible and to name what was
not, which is the cheapest honesty available in this chapter. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The intra-articular course is a physical barrier, not a technique problem.
- A reader cannot tell from a normal report how much was seen.
- Two wordings describe the same examination and license different conclusions.
- The popliteus complex is central to posterolateral restraint.
- Report what was accessible and name what was not.
