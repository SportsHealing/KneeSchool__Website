# Lachman Test

The most reliable single manoeuvre in the knee. Why its position is the whole
reason, and what the endpoint adds.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The Lachman test measures anterior translation of the tibia on the femur with
the knee in slight flexion, and it tests the anterior cruciate directly.

It is the most reliable single examination manoeuvre in the knee, and the
reason is its position rather than anything about the hands.

At around twenty to thirty degrees of flexion the hamstrings are shortened and
less able to resist, and the ligament is the structure taking the load. 3.7.8
covers anterior stability.

### How It Is Performed

Position the knee in slight flexion, around twenty to thirty degrees, with the
patient supine and relaxed.

Stabilise the femur with one hand and grasp the proximal tibia with the other,
with your thumb on the tibial tubercle.

Apply a firm anterior force to the tibia and feel both the amount of
translation and the quality of the endpoint.

Compare with the other knee, which is what makes the amount interpretable.
4.1.2 covers the principle.

Record the translation relative to the other side and the endpoint character,
which is what makes the finding comparable later. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

Increased anterior translation compared with the other knee, with a soft or
absent endpoint, is a positive and indicates a ruptured ligament.

The endpoint is as important as the translation. Increased translation with a
firm endpoint suggests a partial injury or physiological laxity; a soft
endpoint suggests a complete rupture.

A hard endpoint with normal translation is a normal test.

A positive test does not say whether the patient is functionally unstable,
which is the history's job. 4.10.1 covers it.

A negative test in an acutely guarding knee is weak and should not be recorded
as excluding the injury. 4.10.2 covers the acute knee.

### Key Learning Points

- The most reliable single examination manoeuvre in the knee.
- Its position is the reason: slight flexion shortens the hamstrings so the ligament takes the load.
- Record the translation relative to the other side and the endpoint character.
- The endpoint distinguishes a complete rupture from a partial injury or physiological laxity.
- A positive test says nothing about whether the patient is functionally unstable.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected points are the flexion angle, the hand positions, the
stabilised femur, the endpoint, and the comparison.

The candidate should say why the angle matters, because that is the test's
whole advantage over the anterior drawer and is the most likely follow-up
question. 4.10.5 covers the drawer.

Say that a large thigh or a large examiner-to-patient size mismatch makes the
standard grip difficult, and name an alternative such as stabilising the femur
on your own thigh or using the prone or seated variant.

Say that you would record the grade with an endpoint rather than a grade
alone.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the ligament is the primary restraint to
anterior translation and supplies most of that restraint itself, with the
secondary restraints contributing in a flexion-dependent way. 3.7.2 covers
primary restraints.

At slight flexion the secondary restraints contribute least, which is why the
test isolates the ligament better than a test at ninety degrees does.

The endpoint reflects whether any fibres remain under tension, which is the
anatomical basis for distinguishing partial from complete.

Physiological laxity gives symmetrical increased translation with firm
endpoints bilaterally, which is why the comparison is diagnostic rather than
merely helpful. 4.23.3 covers hypermobile patients.

### How Good It Is

The Lachman test outperforms the anterior drawer substantially, and that is
the single most reproduced comparison in the knee examination literature.
3.17.1 covers the studies.

It is more sensitive than the pivot shift and less specific, so the two are
complementary rather than competing. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift.

It performs well in the chronic knee and less well acutely, because guarding
resists translation. 4.10.2 covers the acute knee.

Examiner experience affects it, mostly through the endpoint judgement rather
than the translation.

made.

### Key Learning Points

- At slight flexion the secondary restraints contribute least, which isolates the ligament.
- The endpoint reflects whether any fibres remain under tension.
- Symmetrical increased translation with firm endpoints is physiological laxity.
- The Lachman outperforms the anterior drawer substantially, which is a well reproduced comparison.
- It is more sensitive than the pivot shift and less specific, so the two are complementary.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the Lachman test establishes the diagnosis and contributes
little to the indication, which is the recurring structure of this chapter.

Its grade does not determine whether to reconstruct. A patient with a grade
three Lachman and no instability does not need surgery, and one with a grade
two and instability at work may.

Where the grade matters is in the comparison over time and in the comparison
with the examination under anaesthesia. 4.10.10 covers the latter.

The endpoint matters more than the grade for partial injuries, which are the
cases where the decision is genuinely difficult.

Instrumented laxity measurement adds precision and has not displaced the
manual test in practice, which is worth knowing when a reported figure appears
in a letter.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording a grade without an endpoint, which makes the
finding almost uninterpretable by the next clinician.

The second is failing to compare sides, which converts a diagnostic test into
a guess, particularly in a hypermobile patient.

The third is performing it with an unstabilised femur, where the whole leg
moves and the translation is apparent rather than real.

And treating a positive Lachman as the indication, which conflates diagnosis
with decision and is the error this chapter returns to.

### Key Learning Points

- The test establishes the diagnosis and contributes little to the indication.
- The grade does not determine whether to reconstruct.
- The endpoint matters more than the grade in partial injuries, where decisions are hardest.
- A grade recorded without an endpoint is almost uninterpretable later.
- An unstabilised femur makes the translation apparent rather than real.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

After reconstruction the Lachman test is the main clinical measure of graft
integrity, and its interpretation changes.

A graft is expected to give a firmer endpoint than a deficient knee and a
slightly softer one than a normal knee, so a small increase in translation
with a firm endpoint is a satisfactory result rather than a failure. 4.10.11
covers post-reconstruction assessment.

A soft endpoint after reconstruction indicates graft failure and is the
finding that matters.

Progressive increase in translation across visits indicates a stretching
graft, which is a trajectory rather than a single finding and requires the
earlier examinations to have been recorded properly. 4.1.8 covers the record.

A normal Lachman in a patient complaining of instability points at rotation
rather than translation, which is this chapter's central gap. 4.10.9 covers
it.

### Key Learning Points

- A small increase in translation with a firm endpoint is a satisfactory graft result.
- A soft endpoint after reconstruction indicates graft failure.
- Progressive increase across visits indicates a stretching graft, which needs earlier records.
- A normal Lachman with instability points at rotation rather than translation.
- That gap between translation and rotation is this chapter's central problem.
