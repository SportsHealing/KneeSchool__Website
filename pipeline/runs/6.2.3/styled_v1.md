# Hyperextension Injury

The one acute pattern in this chapter with a limb question in it, and the limb
question is answered first.

## For Young Learners

### Causes and Risk Factors

Sometimes the knee is forced backwards past straight. A foot catches, a leg is
kicked while planted, or a landing goes wrong. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of
injury.

The knee is not built to bend that way, so the structures at the front and back
are both loaded at once. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### How It Presents

The knee swells as with other ACL injuries, and it often hurts at the back as
well as inside. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

This pattern is treated as more serious than a twist, because more can be
damaged, including things that are not ligaments at all.

### Key Learning Points

- Hyperextension means the knee is forced backwards past straight.
- The knee is not built to move that way.
- Structures at the front and back are loaded together.
- More can be damaged than in a twisting injury.
- It is treated more urgently for that reason.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Causes and Risk Factors

Hyperextension loads the cruciate ligaments and the structures at the back of
the knee together. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

At low energy it can produce an isolated cruciate injury. At higher energy, such
as a fall from height or a vehicle collision, it can injure several structures
and the nerves and blood vessels behind the knee. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

### How It Presents

Swelling within hours is the usual sign, as in any acute cruciate injury. 6.2.5
covers acute haemarthrosis.

What distinguishes this pattern is the possibility of problems in the foot and
lower leg: numbness, weakness, coldness, or a change in colour. 6.1.5 covers the
mechanisms of injury.

Those are the symptoms to report immediately rather than at a follow up
appointment, because they concern the limb and not the knee. 6.2.4 covers
combined injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Hyperextension loads the front and back of the knee together.
- Low energy can produce an isolated cruciate injury.
- Higher energy can injure nerves and blood vessels behind the knee.
- Numbness, weakness, coldness or colour change in the foot matter urgently.
- Those are limb symptoms rather than knee symptoms.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why is this pattern treated differently?
Because the structures behind the knee, including the nerve and the artery, lie
in the path of the force. Most hyperextension injuries do not damage them, and
the consequences if they are damaged are serious enough that they are checked
every time.

**Q.** What should I watch for at home?
A foot that becomes numb, weak, cold or changes colour, or pain that is out of
proportion and increasing. Any of those is a reason to be seen straight away
rather than to wait.

**Q.** Does the knee being hyperextended mean the ligament definitely tore?
No. The knee can be forced backwards and recover without a tear. The mechanism
raises the suspicion; the examination and the scan answer it.

**Q.** Will this need surgery sooner than other ACL injuries?
Not necessarily for the cruciate itself. If other structures are involved, the
timing is set by them, and in a high energy injury the limb is dealt with before
any ligament is.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Causes and Risk Factors

Hyperextension loads the cruciates and the posterior capsule together, and at
sufficient energy the posterolateral corner and the neurovascular structures.
3.7.14 covers the ACL injury mechanisms.

The popliteal artery is tethered above and below the knee, which is why a
hyperextension or dislocation mechanism threatens it. 6.2.4 covers combined
injuries.

A knee dislocation may have reduced spontaneously before arrival, so the absence
of a dislocated knee on examination does not exclude the mechanism. 6.2.2 covers
contact injury.

### How It Presents

Haemarthrosis is usual, as elsewhere in this chapter. 6.2.5 covers acute
haemarthrosis.

Multi directional laxity in the acute setting is the finding that reframes the
injury entirely. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Anterior skin bruising or abrasion over the proximal tibia records the direction
of force. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

### Examination

Examine the limb before the knee: pulses, capillary refill, sensation and motor
function in the common peroneal distribution. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Then examine the ligaments in every plane rather than for the cruciate alone.
4.10.9 covers the rotational instability.

Document the vascular examination with a time, because serial comparison is what
detects an evolving injury. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

### Key Learning Points

- Hyperextension loads the cruciates and posterior capsule together.
- The popliteal artery is tethered above and below the knee.
- A dislocation may have reduced before arrival.
- Examine the limb before the knee.
- Document the vascular examination with a time.

## MRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

In a viva a hyperextension mechanism should change the shape of the answer
immediately.

The expected points are the loading of front and back together, the energy
dependence, the possibility of a reduced dislocation, the neurovascular risk,
and the serial examination. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Say that a spontaneously reduced knee dislocation is the diagnosis most often
missed here. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

Say that a normal pulse at presentation does not exclude an arterial injury,
which is why examination is repeated rather than recorded once. 6.1.5 covers the
mechanisms of injury.

### Examination

The neurovascular examination is the examination, and the ligament assessment
follows it. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Multi directional laxity found under anaesthesia converts the problem into a
multi ligament reconstruction. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

### Investigations and Imaging

Imaging of the knee's ligaments is secondary to imaging the limb's circulation
where there is any doubt about it. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL injury.

The bone bruise pattern in hyperextension differs from the pivot pattern, with
anterior tibial and anterior femoral contact rather than the lateral pair. 6.4.1
covers bone bruising.

### Key Learning Points

- A hyperextension mechanism changes the shape of the answer.
- A spontaneously reduced dislocation is the missed diagnosis here.
- A normal pulse at presentation does not exclude arterial injury.
- The neurovascular examination comes before the ligaments.
- The bruise pattern is anterior rather than the lateral pivot pair.

## FRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

At examination level this mechanism is a multi ligament knee question with a
vascular stem, and the candidate is expected to reach the vascular part first.

The sequence is limb assessment, reduction and stabilisation if needed, then
ligament assessment, then a reconstruction plan. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

An intimal arterial injury can declare itself hours after a normal initial
examination, which is the argument for serial assessment and for a low threshold
to image the vessels. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

The common peroneal nerve is the commonest neurological casualty and the one
with the worst recovery, and a deficit found later cannot be dated. 6.4.4 covers
ALL injuries.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is which patients need formal vascular imaging rather
than serial examination with pressure measurement. 6.2.4 covers combined
injuries.

The second is the timing of ligament reconstruction after a vascular repair,
where the two goals pull against each other. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The third is whether the posterolateral structures should be repaired acutely or
reconstructed later, which is the oldest argument in the multi ligament knee.
6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- This is a multi ligament question with a vascular stem.
- The sequence is limb, reduction, ligaments, plan.
- An intimal injury can declare itself after a normal examination.
- The common peroneal nerve recovers worst and a late deficit cannot be dated.
- Acute repair against delayed reconstruction of the corner is unsettled.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this is the pattern where a knee result and a limb result
are different things, and the second outranks the first.

A technically excellent multi ligament reconstruction in a limb with a
neurological deficit delivers a stable knee the patient cannot use well, and the
deficit was present before anybody operated. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

Stiffness is the dominant knee complication, because the injury is extensive,
the operation is long and immobilisation is often necessary for other reasons.
6.5.1 covers instability.

Counselling therefore has to separate what the operation can change from what
the injury already decided, and patients hear those as one thing unless they are
told otherwise. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The useful discipline is to record the limb examination thoroughly on day one,
because every later conversation refers back to it and nothing else can. 6.2.4
covers combined injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- A knee result and a limb result are different things.
- A stable knee on a deficient limb is a poor outcome.
- Stiffness dominates the knee complications.
- Counselling separates what surgery changes from what the injury decided.
- The day one limb examination is what every later conversation refers to.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant judgement here is about thresholds rather than technique, and
both available errors are expensive.

Imaging every hyperextension knee's vessels overtreats a population in which
most are intact. Relying on serial examination risks the injury that declares
late, and that one costs a limb. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Departments resolve this with a protocol, and the honest position is that the
protocol encodes a risk appetite rather than a finding, because the evidence
does not separate the strategies cleanly. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

For the individual the five things still decide. Their age and physiology, which
bear on vascular reserve as much as on healing. Their medical comorbidities,
because established vascular disease changes everything on this page. The
demands they put on the knee, which matter less here than anywhere else in the
chapter. Their psychological state, facing a long and uncertain recovery. And
their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which are usually about the limb
rather than the ligament.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

### Key Learning Points

- The judgement is about thresholds rather than technique.
- Imaging everybody overtreats; serial examination risks the late declaration.
- A protocol encodes a risk appetite rather than a finding.
- Established vascular disease changes everything on this page.
- The patient's concerns here are usually about the limb, not the ligament.
