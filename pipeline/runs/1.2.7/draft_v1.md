# Nerves and Blood Supply

The artery and nerves behind the knee, and why an injury there is treated as
an emergency.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

Behind your knee is a soft hollow. Feel gently and you may notice a pulse.
That is the popliteal artery, the main blood supply to everything below the
knee.

Two large nerves pass nearby. One continues down the back of the calf. The
other winds around the outside of the shin bone, just below the knee, where it
lies very close to the surface.

If you have ever banged the outside of your knee and felt a strange electric
tingle down to your foot, that was that nerve.

### Function

The artery carries blood to the lower leg and foot. The nerves carry signals
both ways: instructions out to the muscles, and sensation back from the skin.

Because the artery is tethered at the back of the knee, a severe injury can
damage it, and the lower leg can lose its blood supply quickly. That is why a
knee that looks badly out of shape after an accident is treated as an
emergency rather than something to watch.

### Key Learning Points

- The popliteal artery runs behind the knee and supplies the whole lower leg.
- Two large nerves pass close by, one of them very near the surface on the outer side.
- A severe knee injury can damage the artery, and that is an emergency.
- Sensation and muscle control below the knee travel through these nerves.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

The popliteal artery is the continuation of the femoral artery as it passes
behind the knee. It gives off a network of smaller branches, the geniculate
arteries, which supply the joint itself, before dividing below the knee into
the vessels that supply the lower leg and foot.

The sciatic nerve divides above the knee into the tibial nerve, which
continues down the back of the calf, and the common fibular nerve, which winds
around the neck of the fibula on the outer side of the knee, where it lies
just under the skin.

### Function

The geniculate network supplies the joint capsule, the lining, the ligaments
and the outer parts of the menisci. The limited reach of these vessels into
the cartilage and the inner meniscus explains why those tissues heal so
poorly.

The nerves carry movement signals to the lower leg muscles and sensation back
from the skin. They also supply the joint itself, which is how you know where
your knee is without looking.

### Clinical Relevance

The popliteal artery is relatively tethered behind the knee, so a severe
injury such as a dislocation can tear or block it. The lower leg tolerates
loss of blood supply for only a few hours, which is why such injuries are
assessed urgently and why pulses and foot colour are always checked.

The common fibular nerve lies very superficially at the outer knee, so it is
vulnerable to direct blows, to tight casts and to prolonged pressure. Damage
causes difficulty lifting the foot, known as foot drop, and numbness over the
top of the foot.

Surgical neurovascular anatomy and the management of these injuries are
covered elsewhere.

### Key Learning Points

- The popliteal artery supplies the whole lower leg and is tethered behind the knee.
- Geniculate branches supply the joint, its capsule, ligaments and outer menisci.
- The common fibular nerve is superficial at the outer knee and easily injured.
- Fibular nerve injury causes foot drop and numbness on the top of the foot.
- Suspected vascular injury after a knee dislocation is a surgical emergency.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why do they always check the pulses in my foot?
To confirm the artery behind the knee is intact. Loss of blood supply there
threatens the whole lower leg and needs treating within hours.

**Q.** What is foot drop?
Weakness lifting the front of the foot, caused by injury to the nerve that
winds around the outer side of the knee. It often recovers, and the time
course varies.

**Q.** Why does my knee feel numb after surgery?
Small skin nerves cross the front of the knee and are sometimes cut during an
incision. A patch of numbness is common and usually shrinks over months.

**Q.** Why does cartilage heal badly if the knee has a good blood supply?
The blood supply reaches the capsule, lining and outer parts of the meniscus,
but barely penetrates cartilage or the inner meniscus. Healing follows the
blood supply.

Seek emergency care if your lower leg or foot becomes cold, pale, numb or weak
after a knee injury, or if you cannot lift your foot.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The femoral artery becomes the popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus and
runs through the popliteal fossa, dividing at the lower border of popliteus
into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries. It gives five geniculate
branches, superior medial and lateral, middle, and inferior medial and
lateral, which form the periarticular anastomosis.

The sciatic nerve divides into tibial and common fibular components, usually
in the distal thigh. The tibial nerve passes vertically through the fossa
superficial to the vessels. The common fibular nerve follows the medial border
of biceps femoris and winds around the fibular neck, where it is subcutaneous.

Contents of the popliteal fossa from superficial to deep are nerve, vein, then
artery, which is worth remembering for approaches to the region.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The middle geniculate artery pierces the posterior capsule to supply the
cruciate ligaments. The remaining geniculate branches supply the capsule,
synovium, collaterals and the peripheral menisci. Penetration into articular
cartilage and the inner meniscal zones is negligible, which determines their
healing capacity.

Articular innervation follows Hilton's law, arising from the nerves supplying
the muscles acting on the joint: branches of the femoral, obturator, tibial
and common fibular nerves. Mechanoreceptors within capsule and ligament
provide proprioceptive afferents.

### Clinical Relevance

The popliteal artery is relatively fixed proximally and distally, so knee
dislocation carries a recognised risk of intimal tear or complete disruption.
Vascular assessment is mandatory, pulses alone are insufficient to exclude
injury, and the limb tolerates warm ischaemia for only a few hours.

The subcutaneous course of the common fibular nerve at the fibular neck makes
it the most frequently injured peripheral nerve around the knee, whether from
direct trauma, traction in varus injury, or external compression. The
resulting deficit is weakness of ankle dorsiflexion and eversion with sensory
loss over the dorsum of the foot.

The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the anterior knee and
is commonly injured during anterior incisions, producing a numb patch lateral
to the scar.

Surgical approaches and the management of neurovascular injury are covered in
the anatomy and surgery sections.

### Key Learning Points

- Popliteal fossa contents run nerve, vein, artery from superficial to deep.
- Five geniculate branches form the periarticular anastomosis; the middle supplies the cruciates.
- Vascular penetration into cartilage and inner meniscus is negligible, limiting healing.
- Knee dislocation carries a real risk of popliteal arterial injury; pulses do not exclude it.
- The common fibular nerve at the fibular neck is the most commonly injured nerve here.
