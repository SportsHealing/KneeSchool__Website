# Posterior Stability

Resistance to the tibia going backwards. The ligament holds nearly all of it,
which is why losing it produces a sign you can see rather than feel.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Stability in a direction means the joint does not travel too far when pushed
that way.

If one structure holds almost all of a direction's restraint, losing it
produces a large and obvious change.

Where the loss also changes the resting position, the change becomes visible
rather than only measurable.

### At the Knee

Posterior stability is resistance to the tibia translating backwards relative
to the femur. 3.2.8 covers the motion.

The posterior cruciate holds nearly all of it in flexion. Conventional
teaching gives it roughly 95 per cent at 90 degrees. 3.8.1 covers the
functions.

Losing it lets the tibia sag back under gravity when the knee is flexed, which
produces a visible loss of the normal step off at the front of the knee.

Conventional teaching grades the loss by that step off: 0 to 5 millimetres, 5
to 10, and more than 10, which correspond to grades one to three.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, since the ligament's restraint rises with flexion.

Quadriceps activity, which pulls the tibia forward and reduces the sag.

Posterolateral corner integrity, which adds rotational laxity to the picture.
2.10.4 covers the corner.

Partial healing, which reduces the measured laxity over months.

### Clinical Relevance

The step off is examined before any drawer, because the drawer is measured
from it. 3.8.6 covers the drawer.

The quadriceps active test uses the muscle to pull the sagged tibia forward,
which demonstrates the sag mechanically. Section 4 covers the tests.

A grade three injury suggests the corner is involved too, rather than a worse
isolated injury.

Patients report difficulty with descending rather than with turning, which
follows from the direction. 3.8.7 covers gait.

### Key Learning Points

- Posterior stability is resistance to the tibia translating backwards.
- Conventional teaching gives the posterior cruciate roughly 95 per cent at 90 degrees.
- Losing it lets the tibia sag back under gravity in flexion.
- Conventional teaching grades it by step off: 0 to 5, 5 to 10, and more than 10 millimetres.
- A grade three injury suggests corner involvement rather than a worse isolated injury.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Grading by step off is grading a position, not an excursion, which makes it
unusual among laxity grades. A grade is a statement about where the tibia
sits.

That is why the measurement is more reproducible radiographically than by
hand: a radiograph records a position and a hand records a judgement about
one.

The distinction matters because the starting position is the quantity most
affected by muscle activity and limb support.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the normal anterior step off of the medial tibial
plateau relative to the medial femoral condyle as around 10 millimetres at 90
degrees of flexion.

Grade one is a reduced but present step off, grade two is a flush plateau, and
grade three is a plateau posterior to the condyle.

Posterior restraint is shared differently near extension, where the posterior
capsule and the corner contribute more and the cruciate less.

The meniscofemoral ligaments contribute independently, which is why a divided
posterior cruciate does not produce unlimited translation. 2.6.3 covers them.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes both the restraint and the gravitational sag.

Muscle activity, which moves the starting position.

Corner integrity, which adds external rotation at 30 degrees.

Healing over time, which reduces the measured laxity.

### How It Is Measured

Stress radiography with a standardised posterior load measures the translation
against bony landmarks and is the reference clinical measurement.

Clinical grading by step off is reproducible between experienced examiners and
depends on limb support and relaxation.

Instrumented arthrometry is less reliable posteriorly than anteriorly, because
the start position is uncertain.

Examination under anaesthetic removes the quadriceps contribution and gives
the structural figure.

### Clinical Relevance

Grade by step off and record the flexion angle and the limb support used.

Use stress radiography where the grade decides the treatment.

Expect an unreliable arthrometer reading posteriorly and prefer radiography.

Suspect corner involvement in a grade three, and test rotation at 30 degrees.

### Key Learning Points

- Grading by step off grades a position, not an excursion.
- A radiograph records a position; a hand records a judgement about one.
- Conventional teaching gives the normal step off as around 10 millimetres at 90 degrees.
- Grade two is a flush plateau; grade three is a plateau posterior to the condyle.
- The meniscofemoral ligaments prevent unlimited translation after division.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the grade as the decision variable and the method of obtaining it as the
weak point. Stress radiography is the measurement that settles it and is
underused.

Conventional teaching holds that a side to side difference above roughly 12
millimetres on stress radiography suggests a combined rather than an isolated
injury, which converts a grade into an anatomical inference.

The grade also drives the non operative decision, because partial healing is
more likely in a lower grade injury. Section 6 covers management.

### What Changes It

Corner and collateral integrity, which raise the grade beyond what the
cruciate alone gives.

Alignment, which loads a deficient or reconstructed knee. 3.13 covers
alignment.

Time and healing, which lower the measured grade.

Quadriceps capacity, which is modifiable and substitutes dynamically.

### How It Is Measured

Stress radiography with a standardised technique is the reference and gives a
side to side difference in millimetres.

Examination under anaesthetic is the cleanest structural assessment and is
made in a knee that will swell.

Serial measurement captures healing and is more informative than a single
reading.

No measurement separates a healed long ligament from a partly reconstructed
one in a patient.

### Clinical Relevance

Obtain stress radiography rather than grading by hand where the treatment
turns on it.

Read a large side to side difference as suggesting a combined injury.

Reassess serially before committing to reconstruction.

Build quadriceps capacity, which substitutes for the restraint.

### Key Learning Points

- The grade is the decision variable and the method of getting it is the weak point.
- Conventional teaching reads above roughly 12 millimetres as suggesting a combined injury.
- A grade converts into an anatomical inference.
- Partial healing is more likely in a lower grade injury.
- Serial measurement is more informative than a single reading.
