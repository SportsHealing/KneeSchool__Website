# KOOS

A knee outcome questionnaire reported as separate domains rather than as one
number.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The KOOS asks about pain, symptoms, daily activity, sport and recreation, and
quality of life, and reports each as its own subscale. 4.22.1 covers the IKDC.

The examination's interest is that the subscales separate what a single score
would blend, so a patient's problem can be located within the report. 4.18.6
covers functional limitation.

It matters because two patients with the same overall burden can have it in
different domains, and the treatment differs. 4.2.10 covers severity.

### How It Is Performed

Give the questionnaire to the patient to complete themselves, before the
consultation.

Explain the recall period, since the questions ask about a defined recent
period.

Check completeness for each subscale, because the subscales are scored
separately. 4.1.8 covers examination documentation.

Score each subscale with the published method rather than combining them.

Record all the subscales rather than selecting one, since the pattern is the
information.

Record the date, because the comparison over time is the use.

Record the examination separately. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

### What a Positive Means

A profile across the subscales is the result, and the shape of the profile is
read rather than a single value.

A low pain subscale with preserved activity means the problem is symptomatic
rather than functional. 4.2.1 covers pain.

A low sport and recreation subscale with preserved daily activity identifies a
patient limited only at higher demand. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

A low quality of life subscale with modest symptom scores identifies the patient
for whom the knee's effect exceeds its symptoms. 4.22.8 covers the use of these
scores.

An incomplete subscale is unscored rather than imputed.

No item count, score range or validation figure appears on this page.
Those are properties of a published instrument and stating one from memory
is the same class of claim as an invented citation, so the page describes
what the instrument does and a reviewer with the questionnaire can add the

### Key Learning Points

- The instrument reports separate subscales rather than one number.
- The shape of the profile is the result.
- Pain and function can be affected independently.
- A sport subscale deficit identifies limitation only at higher demand.
- An incomplete subscale is unscored rather than imputed.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the instrument to name when the question asks about domains
rather than a single score.

The expected points are the self completion, the timing, the recall period, the
per subscale completeness, the separate scoring, the whole profile, the date and
the separate examination record.

Say that the subscales are reported separately, and give the reason. 4.22.1
covers the IKDC.

Say that you would record the whole profile rather than one subscale. 4.1.8
covers examination documentation.

Say that the questionnaire and the examination are both recorded. 4.1.4 covers
the structured approach.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a knee problem affects symptoms, function and
life differently, so collapsing them into one number discards the pattern.
4.18.6 covers functional limitation.

A subscale structure allows a treatment to be judged against the domain it was
aimed at, which a single score does not. 4.22.8 covers the use of these scores.

The sport subscale extends the measurable range upwards, which reduces the
ceiling problem in active patients. 4.22.4 covers the Tegner scale.

A quality of life domain captures the part of the burden that neither the
symptoms nor the function describe. 4.2.10 covers severity.

### How Good It Is

The instrument is reproducible for the same patient over time and more
informative than a single score.

Its best contribution is locating the burden within a domain, which directs both
the treatment and its evaluation. 4.18.6 covers functional limitation.

It is longer than a single score instrument, which reduces completion in
practice.

No examination finding corresponds to a subscale, so the two records sit side by
side. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

### Key Learning Points

- A knee problem affects symptoms, function and life differently.
- A subscale structure lets a treatment be judged against its target domain.
- The sport subscale reduces the ceiling problem in active patients.
- A quality of life domain captures burden the other domains miss.
- Length reduces completion in practice.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the profile supports a more specific conversation than a single
score does.

A patient whose burden sits in the quality of life domain is a different
treatment proposition from one whose burden is pain, and the profile separates
them. 4.22.8 covers the use of these scores.

A sport subscale deficit in an athlete with a normal daily activity score
identifies the demand level at which the knee fails. 4.21.7 covers return to
sport testing.

A profile that does not change after treatment while one subscale does is a more
precise result than an unchanged total. 4.20.1 covers the reconstruction
assessment.

The instrument's length is a practical constraint on using it routinely, which is
a service question rather than a clinical one. 4.1.8 covers examination
documentation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting one subscale and discarding the rest. 4.18.6
covers functional limitation.

Subscales are averaged into a single number, which undoes the instrument's
design. 4.22.1 covers the IKDC.

An incomplete questionnaire is scored anyway.

Subscale values are compared with another instrument's total. 4.22.3 covers the
Lysholm score.

A profile is interpreted without a baseline. 4.22.8 covers the use of these
scores.

### Key Learning Points

- Reporting one subscale and discarding the rest is the commonest error.
- Averaging the subscales undoes the design.
- An incomplete questionnaire is scored anyway.
- Subscale values are not comparable with another instrument's total.
- A profile without a baseline cannot be interpreted.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the subscale structure is the instrument's strength and the
source of its harder problems.

Five subscales mean five comparisons, which raises the chance that one moves by
itself, and interpreting that movement needs care the clinical use rarely gives
it. 4.22.8 covers the use of these scores.

The domains are not independent, since pain drives function and function drives
quality of life, so a change in one appears in the others. 4.18.6 covers
functional limitation.

Each subscale has its own ceiling and floor, and they truncate different
populations. 4.22.4 covers the Tegner scale.

Importance thresholds differ by subscale and are published quantities this
repository does not state. 4.2.10 covers severity.

A profile describes a state and does not explain it, so the examination remains
the instrument that localises. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

### Key Learning Points

- Five subscales mean five comparisons, which needs interpretive care.
- The domains are not independent of each other.
- Each subscale has its own ceiling and floor.
- Importance thresholds differ by subscale and are not stated here.
- A profile describes a state without explaining it.
