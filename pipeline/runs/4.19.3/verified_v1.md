# Discoid Meniscus

A meniscus that formed with the wrong shape, which usually declares itself in
childhood and sometimes not at all.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A discoid meniscus is almost always lateral, and it presents in a child as a
snapping knee, a block to full extension, or lateral joint line pain. 4.9.10
covers meniscal deficiency.

The examination is deciding whether there is a mechanical abnormality on the
lateral side and whether the knee reaches full extension. 4.5.4 covers the
flexion deformity.

Many discoid menisci never cause symptoms, so the examination is looking for
the consequences rather than for the shape. 2.6.1 covers the lateral meniscus
anatomy.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history: the age, whether the knee snaps audibly, whether it has ever
locked, and whether the child can straighten it fully. 4.2.7 covers mechanical
symptoms.

Ask the parent as well as the child, since a snapping knee is often noticed by
an observer first.

Watch the child walk and squat, because the snap frequently appears during
movement rather than on testing. 4.4.10 covers the squat.

Measure the extension carefully, comparing with the other side, since a small
deficit is the common finding. 4.7.2 covers passive motion.

Take the knee through the range with a hand on the lateral joint line, feeling
for a palpable clunk. 4.6.1 covers systematic palpation.

Palpate the lateral joint line for tenderness. 4.6.4 covers joint line
tenderness.

Perform the meniscal provocation tests, accepting that they are harder to
interpret in children. 4.9.3 covers the McMurray test.

### What a Positive Means

A palpable or audible clunk on the lateral side through the range, with or
without an extension deficit, is the characteristic finding.

An extension deficit in a child with a snapping knee suggests an unstable
discoid meniscus rather than a simple variant. 4.5.4 covers the flexion
deformity.

Lateral joint line tenderness with a snap suggests a tear within the discoid
meniscus, which changes the management. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

A snap without pain, without a deficit and without an effusion can be a
symptomless variant, and the examination's conclusion is that no intervention
follows from it. 2.6.1 covers the lateral meniscus anatomy.

Bilateral snapping raises a bilateral variant, which is common. 4.1.4 covers
the structured approach.

### Key Learning Points

- A discoid meniscus is almost always lateral.
- The examination looks for the consequences rather than for the shape.
- A snap felt on the lateral side through the range is the characteristic finding.
- An extension deficit suggests instability rather than a simple variant.
- A painless snap with a full range may need no intervention.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the snapping knee in a child and the expected answer names
the lateral side and the extension deficit early.

The expected points are the age, the parental history, the observed gait and
squat, the extension measurement, the palpated range, the lateral joint line,
the meniscal tests and the other knee.

Say that you would examine both knees, since the variant is often bilateral.
4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Say that you would measure extension against the other side rather than against
a textbook value. 4.7.2 covers passive motion.

Say that the examination cannot confirm the diagnosis and that imaging does.
4.1.6 covers that relationship.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a discoid meniscus covers more of the lateral
plateau than a normal meniscus, so it is caught between the condyle and the
plateau during movement, which produces the snap. 3.5.3 covers load
distribution.

An unstable discoid meniscus displaces, which is why it blocks extension and
why the deficit matters more than the snap.

The abnormal shape also makes it thicker and more prone to tearing within its
substance, which is why pain accompanies the mechanical findings. 4.9.10 covers
meniscal deficiency.

A symptomless variant transmits load differently and is still a variant rather
than a lesion, which is why the examination's negative findings matter. 2.6.1
covers the lateral meniscus anatomy.

### How Good It Is

The examination detects the mechanical consequences well when they are present
and says nothing about the shape.

Its best contribution is the extension deficit and the palpable clunk, both of
which are specific in a child of this age. 4.5.4 covers the flexion deformity.

It cannot grade the variant or detect a tear within it.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows the shape and the tear, so the examination
raises the question and the imaging answers it. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- A discoid meniscus covers more of the plateau and is caught during movement.
- An unstable discoid meniscus displaces and blocks extension.
- The abnormal shape makes the meniscus prone to tearing within its substance.
- The examination detects consequences and not shape.
- A specific extension deficit and a palpable clunk are its best contributions.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the question is whether the discoid meniscus is stable,
unstable or torn, and the examination contributes to the first two.

A stable symptomless variant needs no treatment, and the examination is what
establishes that there are no consequences. 2.6.1 covers the lateral meniscus
anatomy.

An unstable variant with an extension deficit is a different decision, and the
deficit is clinical. 4.5.4 covers the flexion deformity.

A torn discoid meniscus with lateral pain and an effusion moves towards
intervention, and the examination raises it. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

Lateral compartment status in a knee with a long standing discoid meniscus is
examined, because the altered load transmission has consequences over time.
4.18.4 covers the valgus worn knee.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is attributing a child's knee snap to a discoid meniscus
when a patellar or a tendon snap produces the same sound. 4.15.10 covers
patellofemoral arthritis.

A small extension deficit is missed when the knee is not compared with the
other side. 4.7.2 covers passive motion.

Meniscal provocation tests are less interpretable in children, and a positive
result is given more weight than it deserves. 4.9.3 covers the McMurray test.

A symptomless variant found on imaging is treated as the cause of unrelated
pain, and the examination is the check against that. 2.6.1 covers the lateral
meniscus anatomy.

A bilateral finding is read as normal because it is symmetrical. 4.1.4 covers
the structured approach.

### Key Learning Points

- A patellar or tendon snap mimics a meniscal one.
- A small extension deficit is missed without a side to side comparison.
- Meniscal provocation tests are less interpretable in children.
- A symptomless variant on imaging is not automatically the cause of pain.
- A symmetrical bilateral finding is not thereby normal.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the difficulty is deciding whether a known discoid meniscus
is the source of the symptoms, and the examination is the main instrument.

The variant is common enough and often symptomless, so its presence on imaging
does not establish causation and the clinical findings have to do that work.
2.6.1 covers the lateral meniscus anatomy.

An extension deficit, a clunk and lateral tenderness together make a convincing
case, and any one of them alone does not. 4.5.4 covers the flexion deformity.

A previously treated discoid meniscus examines differently, and the baseline
for the examination is the post operative knee rather than normal. 4.20.2
covers the repaired meniscus.

Long term lateral compartment change after a discoid meniscus produces
degenerative findings that overlie the mechanical ones. 4.18.4 covers the
valgus worn knee.

The examination cannot assess the peripheral attachment, which is the finding
that determines whether the meniscus is stable. 4.9.10 covers meniscal
deficiency.

### Key Learning Points

- Presence on imaging does not establish causation.
- The three findings together make the case, and one alone does not.
- A treated discoid meniscus has a post operative baseline, not a normal one.
- Long term lateral compartment change overlies the mechanical findings.
- The peripheral attachment, which determines stability, is not assessable clinically.
