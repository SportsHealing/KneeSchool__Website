# Standard Protocol

The routine knee study, which is a set of choices made before the patient arrives
and is invisible on the images.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A standard knee protocol is a fixed set of sequences and planes acquired for any
routine knee question. 5.2.12 covers the magnetic resonance basic principles.

It combines a detail weighting for the menisci and ligaments with a fluid
sensitive weighting for marrow and oedema, in three planes. 5.2.15 covers proton
density imaging.

It matters because the protocol decides what the report can say, and the report
does not say what the protocol was. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### How It Is Acquired

Acquire an intermediate weighted sequence for internal meniscal and ligament
detail. 5.2.15 covers proton density imaging.

Acquire a fat suppressed fluid sensitive sequence for marrow and oedema. 5.2.16
covers fat suppression.

Acquire in all three planes, because a linear abnormality has to be confirmed on
more than one. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

Use a dedicated knee coil and position the knee in slight flexion with external
rotation, which brings the cruciate into plane. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Keep slice thickness small enough that a meniscus is not averaged with the fat
beside it. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

### What Normal Looks Like

Dark menisci, ligaments and tendons with the expected internal detail. 5.5.8
covers the menisci.

Bright marrow fat on the unsuppressed sequences and dark marrow on the suppressed
ones. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

A thin bright cartilage layer over dark subchondral bone. 5.5.7 covers the
cartilage.

A small amount of physiological joint fluid. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Fluid signal within a normally dark structure. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

Marrow signal on the fat suppressed sequence. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Cartilage thinning or a surface defect. 5.5.7 covers the cartilage.

An effusion beyond the physiological amount. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

### Key Learning Points

- The protocol is a fixed set of sequences and planes.
- It pairs a detail weighting with a fluid sensitive one.
- Three planes are acquired so a linear finding can be confirmed.
- Slight flexion with external rotation brings the cruciate into plane.
- The protocol decides what the report can say and is not stated in it.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer names the two weightings and the three planes
before describing any finding.

The expected points are the intermediate weighting, the fat suppressed fluid
sequence, the three planes, the coil and position, and the slice thickness.

Say that the protocol differs between departments. 5.20.8 covers common reporting
errors.

Say that a question outside the routine set needs a different protocol requested
by name. 5.5.3 covers the post-operative protocol.

Say that the request should state the clinical question. 5.20.1 covers structured
reporting.

### What Normal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that no single weighting shows everything, because
contrast comes from the sequence rather than from the tissue, so a protocol is a
deliberate combination. 5.2.12 covers the magnetic resonance basic principles.

The intermediate weighting gives internal structure and the fat suppressed
sequence gives conspicuity, which is why the two are read together rather than
either alone. 5.2.14 covers T2 imaging.

Three planes exist because a partial volume effect and an oblique course both
produce a linear appearance in one plane, so confirmation is structural rather
than a courtesy. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Position matters because the cruciate runs obliquely, and a knee rotated
differently presents it differently. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

### How Good It Is

A standard protocol answers the common knee questions well and the uncommon ones
only by accident.

Its best contribution is that one study addresses meniscal, ligamentous, chondral
and marrow questions together. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

It is limited for cartilage detail, for metal, for the paediatric physis and for
anything needing contrast, each of which has its own protocol. 5.5.3 covers the
post-operative protocol.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no sequence
parameters, slice thicknesses or field of view values appear either: those are
local protocol quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- No single weighting shows everything.
- Detail and conspicuity come from different sequences read together.
- Three planes exist because one plane produces false linear appearances.
- Knee position changes how the obliquely running cruciate is presented.
- The uncommon questions need their own protocols.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the protocol is the first thing to establish when a report
disagrees with the clinical picture.

A meniscal or cruciate question is answered well by the routine set, which is
what it was built for. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

A cartilage question needs a cartilage sequence, and a routine protocol
understates chondral loss. 5.5.7 covers the cartilage.

A post-operative or metal bearing knee needs a different protocol, and running
the routine one wastes the study. 5.5.3 covers the post-operative protocol.

A report that conflicts with the examination is worth checking against the
protocol before it is believed or dismissed. 5.1.2 covers clinical examination
versus imaging.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is assuming every knee study is the same study. 5.20.8 covers
common reporting errors.

A cartilage assessment is made on a ligament protocol. 5.5.7 covers the
cartilage.

A single plane finding is reported without confirmation. 5.5.9 covers the
ligaments.

A thick slice averages a structure and the average is read as pathology. 5.2.5
covers magnification.

The request gives no clinical question, so the department cannot choose. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Every knee study is assumed to be the same study.
- A cartilage assessment is made on a ligament protocol.
- A single plane finding is reported unconfirmed.
- A thick slice's average is read as pathology.
- Without a clinical question the department cannot choose a protocol.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the standard protocol is the invisible variable behind most
disagreements about a knee study.

Protocols differ between departments in sequences, planes, slice thickness and
field strength, and none of that is in the report, so two reports on the same
knee are not comparable. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The grading conventions for menisci and cartilage were derived on particular
weightings, so applying them to a different protocol applies a rule to data it
was not derived from. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Three dimensional isotropic acquisition is replacing separate plane sequences and
changes the contrast as well as the geometry, which affects those conventions
again. 5.2.18 covers gradient echo imaging.

Accelerated reconstruction is shortening these protocols, and the images are not
identical to the ones the conventions came from. 5.18.8 covers artificial
intelligence in imaging.

The practical recommendation is to state the clinical question on the request and
to ask for the protocol when a report matters, which is the cheapest improvement
available. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

### Key Learning Points

- Protocols differ between departments and the report does not say how.
- Two reports on the same knee are not comparable.
- The grading conventions were derived on particular weightings.
- Isotropic acquisition and acceleration both change the images again.
- State the question on the request and ask for the protocol when it matters.
