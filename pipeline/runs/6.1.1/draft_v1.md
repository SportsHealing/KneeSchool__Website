# Anterior cruciate ligament injury

A twisting injury to the ligament that stops the shin bone sliding forward on the thigh bone, common in pivoting sport and often missed at first.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

The anterior cruciate ligament, or ACL, is a short tough band deep inside the
knee. It runs at an angle from the thigh bone to the shin bone. Its job is to
stop the shin sliding forward, and it helps stop the knee twisting too far.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Most ACL tears happen with nobody touching the player. A footballer plants a foot
and turns. A netballer lands from a jump slightly off balance. The body twists
and the knee does not follow.

Girls and women tear the ACL more often than boys and men playing the same sport.
The reasons are still being studied, and they are not simply about strength.

### How It Presents

There is often a loud pop. The knee swells within a few hours, which happens
because the tear bleeds inside the joint. Afterwards the knee can feel like it
wants to give way when the person turns on it.

Warm up programmes that train landing and turning lower the risk. They work best
when a whole squad does them regularly through the season rather than only at the
start.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL is a band inside the knee that stops the shin sliding forward.
- Most tears happen with no contact, on a planted foot during a turn or landing.
- A pop and swelling within a few hours are the usual signs.
- Warm up programmes that train landing and turning lower the risk.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is a strong cord inside the knee joint. It
is the main restraint stopping the shin bone sliding forward on the thigh bone,
and it helps control rotation when you turn.

### How It Presents

A tear usually happens in a twisting movement rather than a collision. People
describe a pop, swelling within a few hours, and later a knee that feels
unreliable when they change direction.

Walking in a straight line is often comfortable quite early. That is one reason
some tears are missed at first.

### Investigations and Imaging

A clinician examines the knee first. An x-ray looks for a fracture rather than
for the ligament itself. An MRI scan confirms the tear and, more usefully, shows
what else was injured at the same time.

### Management Overview

Not every tear needs an operation. The decision rests on how unstable the knee
feels, what you need it to do, and whether anything else inside the joint was
injured too.

Someone whose knee gives way during pivoting sport is in a different position
from someone whose knee is steady during daily activity. Surgery replaces the
torn ligament with a graft rather than stitching it back together.

### Rehabilitation Outline

Recovery is measured in months rather than weeks, whichever route is taken.
Rehabilitation matters as much as any operation, and it is the part you control.
Returning to sport is judged on what the knee can do rather than on the calendar.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL stops the shin bone sliding forward and helps control rotation.
- Most tears are twisting injuries without contact.
- A comfortable walk does not rule out a tear.
- Whether to operate depends on instability and demand, not on the scan alone.
- Recovery takes months, and rehabilitation drives the result.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Does a torn ACL always need surgery?
No. Many people manage well with a structured rehabilitation programme,
particularly if the knee stays steady during the activities they need.

**Q.** Will the ligament heal on its own?
A completely torn ACL usually does not heal back to its original strength, which
is why reconstruction uses a graft rather than repair.

**Q.** How soon would an operation happen?
Usually not immediately. Operating on a stiff, swollen knee raises the risk of
stiffness afterwards, so the swelling is settled and movement restored first.

**Q.** Can I damage the knee further by waiting?
Repeated episodes of giving way can injure the meniscus and the cartilage, which
is one reason instability is taken seriously.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

The ACL is an intracapsular, extrasynovial ligament running from the
posteromedial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle to the anterior
intercondylar area of the tibia. It carries two functional bundles, anteromedial
and posterolateral, named for their tibial insertions.

It is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation and a secondary
restraint to internal rotation.

### Causes and Risk Factors

The classical mechanism is non contact: deceleration or landing with the knee
near extension, valgus and internal rotation of the tibia. Female sex, previous
injury, neuromuscular control and anatomical factors including tibial slope are
recognised contributors.

### How It Presents

An audible pop, immediate loss of confidence in the knee, and a haemarthrosis
developing over the first few hours. Later the complaint is rotatory instability
on pivoting rather than pain.

### Examination

Lachman is performed at about twenty to thirty degrees of flexion and is the most
sensitive clinical test. The anterior drawer at ninety degrees is less sensitive,
particularly in the acute knee where hamstring guarding masks translation. The
pivot shift reproduces the rotatory instability patients describe.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL runs from the lateral femoral condyle to the anterior intercondylar area.
- It has anteromedial and posterolateral bundles tightening at different flexion angles.
- It is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation.
- The classical mechanism is non contact deceleration or landing.
- Lachman is the most sensitive clinical test.

## MRCS Level

### Examination

Examine the whole knee, because the associated injuries change the management.
Assess the collaterals, the posterolateral corner and the extensor mechanism.
Guarding limits the acute examination, and assessment under anaesthetic is
sometimes required before a plan can be made.

### Investigations and Imaging

On plain radiographs look for a Segond fracture, an avulsion of the lateral
tibial rim, which implies a cruciate rupture until proven otherwise. In a
skeletally immature patient look for a tibial spine avulsion rather than a
midsubstance tear. A deep lateral femoral notch sign is a further indirect clue.

MRI confirms the tear and grades the associated injury: lateral compartment bone
bruising, meniscal tears including ramp lesions of the posteromedial
meniscocapsular junction, chondral damage, and collateral or corner injury.

### Management Overview

The immediate priorities are to exclude a knee dislocation with vascular injury,
to restore range of movement, and to settle the effusion. Definitive management
follows once the knee is quiet and the whole injury pattern is understood.

### Key Learning Points

- A Segond fracture implies a cruciate rupture until proven otherwise.
- In the skeletally immature knee look for a tibial spine avulsion.
- Read the whole MRI, because associated injury drives management.
- Restore movement and settle the effusion before definitive surgery.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

Reconstruct on instability and demand rather than on the scan. The clear
indication is a patient whose knee gives way during the activities they need and
who has failed or declined a structured rehabilitation programme.

Graft choice trades re rupture risk against donor site morbidity and is age
dependent. Hamstring autograft, bone patellar tendon bone autograft and
quadriceps tendon autograft each have a defensible case. Allograft in a young
pivoting athlete carries a higher failure rate.

### Complications and Prognosis

Arthrofibrosis is associated with operating on a stiff, swollen knee with poor
quadriceps control. Graft failure, donor site pain, and persistent rotatory
laxity are the recognised early concerns.

Failure modes worth knowing for a viva: non anatomic tunnel placement, missed
posterolateral corner injury, unaddressed ramp lesion or meniscal deficiency,
increased posterior tibial slope, and return to sport before criteria are met.

### Controversies and Evidence

The case for adding a lateral extra articular procedure in selected high risk
patients is now reasonably strong, and its role in routine primary
reconstruction is not settled.

### Key Learning Points

- The indication is instability and demand, not the appearance of the scan.
- Graft choice is a trade between re rupture risk and donor site morbidity.
- Allograft in a young pivoting athlete fails more often.
- Operate on a quiet knee to reduce the risk of arthrofibrosis.
- Know the failure modes; examiners ask for them by name.

## Fellowship Level

### Management Overview

Revision starts with why the first reconstruction failed. Separate a traumatic re
injury from a knee that was never stable. Obtain long leg alignment views, a true
lateral to assess posterior tibial slope, and computed tomography for tunnel
position and diameter.

Tunnel management decides whether this is one operation or two. Acceptably placed
tunnels of modest diameter allow a single stage revision. Confluent, widened or
badly malpositioned tunnels need bone grafting and a staged approach, with the
interval set by graft incorporation rather than a fixed number of months.

### Complications and Prognosis

Treat the knee rather than the ligament. Meniscal deficiency, uncorrected coronal
malalignment and increased posterior tibial slope each load a revision graft, and
leaving them is a common route to a second failure.

Revision outcomes sit below primary outcomes for return to sport and for patient
reported scores. Counsel the patient before the operation rather than after it.

### Controversies and Evidence

Slope reducing anterior closing wedge osteotomy has a place in the revision knee
with a markedly increased slope. The threshold that justifies the additional
operation is not agreed.

### Key Learning Points

- Establish the mode of failure before planning a revision.
- Tunnel position and diameter decide single stage against staged revision.
- Address meniscal deficiency, alignment and slope, or expect a second failure.
- Revision outcomes are poorer than primary outcomes, and patients should hear that first.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

Four decisions sit in front of a consultant here, and the literature settles
none of them.

The first is whether to repair a proximal avulsion rather than reconstruct it.
Repair offers a native ligament with its innervation and no graft harvest. It
risks a second operation in a young pivoting athlete, where reconstruction has a
known medium term result. Tissue quality and age move the weight. The early
series are not long enough to move it for anybody.

The second is when an increased posterior tibial slope justifies correcting it.
Correction reduces the load the graft carries. It adds an osteotomy, its recovery
and its complications to an operation the patient already needs. Previous failure
moves the weight further than slope magnitude does, which is the part most often
reversed.

The third is what clears a patient to return to sport. Criterion based batteries
test what looks relevant, and their effect on re rupture in adolescents is not
established. Time based clearance alone is inadequate, which is the only part
everyone agrees on. What moves the weight is the sport, and whether anyone will
see the patient again.

The fourth is how far registry data should reach into an individual decision. It
is good enough to shape graft choice across a population and stays blunt for the
person in clinic. The question a department can actually answer is narrower:
whether its own outcomes match the registry, and whether anyone is checking.

### Key Learning Points

- Repair offers a native ligament and risks a second operation in a pivoting athlete.
- Slope correction reduces graft load and adds an osteotomy to an operation already needed.
- Previous failure moves the slope decision further than slope magnitude does.
- Return to sport batteries test what looks relevant and are not shown to change re rupture rates.
- Registry data shapes populations and stays blunt for the individual.
