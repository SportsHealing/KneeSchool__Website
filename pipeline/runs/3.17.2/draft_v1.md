# PCL Landmark Studies

A smaller literature than the ACL's, built on the same sectioning method, and
the one place where that method produced a finding surgery still argues about.

## FRCS Level

### What the Question Was

The PCL literature asks a narrower set of questions than the ACL's, because
the injury is less common and the ligament is harder to reach.

What restrains posterior translation and how much of it is the PCL. What
happens when the posterolateral structures are cut as well. What does an
isolated PCL injury do to the knee over time. And can a reconstruction restore
posterior stability.

The second question is the one that made the literature matter, because it
produced a finding that changed how combined injury is recognised. 3.8.1
covers the function.

### What Was Done

Girgis and colleagues described both cruciates in the same cadaveric work,
including the PCL's bundle arrangement and its reciprocal tension pattern
through flexion.

Gollehon, Torzilli and Warren ran the sectioning study in the 1980s that cut
the PCL and the posterolateral structures in sequence and in combination,
measuring posterior translation and external rotation at a range of flexion
angles.

Grood and colleagues ran related sectioning work that separated posterior
translation from rotation and showed that the two behave differently depending
on which structure has gone.

Harner quantified the PCL's cross sectional area and compared the bundles
mechanically, which is the anatomical argument behind double bundle
reconstruction.

Race and Amis tested PCL bundle function and reconstruction constructs in the
1990s, including the question of whether a single graft can reproduce the
reciprocal pattern.

### What It Showed

The PCL is the primary restraint to posterior translation, and it supplies
most of that restraint at higher flexion angles rather than uniformly.

Isolated PCL sectioning increases posterior translation and does not produce a
large increase in external rotation. Cutting the posterolateral structures as
well does.

That dissociation is the mechanical basis of the dial test: rotation at thirty
degrees and at ninety degrees separates an isolated posterolateral injury from
a combined one. 3.10.7 covers it.

The bundles are unequal in size and in behaviour, and the larger anterolateral
bundle carries most of the load in flexion. 3.8.3 covers bundle function.

No reconstruction tested in these studies restored normal posterior
translation across the flexion range, which is a consistent finding rather
than a single result.

### What It Changed

Recognition of combined injury. The posterolateral corner became a structure
examined deliberately rather than an afterthought, and that change is
traceable to the sectioning work.

The dial test entered examination teaching as a test with a mechanical
rationale rather than as a manoeuvre with a name.

Double bundle PCL reconstruction was designed from the bundle comparison work,
which is a clear case of a laboratory finding generating an operation.

The consistent failure to restore normal translation changed the stated aim of
PCL surgery in much of the literature, from restoration to improvement.

### Key Learning Points

- The PCL is the primary restraint to posterior translation and does most of that work in flexion rather than through the whole range.
- Isolated PCL sectioning gives translation without much rotation; adding the posterolateral structures gives both.
- That dissociation is why the dial test is performed at two flexion angles.
- The bundles differ in size and in loading pattern, which is the anatomical case for double bundle reconstruction.
- No construct in this literature restored normal posterior translation across the range.

## Fellowship Level

### What Was Done

The whole chapter rests on sequential sectioning, so its strengths and its
limits are the chapter's strengths and limits.

Sequence order matters more here than in the ACL work, because the structures
being cut share function. A study that cuts the PCL first and a study that
cuts the corner first can produce different attributions from the same
specimens.

Flexion angle matters as much as structure. A posterior drawer measured only
at ninety degrees misses the pattern that makes the finding useful.

Specimen number is small throughout. These are studies of ten or so knees, and
the variability between specimens is large relative to the effects being
measured.

### What It Showed

Where the literature is strong: the restraint hierarchy and the translation
and rotation dissociation. Both have been reproduced in several laboratories.

Where it is weak: what an isolated PCL injury does over years. The natural
history literature is observational, small, and drawn from patients who
presented, which selects for the ones doing badly.

Where it is contested: whether double bundle reconstruction outperforms single
bundle in patients. The laboratory case is clear and the clinical case is not.
3.8.9 covers it.

The gait literature adds something the sectioning studies cannot: a PCL
deficient knee adapts, and the adaptation is visible in walking long before it
is symptomatic. 3.8.7 covers the pattern.

### What to Read Now

Read a sectioning study's cutting order before its conclusion, because the
order constrains what can be attributed.

Read posterior translation figures with their flexion angle attached, and
discard any quoted without one.

Treat the double bundle question as open and read new work for whether its
comparator is a well done single bundle procedure.

Read the gait work alongside the cadaveric work, since it is the only evidence
here about a knee that is alive and loaded.

### Key Learning Points

- Cutting order constrains attribution, and it varies between the studies this chapter rests on.
- A posterior translation figure without a flexion angle is uninterpretable.
- Natural history evidence here is observational and selected, which is weaker than the laboratory evidence beside it.
- The double bundle case is strong in the laboratory and unproven in patients.

## Consultant Perspective

### What It Changed

The decision this literature informs is whether to operate on a PCL injury at
all, and the evidence supports a balance rather than a position.

Arguing for surgery: the sectioning work shows real and measurable
instability, the contact mechanics work shows altered loading that plausibly
leads to degeneration, and the gait work shows adaptation that the patient
does not choose.

Arguing against: no construct in this literature restored normal translation,
the natural history evidence is too weak to say who will deteriorate, and a
procedure that improves a measurement without changing the trajectory has
bought little.

Take the patient's age and physiology into account, because the degeneration
argument has decades to act in a young patient and may not in an older one.
Take their comorbidities into account, since this is a long and demanding
rehabilitation. Take the demands on their knee into account, because posterior
instability declares itself on stairs and descending slopes rather than in a
straight line. Take their psychological state into account, since a knee that
feels unreliable produces guarding that outlasts the ligament. And ask for
their own ideas, concerns and expectations, because many patients with an
isolated PCL injury are functioning well and have come for reassurance rather
than for an operation.

Record the balance and what tipped it, since the literature will not tip it.

### What to Read Now

What a trainee should take from this chapter is that a strong mechanical
finding and a weak clinical one can sit in the same subject, and that the PCL
is the clearest example in the knee.

Read the combined injury literature first, because recognising the corner
changes management more than any decision about the PCL itself.

Be explicit when teaching that the stated aim of PCL reconstruction in most of
this literature is improvement rather than restoration, and that the
difference is not a matter of surgical skill.

### Key Learning Points

- Whether to operate on an isolated PCL injury is a balance the literature does not settle.
- The mechanical case is strong, the natural history case is weak, and the two are often quoted as though they were one body of evidence.
- The five patient factors carry more weight here than the laboratory findings.
- Recognising a combined injury changes management more than any isolated PCL decision.
- Teach improvement rather than restoration as the stated aim, because that is what the literature reports.
