# Anatomy

The proximal fibula: a small bone with one articulation, three attachments and
one relation that makes all of it matter.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The proximal fibula is the head and neck of a long, thin bone lying lateral
and posterior to the tibia. The head is expanded and carries a flat facet on
its anteromedial surface for the tibia. From the posterolateral part of the
head a small upward projection rises, the styloid process.

Below the head the bone narrows into the neck before continuing as the shaft.
The neck is the part that matters clinically, because of what crosses it.

The fibula takes no significant part in the knee joint itself. It does not
articulate with the femur and it carries no meniscus.

### Relations

Medially: the lateral surface of the proximal tibia, with the superior
tibiofibular joint between them.

Laterally and posteriorly: the common peroneal nerve, which crosses the neck
of the fibula from behind to the front. It is subcutaneous at that point and
lies directly on bone.

Above: the lateral collateral ligament and the biceps femoris tendon converge
on the head. 2.3.2 covers the attachments.

Below: the peroneus longus arises from the head and the upper shaft, and its
fibres arch over the nerve as it passes forward.

### Function

The proximal fibula is an attachment point rather than a load bearing column.
It transmits very little axial load compared with the tibia.

Its contribution to the knee is indirect. By anchoring the lateral collateral
ligament and the posterolateral structures, it is the distal end of the
lateral restraint of the knee.

The superior tibiofibular joint allows small movement as the ankle moves, so
the proximal fibula is not rigidly fixed to the tibia.

### Clinical Relevance

The head is palpable in everyone and is the landmark for the lateral side of
the knee.

The common peroneal nerve lying on the neck is the single most important fact
about this bone. Anything done to the proximal fibula puts that nerve at risk,
and 2.3.3 covers it.

Because the fibula carries little load, the proximal part can be sacrificed in
some operations. The cost is the attachments and the nerve, not the weight
bearing.

### Key Learning Points

- The fibula does not articulate with the femur and carries no meniscus.
- The common peroneal nerve crosses the neck of the fibula, subcutaneous and directly on bone.
- The proximal fibula is an attachment point rather than a load bearing column.
- The superior tibiofibular joint permits small movement, so the fibula is not rigidly fixed.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe the head, the styloid and the neck. The head carries the tibial facet
anteromedially, facing up, forward and medially. The styloid process rises
from its posterolateral part and is the attachment of the posterolateral
structures. The neck is the constriction immediately below.

The superior tibiofibular joint is a plane synovial joint with its own
capsule, reinforced by anterior and posterior tibiofibular ligaments. Its
obliquity varies between people, and a more horizontal joint permits more
movement than a more oblique one.

### Relations

The common peroneal nerve emerges from behind the biceps femoris tendon,
crosses the neck under the peroneus longus origin, and divides into the
superficial and deep peroneal nerves. It lies of the order of 2 cm distal to
the tip of the fibular head at that point.

The superior tibiofibular joint occasionally communicates with the knee
through a recess behind the popliteus tendon, which is how a ganglion arising
from the joint can reach the nerve.

Anteriorly the anterior compartment arises from the tibia and the interosseous
membrane, with the anterior tibial artery passing forward through the membrane
above.

Posteriorly the soleus arises in part from the posterior surface of the head
and the upper shaft.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The proximal fibula is supplied by the inferior lateral genicular artery above
and by the anterior tibial recurrent artery, with branches of the peroneal
artery below. The periosteal supply around the head is good, which is relevant
where it is used as a vascularised graft elsewhere in the body.

Innervation of the superior tibiofibular joint capsule is from the recurrent
branch of the common peroneal nerve and from the nerve to popliteus.

The common peroneal nerve itself is a relation rather than a supply, and it is
the structure every description of this bone has to account for.

### Function

Axial load through the fibula is a small fraction of that through the tibia,
and almost all of it is transmitted distally rather than proximally.

The proximal fibula's real function is to anchor the lateral collateral
ligament, the biceps femoris and the popliteofibular ligament. It is the fixed
point against which the lateral side of the knee is restrained.

Because the superior tibiofibular joint moves with ankle motion, that fixed
point is not entirely fixed, which is part of why posterolateral
reconstructions behave differently from medial ones.

### Clinical Relevance

Fibular head excision or osteotomy divides the lateral collateral and the
posterolateral attachments, so it destabilises the knee laterally unless they
are reconstructed.

The nerve's position of a couple of centimetres below the head tip is the
practical reason a lateral incision is planned from the head rather than from
the joint line.

A ganglion of the superior tibiofibular joint is a recognised cause of common
peroneal nerve palsy, and it reaches the nerve because the joint may
communicate with the knee.

The fibula is used as a free vascularised graft in reconstructive surgery
elsewhere, and the proximal part is usually left to preserve the knee
attachments and the nerve.

### Key Learning Points

- The styloid process carries the posterolateral attachments; the facet faces up, forward and medially.
- The nerve crosses the neck of the order of 2 cm below the tip of the head.
- The superior tibiofibular joint may communicate with the knee, which is how a ganglion reaches the nerve.
- Excising the fibular head divides the lateral collateral and posterolateral attachments.
- The fibula transmits little axial load, and almost none of it proximally.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

In a posterolateral approach the fibular head is the landmark and the nerve is
the constraint. The nerve is identified behind the biceps tendon and followed
round the neck, because estimating its position from the skin is less reliable
than finding it.

The joint's possible communication with the knee means fluid or air introduced
into the knee can appear at the fibular head, and a cyst there can be of knee
origin.

### Function

Treating the proximal fibula as a fixed point is an approximation. It moves
with the ankle, and a reconstruction anchored to it inherits that movement.

That is one argument for tibial based rather than fibular based posterolateral
reconstruction, and it is anatomical rather than empirical. Section 7 owns the
choice.

### Clinical Relevance

Any procedure at the fibular head is a nerve operation as much as a bone
operation. Identifying the nerve first is the standard of care rather than a
precaution.

In high tibial osteotomy the proximal fibula and the superior tibiofibular
joint constrain the correction, and whether the joint is released is part of
the plan.

In tumour resection around the proximal fibula the nerve is usually the
structure that decides whether limb salvage is possible.

Every published measurement of the nerve's course here is a distance from the
fibular head or styloid. One figure appears on this page, registered and
unchecked; the rest need a source.

### Key Learning Points

- Find the nerve rather than estimate it; it is identified behind the biceps tendon and followed round the neck.
- The fibula is not a fixed point, which is an argument for tibial based posterolateral reconstruction.
- Any procedure at the fibular head is a nerve operation as much as a bone operation.
- The superior tibiofibular joint constrains correction in high tibial osteotomy.
