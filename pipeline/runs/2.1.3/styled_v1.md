# Medial Femoral Condyle

The shape of the medial condyle, its articular surface, and the landmarks on
its outer wall that locate the medial soft tissue attachments.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The medial condyle is the longer of the two in the sagittal plane and extends
further distally. Held at the end of a femur, it is the condyle that reaches
the table first.

Its articular surface is more curved than the lateral and narrower from side
to side, and the cartilage over it is of the order of 2 to 3 mm thick. The
surface continues round onto the posterior aspect, and the most posterior part
only articulates at deep flexion.

On the outer wall are the medial epicondyle and, above it, the adductor
tubercle. The lateral surface of the condyle forms the medial wall of the
intercondylar notch.

### Relations

Medially, outside the capsule: the superficial medial collateral ligament from
the epicondyle, adductor magnus inserting on the adductor tubercle, the medial
patellofemoral ligament in the region between the two, and the medial head of
gastrocnemius arising above and behind.

Inside the notch: the posterior cruciate ligament attaches to the lateral
surface of this condyle.

Posteriorly the medial head of gastrocnemius separates the condyle from the
popliteal contents, and the semimembranosus passes distally to the
posteromedial tibia.

### Function

The medial condyle carries the greater share of load through most of the
range, conventionally taught as about 60 per cent in a normally aligned knee
in stance. That follows from its position relative to the mechanical axis
rather than from anything about its shape.

Because it is longer front to back, the medial side of the joint translates
less through flexion than the lateral. The medial condyle acts more as a pivot
and the lateral as the side that travels.

### Clinical Relevance

The adductor tubercle and the medial epicondyle are the two landmarks used to
find the medial patellofemoral ligament and the superficial collateral through
skin.

The medial condyle is the commonest site of osteochondral problems on the
femoral side, and its load bearing position is the reason. The conditions
themselves belong to Section 6.

### Key Learning Points

- The medial condyle is longer front to back, extends further distally, and is more curved.
- Its lateral surface forms the medial wall of the notch and carries the posterior cruciate attachment.
- The adductor tubercle and medial epicondyle locate the medial soft tissue attachments.
- The medial side pivots while the lateral side travels.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Three features are worth naming. The articular surface is convex in both
planes and more sharply curved in the sagittal plane than the lateral condyle.
The outer wall carries the epicondyle and the adductor tubercle, with a
shallow depression between them. The inner wall is flat and forms the notch.

The relationship of the three medial attachments to each other is a standard
question. The adductor magnus inserts highest, on the tubercle. The
superficial collateral arises lowest, from the epicondyle. The medial
patellofemoral ligament arises between them, in the depression, closer to the
tubercle than to the epicondyle.

The posterior part of the surface carries a flattened area against which the
posterior horn of the medial meniscus lies at deep flexion.

### Relations

Capsular: the capsule attaches close to the articular margin anteriorly and
medially, and further from it posteriorly, so the posterior condyle has an
intracapsular portion.

Medial layered anatomy: superficial to the condyle lie, in order, the deep
fascia with the sartorius, then the superficial medial collateral ligament,
then the capsule with its deep collateral fibres attaching to the meniscus.
That layering is the subject of 2.9.1.

Posteromedially, the medial head of gastrocnemius arises from the condyle
above the joint line, and the semimembranosus and the posterior oblique fibres
reinforce the corner.

The saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch cross the medial side distal
to the condyle and are relations of the approach rather than of the bone.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Supply is from the superior medial and inferior medial genicular arteries,
reaching the epiphysis through the capsular and retinacular attachments, with
the descending genicular artery contributing above.

The notch surface and the posterior cruciate attachment are supplied by the
middle genicular artery.

Articular innervation is from the obturator nerve medially, with contributions
from the femoral nerve through the nerve to vastus medialis and from the
tibial nerve posteriorly.

### Function

The medial compartment behaves as the more constrained of the two: a more
curved femoral surface on a concave tibial surface, with the meniscus less
mobile than its lateral counterpart.

That constraint is why medial translation through flexion is small and why
rotation of the tibia occurs about an axis closer to the medial side than to
the centre of the joint. 3.13 develops this.

### Clinical Relevance

The medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle are the landmarks for medial
patellofemoral ligament reconstruction, and the ligament's femoral attachment
lying between them rather than on either is the reason placement is checked
rather than assumed.

Gastrocnemius arising from the condyle above the joint line means a
posteromedial exposure can be developed by taking the muscle off the bone
without entering the joint.

The medial condyle is where femoral osteochondral lesions and spontaneous
osteonecrosis most often appear. The anatomy explains the site; Section 6 owns
the disease.

### Key Learning Points

- Adductor magnus inserts highest, the superficial collateral arises lowest, and the medial patellofemoral ligament between them.
- The medial side is more constrained: a more curved femoral surface on a concave tibia with a less mobile meniscus.
- Supply is from the medial geniculars, with the middle genicular reaching the notch.
- Medial gastrocnemius arises above the joint line, which gives a posteromedial exposure that avoids the joint.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The posteromedial corner is a layered structure and the condyle is its anchor.
Working there means knowing which layer carries which restraint, because the
layers separate cleanly in a cadaver and far less cleanly in a swollen knee.

The gastrocnemius origin is the structure that protects the popliteal contents
during a posteromedial exposure, and the plane anterior to it is the one that
stays safe.

### Function

The medial compartment carries more load and accommodates less motion, which
is why it tolerates malalignment poorly and why varus loads it further.

Restoring medial geometry matters more than restoring lateral geometry for the
same reason. The lateral side can absorb a difference in translation; the
medial side cannot, because it was not translating in the first place.

### Clinical Relevance

Medial condylar geometry is the reference for the medial resection in
arthroplasty, and for the correction in a distal femoral osteotomy. Both are
technical decisions owned elsewhere, and both are made against this surface.

In cartilage work on the medial condyle, the load bearing zone is the part in
contact near extension rather than the geometric centre of the surface. That
distinction decides whether a lesion is weight bearing.

The femoral attachment of the medial patellofemoral ligament lies close to the
physis in a skeletally immature knee, which constrains reconstruction in that
group. Section 10 owns the paediatric decision.

### Key Learning Points

- The posteromedial corner is layered and the condyle anchors it; the plane anterior to gastrocnemius stays safe.
- The medial compartment carries more and moves less, so it tolerates malalignment poorly.
- The load bearing zone is the contact area near extension, not the geometric centre of the condyle.
- The medial patellofemoral ligament attachment lies close to the physis in a growing knee.
