# Femoral Avulsion

The ligament pulled off its upper attachment, which is the pattern with the best
blood supply and the strongest case for repairing rather than replacing.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

A ligament can tear in the middle, or it can pull away from the bone at one end.
6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

When the ACL pulls away from the top end, where it attaches to the thigh bone,
that is a femoral avulsion. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

Where it comes apart matters, because the ends are not the same. The top end has
a better blood supply than the middle. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

### How It Presents

It feels like any other ACL tear: a pop, swelling within hours, and a knee that
is unreliable afterwards. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

The difference is not in how it feels but in what a surgeon can do about it.

### Key Learning Points

- A ligament can tear in the middle or pull off at an end.
- Pulling off the thigh bone end is a femoral avulsion.
- The top end has a better blood supply than the middle.
- It feels the same as any other ACL tear.
- The difference is in what can be done about it.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

A femoral avulsion means the ligament has detached from its attachment on the
thigh bone rather than snapping across its middle. 6.3.2 covers the
mid-substance tear.

The torn ligament is otherwise intact, and it is the attachment that has failed.
6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

### How It Presents

The symptoms are those of any acute ACL injury: rapid swelling, difficulty
continuing, and later a knee that does not feel trustworthy on turning. 6.2.5
covers acute haemarthrosis.

The pattern is identified on the scan rather than from the symptoms. 6.3.5
covers complete tears.

### Management Overview

Because the ligament itself is largely intact and the upper end has a reasonable
blood supply, repairing it back onto the bone is a possibility here that it is
not in a mid-substance tear. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Repair preserves the person's own ligament with its nerve supply, and avoids
taking a graft from elsewhere. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

It is not an established alternative to reconstruction, and whether it holds up
in a young person returning to pivoting sport is the open question. 6.8.1 covers
graft failure.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament detached from the thigh bone rather than snapping.
- The ligament itself is largely intact.
- The upper end has a reasonable blood supply.
- Repair is a possibility here that it is not in a mid-substance tear.
- Whether repair holds up in pivoting sport is unsettled.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is this better or worse than a tear in the middle?
Neither, as an injury. The knee is just as unstable. What differs is that this
pattern can sometimes be repaired, which is an extra option rather than a better
outlook.

**Q.** Why not repair every ACL tear?
Because most tear across the middle, where the two ends are frayed and poorly
supplied with blood, and stitching them has a long history of failing. The
proximal avulsion is the exception being revisited.

**Q.** If repair preserves my own ligament, why is it not standard?
Because the evidence is shorter than for reconstruction. The early results are
encouraging and the follow up is not yet long enough to know how it performs in
someone who plays pivoting sport for another decade.

**Q.** What happens if a repair fails?
A reconstruction can still be done afterwards. That is part of the argument on
both sides: it is recoverable, and it means a second operation and a second
recovery.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

A proximal avulsion detaches the ligament from the lateral femoral condyle,
leaving the substance of the ligament and its tibial attachment intact. 2.7.6
covers the femoral footprint.

The ligament's blood supply enters predominantly from above, which is the basis
for the claim that this pattern has biological potential the mid-substance tear
lacks. 2.7.4 covers the blood supply.

A proximal remnant that remains apposed to the femur is the appearance that
prompts the repair discussion. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

### Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging identifies the location of the discontinuity, which
is the finding that distinguishes this pattern. 5.7.3 covers complete tears.

The remnant's position and quality are assessed as well as its presence, because
both bear on whether repair is feasible. 5.7.8 covers ACL healing.

### Management Overview

Reconstruction remains the standard treatment for a complete tear in any
location. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Primary repair, with or without augmentation, is offered selectively on the
basis of tear location and tissue quality. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

### Key Learning Points

- A proximal avulsion detaches the ligament from the lateral femoral condyle.
- The substance and tibial attachment remain intact.
- The blood supply enters predominantly from above.
- An apposed proximal remnant prompts the repair discussion.
- Reconstruction remains the standard for a complete tear anywhere.

## MRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer locates the tear before discussing what to do
about it.

The expected points are the proximal location, the intact substance, the blood
supply argument, the remnant's quality, and the fact that reconstruction remains
standard. 5.7.3 covers complete tears.

Say that tear location is a treatment variable rather than a severity grading.
6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

Say that the scan is the only practical way to identify the pattern before
arthroscopy. 5.7.8 covers ACL healing.

### Management Overview

Repair is a selective option and reconstruction is the default, and a candidate
who reverses that has misstated current practice. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Selection turns on tear location, tissue quality and the patient's demands,
which is the same triad as everywhere else in this group. 6.1.4 covers the
natural history.

### Complications and Prognosis

Repair failure returns the patient to the reconstruction they would otherwise
have had, with a second anaesthetic and a second rehabilitation. 6.8.1 covers
graft failure.

That recoverability is the strongest argument for trying repair in a selected
patient and the strongest argument against trying it in an unselected one. 6.1.4
covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Locate the tear before discussing treatment.
- Tear location is a treatment variable rather than a grading.
- Repair is selective and reconstruction is the default.
- Selection turns on location, tissue quality and demand.
- Recoverability argues both for selective repair and against unselected repair.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to hold a position on repair and
to know what would change it.

The case for repair is a preserved native ligament with its proprioceptive
supply, no graft harvest and no donor site. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the
ACL.

The case against is that reconstruction has a known medium term result in the
population most affected, which is young pivoting athletes, and repair does not
yet. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Tissue quality and the interval since injury move the balance, and both degrade,
which is why this is a decision with a deadline. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is which proximal tears are suitable for repair, since
not all of them are. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

The second is whether augmentation changes the failure rate enough to widen the
indication. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

The third is whether the early series follow up is long enough to counsel an
adolescent, which it is not yet. 6.7.3 covers reconstruction strategies.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- The case for repair is a native ligament with its own nerve supply.
- The case against is that reconstruction's medium term result is known.
- Tissue quality and interval both degrade, so the decision has a deadline.
- Which proximal tears suit repair is unresolved.
- Follow up is not yet long enough to counsel an adolescent.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this pattern is where enthusiasm and evidence are furthest
apart in the whole group, and a fellow is likely to meet both.

The biological argument is sound and the clinical question it raises is not
answered by the biology, which is the distinction that keeps getting collapsed.
2.7.4 covers the blood supply.

A repair that fails in an adolescent costs a second operation at an age where
compliance with a second rehabilitation is not guaranteed. 6.7.3 covers
reconstruction strategies.

A reconstruction that was never needed costs a graft harvest and a donor site in
somebody whose own ligament could have been kept. 6.1.2 covers the functions of
the ACL.

The defensible stance is selective, explicit and audited: state the criteria
used, record them, and look at the results rather than at the series. 6.8.1
covers graft failure.

### Key Learning Points

- Enthusiasm and evidence are furthest apart here.
- The biology is sound and does not answer the clinical question.
- A failed repair in an adolescent costs a second rehabilitation.
- An unnecessary reconstruction costs a graft and a donor site.
- The defensible stance is selective, explicit and audited.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question is whether to offer a procedure whose evidence is
shorter than the patient's remaining knee life.

Offering it means acting ahead of the evidence in a recoverable direction, which
is defensible when the patient understands what is and is not known. 6.3.6
covers healing ACL tears.

Declining it means a reliable operation and a ligament discarded that might have
been kept, which is also defensible and is the current default. 6.3.5 covers
complete tears.

What decides it is the same five things. The patient's age and physiology,
because tissue quality is the technical precondition and healing capacity the
biological one. Their medical comorbidities, because smoking and diabetes bear
on healing more here than in a reconstruction. The demands they put on the knee,
because a pivoting athlete is where the evidence gap matters most. Their
psychological state, because accepting a less proven operation requires
tolerating uncertainty. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations,
including how they weigh their own tissue against a known result.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and record which way they
pointed. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- The evidence is shorter than the patient's remaining knee life.
- Offering it acts ahead of the evidence in a recoverable direction.
- Declining it is also defensible and is the current default.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide it.
- Record which way they pointed.
