# Normal Fluid Physiology

Why a normal knee holds almost no fluid, and why that is what makes an effusion
detectable.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Understanding the normal fluid state is what makes the effusion tests
interpretable, because every one of them depends on moving a volume from one
part of the capsule to another. 4.8.5 covers the test that does it most
directly.

A normal knee contains only a small volume of synovial fluid, enough to
lubricate and nourish the cartilage and not enough to be detectable by hand.

The capsule, by contrast, has a large potential volume including the
suprapatellar pouch, so a knee can accommodate a substantial effusion before it
becomes tense. 4.3.8 covers the appearance.

### How It Is Performed

This page is not a manoeuvre. It is the physiology the manoeuvres rest on, and
the examination it informs is the effusion assessment. 4.6.3 covers that
assessment.

In practice the relevant steps are establishing that the knee's parapatellar
hollows are present, which indicates a normal small volume, and establishing
that the suprapatellar region is not full.

A normal knee should also move freely into full extension, since a knee holding
fluid rests in slight flexion. 4.3.5 covers the supine assessment.

A normal knee should hold the leg straight against gravity, since even a modest
effusion inhibits the quadriceps. 4.7.8 covers extension lag.

Comparing the two knees is what establishes a patient's normal, because the
contours vary between people. 4.1.2 covers the principle.

### What a Positive Means

Present parapatellar hollows, a flat suprapatellar region and a knee that rests
straight is a knee with a normal fluid volume.

Loss of the hollows is the earliest visible change, because they are the
shallowest part of the capsular space. 4.8.3 covers the test that uses that.

A knee resting in slight flexion with no other explanation suggests fluid, since
flexion is the position of greatest capsular volume and therefore of least
pressure. 4.8.2 covers the significance.

A knee that cannot hold the leg straight with no weakness and no tendon problem
suggests fluid, through reflex inhibition of the quadriceps. 4.7.8 covers it.

A knee with a normal fluid volume and a complaint of swelling means the swelling
is extra-articular, intermittent or something other than fluid. 4.2.3 covers
swelling as a symptom.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal knee holds a small volume, not detectable by hand.
- The capsule's potential volume is large, including the suprapatellar pouch.
- The parapatellar hollows are the shallowest part of the space, so they change first.
- Flexion is the position of greatest capsular volume, so an effused knee rests flexed.
- Even a modest effusion inhibits the quadriceps.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this physiology is the reasoning behind the tests, and giving it
explains why one test is more sensitive than another.

The expected points are the small normal volume, the large potential volume, the
suprapatellar pouch's communication with the joint, and the pressure volume
relationship.

Say that the tests work by moving fluid, which is why a sweep followed by an
observation detects less fluid than a tap does. 4.8.5 covers the sweep.

Say that the capsule's compliance explains why a knee can hold a large volume
painlessly and why a rapidly accumulating volume is painful at a smaller size.
4.8.7 covers the acute haemarthrosis.

Say that the suprapatellar pouch's communication is what allows fluid to be
milked downwards, and that it is occasionally separate, which changes the
examination. 4.6.3 covers the assessment.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that synovial fluid is produced by the synovium and
cleared through the lymphatics, so an effusion represents production exceeding
clearance rather than a fixed volume. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

The capsule is compliant up to a point and then stiff, which is why a slowly
accumulating effusion is painless and a rapidly accumulating one is tense and
painful at a smaller volume. 4.8.7 covers it.

Flexion increases the capsular volume, which is why the knee adopts flexion and
why extension is the position in which an effusion is most uncomfortable.
4.5.4 covers flexion deformity.

Raised intra-articular pressure inhibits the quadriceps through a reflex arc,
which is why an effusion produces weakness that resolves rather than weakness
that needs strengthening. 4.8.2 covers that mechanism.

### How Good It Is

The physiology is established and the clinical application is robust, which
makes this page unusual in a section where most pages describe an imperfect
test.

What varies between patients is the capsular compliance and the baseline
contours, which is why every effusion test is a comparison. 4.1.2 covers the
principle.

The composition of the fluid cannot be determined by examination, which is what
aspiration is for, and the distinction between blood and synovial fluid is
clinically important. 4.8.7 covers the acute haemarthrosis.

The physiology also explains the limits of the tests, which is why it is worth
knowing rather than only the manoeuvres. 4.8.3 covers the most sensitive.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- An effusion represents production exceeding clearance rather than a fixed volume.
- The capsule is compliant up to a point and then stiff.
- A slowly accumulating effusion is painless; a rapid one is tense at a smaller volume.
- Raised pressure inhibits the quadriceps through a reflex arc.
- The composition cannot be determined by examination.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the physiology contributes to interpretation rather than to a
decision, and the interpretation changes several decisions.

A tense effusion in an acute knee is a small volume accumulating fast, which
narrows the differential and raises the urgency. 4.8.7 covers it.

A large comfortable effusion is a larger volume accumulating slowly, which
points at a chronic process. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

The quadriceps inhibition explains why an effused knee is not ready for a
reconstruction and why rehabilitation stalls while the effusion persists.
4.7.8 covers the lag.

The capsular volume explains why an effused knee loses flexion before
extension, which is worth knowing when interpreting a post-operative range.
4.7.4 covers flexion assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating the volume as the finding when the rate of
accumulation is the more informative variable. 4.8.7 covers it.

The second is attributing quadriceps weakness in an effused knee to the muscle.

The third is attributing lost flexion in an effused knee to stiffness.
4.7.7 covers contractures.

The fourth is assuming an effusion means an intra-articular injury, when
inflammatory and crystal causes produce the same thing. 4.8.10 covers crystal
arthropathy.

The fifth is drawing a conclusion about fluid composition from the examination.

### Key Learning Points

- The rate of accumulation is more informative than the volume.
- Quadriceps weakness in an effused knee is inhibition, not muscle failure.
- Lost flexion in an effused knee is not stiffness.
- An effusion does not imply an intra-articular injury.
- Fluid composition cannot be concluded from the examination.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the physiology is most useful in explaining the knee that
keeps effusing and has nothing mechanical wrong with it.

A knee producing fluid faster than it clears it has a synovial process, and that
is an inflammatory or crystal question rather than a mechanical one.
4.8.9 covers synovitis.

A knee whose effusion fills a popliteal cyst has a valve like communication, so
the cyst's behaviour reflects the joint's production and treating the cyst alone
recurs. 4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa.

A knee that has been aspirated repeatedly without the cause being addressed will
refill, which is a predictable consequence of the physiology rather than a
treatment failure. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

In a knee with a previous synovectomy or a chronically scarred capsule the
compliance is altered, so the usual relationship between volume and tension does
not hold. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

In the chronically effused knee the quadriceps cannot be rebuilt while the
effusion persists, which sets the order of treatment and is the most practical
consequence on this page. 4.3.9 covers wasting.

### Key Learning Points

- A knee producing fluid faster than it clears it has a synovial process.
- A popliteal cyst's behaviour reflects the joint's production, so treating the cyst alone recurs.
- Repeated aspiration without addressing the cause refills predictably.
- A scarred capsule alters the usual relationship between volume and tension.
- The quadriceps cannot be rebuilt while the effusion persists.
