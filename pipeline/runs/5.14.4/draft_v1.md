# Crystal Arthropathy

Deposits with an appearance of their own, where the radiograph outperforms the
scan and an acute attack cannot be told from infection.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Crystal arthropathy is joint disease caused by crystal deposition, chiefly urate
and calcium pyrophosphate. 5.5.11 covers the synovium.

Radiographs show calcium pyrophosphate deposits in cartilage and menisci, and
erosions with overhanging margins in gout. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

It matters because the deposits are visible and specific, which is unusual in
this chapter, and because an acute attack mimics infection. 5.14.7 covers septic
arthritis.

### How It Is Acquired

Start with radiographs, because calcification is the finding and they show it
best. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

Look at the menisci and the hyaline cartilage for calcification. 5.5.8 covers the
menisci.

Look at the bone for erosion with a sclerotic overhanging margin. 5.5.6 covers
the bones.

Use ultrasound where a tendon or a superficial deposit is the question. 5.4.5
covers the quadriceps tendon.

Aspirate when the knee is acutely hot, because the crystal and the organism are
both found that way. 5.4.17 covers the aspiration.

### What Normal Looks Like

Cartilage and menisci with no calcification. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

No erosion with an overhanging margin. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Joint space appropriate to age with no disproportionate pattern. 5.3.17 covers
joint space narrowing.

No tophaceous soft tissue mass. 4.3.8 covers swelling.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Linear calcification in the hyaline cartilage and in the menisci. 5.5.8 covers
the menisci.

Joint space loss in a pattern that does not fit the alignment, often
patellofemoral predominant. 5.3.3 covers the skyline view.

Erosion with a sclerotic overhanging margin, which is characteristic of gout.
5.5.6 covers the bones.

A soft tissue mass of intermediate signal containing deposits. 5.13.7 covers bone
tumours.

An acutely swollen joint with synovitis and nothing else to explain it. 5.14.1
covers synovitis.

### Key Learning Points

- The deposits are visible on radiographs and are specific.
- Calcification sits in cartilage and in menisci.
- Gout erodes with an overhanging margin.
- An acute attack mimics infection.
- Aspiration finds both the crystal and the organism.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer asks for the radiograph and then the aspirate.

The expected points are the radiograph, the cartilage and meniscal
calcification, the erosion pattern, ultrasound for a superficial deposit, and
the aspiration.

Say that this is the chapter's exception, where the radiograph beats the scan.
5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

Say that an acute crystal attack and an infection cannot be separated by
imaging. 5.14.7 covers septic arthritis.

Say that calcium pyrophosphate deposition produces a joint space pattern that
does not match the alignment. 5.20.6 covers reporting osteoarthritis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a crystal has a density and a signal of its own,
so unlike every other condition in this chapter the agent itself is directly
visible. 5.2.22 covers the CT acquisition.

That visibility is why a plain film is informative, and why dual energy computed
tomography has a role that urate in particular suits. 5.2.22 covers the CT
acquisition.

Calcium pyrophosphate deposition produces arthritis in a distribution that
mechanical wear does not, which is the clue on a radiograph reported as
osteoarthritis. 5.20.6 covers reporting osteoarthritis.

Chondrocalcinosis also makes a meniscus look abnormal on magnetic resonance
images, which is a direct cause of over-reported tears. 5.6.2 covers the
meniscal signal grading.

### How Good It Is

Radiography is the best available first test here and the aspirate is the
diagnostic one.

Its best contribution is the calcification and the erosion pattern, both of
which are specific when present. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

It is limited because absence of calcification excludes nothing, because the
acute attack cannot be told from infection, and because magnetic resonance
appearances mislead towards meniscal pathology. 5.6.2 covers the meniscal signal
grading.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
attenuation values or deposit dimensions appear either: those are published and
protocol quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The crystal itself is visible, unlike every other agent here.
- Dual energy computed tomography suits urate.
- The joint space pattern does not match the alignment.
- Chondrocalcinosis causes over-reported meniscal tears.
- Absence of calcification excludes nothing.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the finding changes whether an operation happens at all. 5.1.3
covers choosing the correct investigation.

Chondrocalcinosis with a reported meniscal tear is a reason to doubt the tear
rather than to resect it. 5.6.2 covers the meniscal signal grading.

A crystal arthropathy pattern explains a knee whose wear does not fit its
alignment. 5.20.6 covers reporting osteoarthritis.

An acute presentation is aspirated before anything else, and a scan delays that.
5.4.17 covers the aspiration.

Deposits around an implant complicate the assessment of loosening and infection
together. 5.17.9 covers the revision arthroplasty.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is resecting a meniscus that looks torn because it is
calcified. 5.6.2 covers the meniscal signal grading.

An acute attack is treated as an infection, or an infection as an attack. 5.14.7
covers septic arthritis.

A tophus is reported as a soft tissue tumour. 5.13.7 covers bone tumours.

Calcification is read as an old injury. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

A disproportionate joint space pattern is reported as osteoarthritis with no
comment. 5.20.6 covers reporting osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- A calcified meniscus is resected as a torn one.
- Attack and infection are mistaken for each other.
- A tophus is reported as a tumour.
- Calcification is read as an old injury.
- A disproportionate pattern deserves a comment.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level these patients arrive after an arthroscopy that found
calcified cartilage and did not change the symptoms. 5.1.2 covers clinical
examination versus imaging.

The imaging was right and the inference was wrong, which is the pattern worth
recognising rather than the appearance. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Deposition accelerates after meniscectomy and after arthroplasty, so a
post-operative knee with new calcification is following a known course rather
than developing a complication. 5.20.7 covers reporting the post-operative knee.

Dual energy computed tomography for urate is available in few centres and
answers a question the aspirate already answers more cheaply. 5.1.5 covers the
cost-effectiveness of imaging.

An acute crystal attack in a replaced knee is the diagnosis nobody wants to make
without an aspirate. 5.17.9 covers the revision arthroplasty.

The useful report names the deposits, their sites, the joint space pattern, the
erosion morphology, and that an acute presentation needs an aspirate. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The imaging was right and the inference was wrong.
- Deposition accelerates after meniscectomy and arthroplasty.
- Dual energy computed tomography answers what the aspirate does.
- An attack in a replaced knee still needs an aspirate.
- The report should direct an acute knee to aspiration.
