# Dashboard Injury

A posteriorly directed force on a flexed knee. The classic posterior cruciate
mechanism, and the one most often accompanied by injuries elsewhere.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A dashboard injury is a blow to the front of the flexed tibia that drives it
backwards relative to the femur. The name comes from a car collision but the
mechanism occurs in falls onto a flexed knee and in sport.

Asking about it tests whether the posterior cruciate was loaded, because it is
the structure that resists exactly this.

It also screens for injuries elsewhere, since the energy involved is
frequently high and the knee is rarely the only thing hurt. 3.8.1 covers the
ligament's function.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether the knee was bent and whether something struck the front of the
shin rather than the knee itself.

Ask about the energy: a road traffic collision, a fall from height, or a
sporting impact.

Ask about the hip and the femur, because a posteriorly directed force on a
flexed knee also loads them and a hip dislocation or femoral fracture takes
priority.

Ask about the foot and ankle, which are injured in the same events and are
frequently missed.

Ask about numbness, tingling or weakness, and whether the knee was ever
obviously out of shape.

### What a Positive Means

A dashboard mechanism with posterior sag on examination is a posterior
cruciate injury. 4.11.1 covers the sag sign.

The same mechanism with external rotation instability raises the
posterolateral corner as well, which is the combination that matters most.
4.11.7 covers combined assessment.

A dashboard injury with an inability to weight bear raises a tibial plateau or
patellar fracture.

A high energy dashboard injury with gross multidirectional laxity is a knee
dislocation and the vascular assessment comes first.

An isolated posterior cruciate injury with no other findings is possible and
is less common than the combination.

### Key Learning Points

- A posteriorly directed force on a flexed tibia is the posterior cruciate mechanism.
- The energy is usually high, so the knee is rarely the only injury.
- Ask about the hip, the femur, the foot and the ankle in the same breath.
- The combination with a posterolateral injury matters more than the isolated injury.
- Gross multidirectional laxity after high energy is a dislocation until excluded.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning is that the posterior cruciate is the primary restraint
to posterior translation and does most of that work in flexion, which is
exactly the position of this mechanism. 3.8.2 covers posterior stability.

So a flexed knee struck from the front loads the posterior cruciate maximally,
which is why the mechanism is so characteristic.

The posterolateral structures resist external rotation and are loaded when the
force has a rotational component, which is common in real collisions.

The dial test at two flexion angles is what separates the isolated from the
combined injury, and the mechanism is what prompts it. 4.13.5 covers the test.

A posterior cruciate injury is under-diagnosed acutely because posterior sag
is subtle in a swollen knee and the patient can often walk.

### How Good It Is

The dashboard mechanism is one of the most specific histories in the knee,
because very little else produces it.

When the mechanism is clear it should direct the examination towards posterior
translation and rotation specifically, and that direction is its whole value.

It performs poorly for grading, because the acute examination under-reports
posterior laxity for the same reasons it under-reports anterior laxity.

A negative answer excludes little, because posterior cruciate injuries also
occur with hyperextension and with rotational mechanisms. 4.2.19 covers
hyperextension.

### Key Learning Points

- The posterior cruciate does most of its work in flexion, which is this mechanism's position.
- The dial test at two flexion angles separates isolated from combined injury.
- Posterior cruciate injury is under-diagnosed acutely because sag is subtle and patients walk.
- The mechanism is specific; a negative answer excludes little.
- Its value is directing the examination towards translation and rotation specifically.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the dashboard history is mainly a prompt to assess breadth,
because the isolated and combined injuries are managed differently and the
combined one is easy to miss.

An isolated posterior cruciate injury is frequently managed without surgery,
and 3.17.2 sets out why that balance is genuinely close.

A combined posterior cruciate and posterolateral injury is a different
proposition and much more often reconstructed. 4.13.8 covers combined lateral
injuries.

The history also establishes whether this was a polytrauma event, which
changes the timing of everything: a knee ligament injury in a patient with
other injuries is assessed early and reconstructed late.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is diagnosing an isolated posterior cruciate injury
because the posterior drawer was positive and nothing else was tested.

The second is accepting an acute examination as definitive. A posterior
cruciate injury assessed at two to six weeks gives a different and more useful
picture. 4.1.5 covers the timing problem.

The third is missing the associated injury outside the knee, particularly the
ankle and the hip, in a patient whose knee is the loudest complaint.

And a dashboard injury in an elderly patient raises a fracture at lower
energy, so the radiograph threshold is lower.

### Key Learning Points

- The history prompts an assessment of breadth, because the combined injury is easy to miss.
- An isolated posterior cruciate injury is often managed without surgery; the combined one often is not.
- A knee assessed at two to six weeks gives a more useful picture than the acute one.
- In polytrauma the knee is assessed early and reconstructed late.
- In an elderly patient the radiograph threshold is lower.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the dashboard injury appears as a chronic posterior
cruciate deficiency, often years later, with a gait pattern the patient has
adapted to. 4.11.6 covers the chronic presentation.

The history that matters then is the original mechanism and whether the
posterolateral corner was ever assessed, because a chronic combined injury is
the common reason a posterior cruciate reconstruction fails. 4.13.4 covers the
assessment.

A varus thrust during walking in a chronic posterior cruciate deficient knee
indicates a lateral side component and changes the operation from a
reconstruction to a reconstruction plus a realignment. 4.4.4 covers the gait
pattern.

The second question is what the patient actually cannot do, since many
chronically deficient knees function well and the mechanical finding is not
the indication. 3.17.2 covers the balance.

### Key Learning Points

- Chronic posterior cruciate deficiency often presents years later with an adapted gait.
- An unassessed posterolateral corner is the common reason a posterior cruciate reconstruction fails.
- A varus thrust changes the operation from a reconstruction to a reconstruction plus realignment.
- Many chronically deficient knees function well, so the mechanical finding is not the indication.
