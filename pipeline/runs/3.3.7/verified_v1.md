# Instant Centre of Rotation

The knee has no fixed hinge pin. Its axis of rotation moves through the arc,
which is why a single axis brace never matches it.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Any rotation at a given instant happens about some point. That point is the
instant centre of rotation.

A door hinge has one instant centre and it never moves. The knee's moves
continuously, which is what makes it polycentric rather than a hinge.

The centre is a mathematical consequence of the surface motion rather than a
structure. There is nothing at that point to find on a dissection.

### At the Knee

Because the knee rolls and glides, its instant centre moves through the arc.
3.3.2 covers roll and glide.

The path the centre traces is roughly within the lateral femoral condyle, and
it moves posteriorly as the knee flexes.

Conventional teaching places the centres in a small cluster in the normal knee
and describes them as scattered in an abnormal one.

The axis of flexion is therefore not the line between the two epicondyles at
every angle, although that line is a usable approximation for many purposes.

### What Changes It

Surface damage, which changes how the condyle travels and therefore where the
centre sits.

Ligament injury, since the centre's path is set by the constraints and a lost
constraint changes it.

Meniscal loss, which changes the congruence that guides the condyle.

An implant, which imposes a centre path by its geometry instead of negotiating
one.

### Clinical Relevance

A brace with one fixed axis cannot follow a moving centre, so it slides on the
limb through the arc. That is a design limit rather than a fitting error.

Knee replacement has to choose a centre path, which is part of why implants
feel different from a native knee in deep flexion.

A moving centre means a muscle's lever arm changes continuously through the
range, which 3.4.4 covers.

Describing the knee as a hinge is a useful simplification that fails exactly
where the interesting problems are.

### Key Learning Points

- The instant centre is the point a rotation happens about at a given moment.
- The knee's centre moves through the arc, which makes it polycentric.
- The centre is a consequence of surface motion, not a structure.
- Conventional teaching clusters the centres in a normal knee and scatters them in an abnormal one.
- A single axis brace cannot follow a moving centre, so it slides on the limb.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Define the quantity before using it. The instant centre is the point about
which one segment rotates relative to another at a given instant, with zero
relative velocity at that point.

In three dimensions the equivalent is a helical or screw axis rather than a
point, because the motion includes translation along the axis.

The locus of successive centres is the centrode, and its shape rather than any
single centre is what characterises a joint.

### At the Knee

The sagittal plane centrode of the normal knee is small and lies within the
lateral femoral condyle, moving posteriorly with flexion.

The axis of axial rotation is separate and lies medially, because the medial
contact point translates little while the lateral translates considerably.
3.3.2 covers that asymmetry.

The epicondylar axis approximates the flexion axis well enough for many
surgical purposes, and it is an approximation rather than the axis itself.

Patellofemoral motion has its own instant centre, which is why patellar
tracking is described separately. 3.12 covers it.

### What Changes It

Cruciate deficiency, which enlarges and displaces the centrode in cadaveric
work.

Articular surface loss, which changes the local geometry the centre depends
on.

Implant design, which replaces the native centrode with the one the geometry
dictates.

Measurement method, since a centrode computed from two nearby positions is
sensitive to the error in each.

### How It Is Measured

The classical method takes successive lateral radiographs through flexion and
constructs the centre geometrically from paired points, which is sensitive to
small positional error.

Fluoroscopic and model based tracking computes it from continuous bone
position and is the current approach.

Cadaveric rigs measure it under controlled load, without muscle.

Error grows as the angular increment shrinks, which is the practical reason
published centrodes differ between studies.

### Clinical Relevance

Use the epicondylar axis as the working approximation in surgery and keep its
approximate status in mind.

Expect a single axis brace or external fixator spanning the knee to fit the
limb imperfectly through range.

Read a published centrode with its angular increment and its method attached.

Explain changing muscle lever arms through range by the moving centre rather
than by muscle length alone.

### Key Learning Points

- The instant centre has zero relative velocity; in three dimensions it is a screw axis.
- The centrode's shape, not any one centre, characterises a joint.
- The sagittal centrode lies in the lateral femoral condyle; the rotation axis lies medially.
- The epicondylar axis is a working approximation of the flexion axis.
- Centrode error grows as the angular increment shrinks, so published curves differ.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the centrode as the design variable that single radius and multi radius
implants disagree about. A single radius design imposes a fixed centre, a
multi radius one approximates a moving centre, and both are approximations of
a native path.

Hinged and rotating hinge implants abandon the native centrode deliberately,
trading kinematic fidelity for constraint when the soft tissues cannot provide
it.

The same quantity underlies the mechanics of a distraction or hinged external
fixator across the knee: an axis set wrongly translates the joint while it
flexes.

### What Changes It

Implant sagittal geometry, which sets the imposed centre path.

Component position, since a well designed implant placed off axis imposes a
different path from the intended one.

Soft tissue balance, which decides whether the imposed path and the preferred
one agree.

Bone loss and the resulting constraint choice, which may remove the question
by making the implant the whole constraint.

### How It Is Measured

Fluoroscopic kinematic series of replaced knees are the evidence base for
whether a design achieves its intended centre path, in prescribed tasks.

Simulator testing compares designs under a repeatable profile and removes the
patient variability the clinical question depends on.

Navigation and robotic systems measure the axis intraoperatively in an
anaesthetised knee, which is a different soft tissue state from the
functioning one.

No routine postoperative measurement of the centrode exists, so design
arguments rest on laboratory kinematics and outcome data.

### Clinical Relevance

Set a hinged external fixator's axis to the epicondylar line, since an off
axis hinge translates the joint through range.

Expect deep flexion behaviour to differ between single and multi radius
designs on kinematic grounds.

Consider component position before design when a replaced knee's motion is
wrong.

Keep intraoperative axis measurement distinct from the functioning knee's
behaviour.

### Key Learning Points

- Single radius and multi radius designs disagree about the centre path; both approximate.
- Hinged implants abandon the native centrode deliberately for constraint.
- An off axis hinged fixator translates the joint while it flexes.
- A well designed implant placed off axis imposes the wrong path.
- No routine postoperative measurement of the centrode exists.
