# PCL Deficient Gait

The opposite adaptation to the cruciate on the other side, and the one that
loads the kneecap joint. Chapter 3.8 covers the ligament; this covers the
gait.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

If a restraint on one side of a joint is missing, a useful adaptation is to
use the muscle that pulls the other way.

That muscle then works harder than it was designed to, all day.

A protective adaptation that overworks something else is how one problem
becomes two.

### At the Knee

The posterior cruciate stops the shin sliding backwards. Section 6 covers the
injury.

The quadriceps pull the shin forwards, which is the direction the missing
ligament was protecting.

So a knee without a posterior cruciate tends to use the quadriceps more during
walking, which is the opposite of the anterior cruciate pattern. 3.14.9 covers
that one.

The cost is more load through the kneecap joint, because the quadriceps press
it against the femur. 3.12.3 covers those pressures.

3.8.7 covers what this does to the ligament and the joint; this page covers
the walking pattern.

### What Changes It

How much backward sag there is.

Quadriceps strength, which this adaptation depends on.

Whether other structures were injured at the same time.

Walking surface and slope, since downhill walking demands more.

### Clinical Relevance

Kneecap pain years after a posterior cruciate injury is a common and
mechanical story.

Strengthening the quadriceps is the main treatment and it is also what the
adaptation relies on. Section 8 covers it.

Many of these knees function well, which is why they are often managed without
surgery.

4.4.4 covers the gait examination.

### Key Learning Points

- The posterior cruciate stops the shin sliding backwards.
- The quadriceps pull it forwards, so they substitute for the missing ligament.
- This is the opposite adaptation to the anterior cruciate pattern.
- The cost is higher load through the patellofemoral joint.
- Kneecap pain years later is a common and mechanical story.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Where the anterior cruciate deficient knee reduces quadriceps demand, the
posterior cruciate deficient knee increases it. The two adaptations are mirror
images with asymmetrical costs.

The anterior cruciate adaptation costs disuse. The posterior cruciate
adaptation costs compartment load, which is cumulative.

That is why the long term consequences of the two injuries differ in location
as well as in degree.

### At the Knee

Posterior sag places the tibia posteriorly at rest, which shifts the extensor
mechanism's working position. 2.4.1 covers the patella.

Increased quadriceps activity through stance raises patellofemoral contact
force, and medial compartment load rises too.

The reported degenerative pattern after posterior cruciate deficiency is
medial and patellofemoral, which matches that mechanical account. 3.12.10
covers the wear.

Walking on level ground is often well tolerated; descending stairs and slopes
provoke the symptoms, because quadriceps demand rises. 3.3.12 covers stair
kinematics.

Chapter 3.8 covers the ligament's own mechanics and 3.8.7 the gait from that
perspective.

### What Changes It

Grade of posterior laxity and whether it is isolated or combined.

Posterolateral corner competence, since a combined injury behaves differently.
3.10.6 covers the corner.

Quadriceps strength, which the adaptation depends on and which therefore sets
function.

Activity profile, particularly stairs, slopes and squatting.

Tibial slope, which alters the sagittal equilibrium. 3.13.9 covers osteotomy.

### How It Is Measured

Three dimensional gait analysis, which shows the raised quadriceps demand and
altered sagittal kinematics. 3.16.7 covers it.

Stress radiography for posterior translation, which measures the passive
deficit. 5.3.2 covers alignment imaging.

Isokinetic quadriceps testing, which measures the capacity the adaptation
relies on.

Patellofemoral loading, which is modelled rather than measured. 3.16.4 covers
modelling.

This gait has a smaller literature than the anterior cruciate equivalent, in
proportion to the injury's frequency rather than its consequences.

### Clinical Relevance

Ask about stairs and slopes specifically, since level walking is often well
tolerated.

Measure quadriceps strength, because the adaptation depends on it and function
follows it.

Expect the degenerative pattern to be medial and patellofemoral rather than
diffuse. 3.12.10 covers the wear.

Assess the posterolateral corner, since a combined injury changes the picture.
3.10.6 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- The anterior cruciate adaptation reduces quadriceps demand; this one raises it.
- Its cost is compartment load rather than disuse, and that cost is cumulative.
- Patellofemoral and medial compartment load both rise.
- Level walking is often tolerated; stairs and slopes provoke symptoms.
- The literature is smaller, in proportion to the injury's frequency rather than its consequences.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The posterior cruciate deficient knee is the clearest example in this chapter
of an adaptation that works and bills later.

Quadriceps substitution restores function well enough that many of these knees
are managed without surgery, and the degenerative pattern that follows is
consistent with the loading that substitution produces.

That gives a mechanical argument for quadriceps rehabilitation as a treatment
rather than as an adjunct, and simultaneously an argument that the treatment
drives the long term cost.

Nobody has resolved that tension with evidence. There is no trial comparing
long term joint outcomes between reconstructed and rehabilitated posterior
cruciate deficiency.

Conventional teaching manages isolated grade one and two injuries non
operatively, and the degenerative consequence is accepted rather than
demonstrated to be avoidable.

### What Changes It

Whether the injury is isolated, which is the dividing line in practice. 3.10.6
covers the corner.

Tibial slope, which can be corrected and which changes the sagittal
equilibrium. 3.13.9 covers it.

Quadriceps capacity, which is both the treatment and the mechanism of the
cost.

Patient demand, which decides whether the adaptation is sufficient.

Time, since the degenerative pattern emerges over years rather than months.

### How It Is Measured

Gait analysis for the quadriceps demand and sagittal kinematics, which few of
these patients receive.

Serial radiography for the degenerative pattern, which is what the long term
series report.

Stress radiography for the passive deficit, which is the measurement most used
in decisions.

Modelled compartment loading, which connects the gait to the degeneration and
is inference rather than measurement. 3.16.4 covers it.

The causal chain from adaptation to degeneration is plausible, consistent and
not demonstrated prospectively.

### Clinical Relevance

Counsel that non operative management works through an adaptation that itself
loads the joint, rather than presenting it as cost free.

Measure and treat quadriceps capacity, which is the mechanism of function
here.

Consider slope in the patient with a poor result, since it changes the
sagittal equilibrium. 3.13.9 covers correction.

State that the link from adaptation to degeneration is plausible and not
prospectively demonstrated.

Weigh the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, since the choice here is
between a known operation and an accepted long term cost.

### Key Learning Points

- This is the clearest example of an adaptation that works and bills later.
- Quadriceps substitution restores function and produces the loading that follows.
- That argues for rehabilitation as treatment and implicates it in the long term cost.
- No trial compares long term joint outcomes between reconstruction and rehabilitation.
- The causal chain is plausible, consistent and not prospectively demonstrated.
