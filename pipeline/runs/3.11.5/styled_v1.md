# Internal Rotation Control

The motion the anterolateral structures restrain. It is shared with the
cruciate, and the sharing is why reconstruction leaves some of it behind.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A motion resisted by several structures is restored by replacing all of them,
and partly restored by replacing one.

The structure furthest from the axis of the motion resists it most
efficiently, because it has the longest lever arm.

A central structure therefore resists rotation less efficiently than a
peripheral one, whatever its strength.

### At the Knee

Internal rotation of the tibia is restrained by the anterior cruciate
centrally and the anterolateral structures peripherally. 3.7.7 covers
rotational stability.

The anterolateral structures have the longer lever arm, so they are efficient
rotational restraints despite being smaller. 3.4.4 covers lever arms.

Internal rotation is coupled to anterior translation, so the two are
restrained together. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

That coupling is why a cruciate injury produces rotational symptoms and why
rotational control is the harder half to restore.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, since the structures tighten at different angles.

Anterolateral complex integrity. 2.11.5 covers the complex.

Lateral meniscus posterior horn status. 3.6.5 covers meniscal load sharing.

Bony shape, which sets how much rotation a given load produces.

### Clinical Relevance

Patients describe rotational failure rather than translation, which is what
the anterolateral structures control. 3.7.1 covers the cruciate's functions.

Restoring rotational control is the aim of a lateral procedure. 3.11.7 covers
the tenodesis.

Over-constraint is the opposite error: a construct that limits internal
rotation below normal restricts the knee. 3.11.8 covers the combined
reconstruction.

The pivot shift is the clinical sign of this motion being uncontrolled. 3.11.4
covers pivot shift control.

### Key Learning Points

- A motion resisted by several structures is only partly restored by replacing one.
- The structure furthest from the axis resists rotation most efficiently.
- The anterolateral structures have the longer lever arm.
- Internal rotation is coupled to anterior translation.
- Rotational control is the harder half to restore.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The apportionment question is how much of internal rotation restraint is
anterolateral and how much cruciate, and the answer is angle dependent. 3.2.10
covers stability through range.

Conventional teaching gives the anterolateral structures an increasing share
as flexion increases, with the cruciate dominant near extension, which is the
reverse of what the pivot shift's position suggests and follows from the
structures' lines of action.

A construct therefore has to be judged at several angles, and a construct that
restores rotation at one angle can over-constrain at another.

### At the Knee

Sectioning the anterolateral structures in a cruciate deficient knee increases
internal rotation measurably, and sectioning them in an intact knee increases
it less. 3.11.3 covers the structures.

The lateral meniscus posterior horn contributes, which is why its repair has a
rotational rationale. 3.6.5 covers meniscal load sharing.

Over-constraint by a lateral construct is measured as internal rotation below
the intact knee's, and conventional teaching reports it as dependent on graft
tension and the rotation position at fixation.

Posterior tibial slope and lateral plateau convexity increase the rotation
produced by a given load. 3.13 covers alignment.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, through the shifting share.

Which structures are injured and which are reconstructed.

Graft tension and the rotation position at fixation.

Bony morphology.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing with an internal rotation torque measures the restraint at
several angles and is the standard.

Instrumented pivot shift measures the coupled event clinically, without a
threshold.

Computed tomography based rotation measurement quantifies it in research
settings.

No routine clinical instrument measures internal rotation laxity.

### Clinical Relevance

Judge a construct at several angles rather than one.

Fix with the tibia in neutral rather than internally rotated, to avoid
over-constraint.

Repair the lateral posterior horn, which has a rotational rationale.

Measure slope where rotation exceeds what the injury explains.

### Key Learning Points

- The apportionment is angle dependent.
- The anterolateral share increases with flexion; the cruciate dominates near extension.
- A construct restoring rotation at one angle can over-constrain at another.
- Over-constraint depends on graft tension and the rotation position at fixation.
- No routine clinical instrument measures internal rotation laxity.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat internal rotation control as the quantity the whole anterolateral debate
is about, and over-constraint as the cost that makes the debate a real one.
3.11.9 covers the controversies.

Conventional teaching reports that a tenodesis fixed with excessive tension or
with the tibia externally rotated restricts internal rotation and has been
associated with lateral compartment loading in cadaveric work. 3.11.7 covers
the tenodesis.

The clinical counterpart is that trials have not shown an excess of stiffness
or lateral compartment problems, which is the reassurance the mechanism's risk
needed. 3.11.8 covers the combined reconstruction.

### What Changes It

Graft tension and the rotation position at fixation.

Construct choice and routing.

Cruciate tunnel position, since the two constructs interact.

Bony morphology, which sets the demand.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing measures over-constraint directly against the intact knee.

Randomised trials measure stiffness and lateral compartment outcomes
clinically.

Instrumented pivot shift measures the intended effect.

No clinical measurement detects over-constraint before it becomes symptomatic.

### Clinical Relevance

Tension a lateral construct modestly and fix with the tibia neutral.

Expect cadaveric over-constraint risk not to have appeared as clinical
stiffness in trials.

Judge the construct by rotational control and by range together.

Plan the two constructs' tunnels together, since they interact.

### Key Learning Points

- Internal rotation control is what the anterolateral debate is about.
- Over-constraint is the cost that makes it a real debate.
- Excessive tension or external rotation at fixation restricts internal rotation.
- Trials have not shown an excess of stiffness or lateral compartment problems.
- No clinical measurement detects over-constraint before it is symptomatic.
