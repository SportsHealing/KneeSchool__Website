# Functional Range

The part of the range the patient actually uses, and the thresholds that make a
number mean something.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Functional range asks what the measured range allows the patient to do, which is
a different question from how many degrees it contains.

The reason it is a separate assessment is that the range needed for a task is
specific. Walking, stairs, sitting in a chair, driving, cycling, kneeling and
squatting each require a different amount. 3.3.11 covers kinematics during
squatting.

An angle is therefore only interpretable against the demand, which is why the
history of what the patient cannot do belongs alongside the measurement.
4.2.11 covers aggravating factors.

### How It Is Performed

Measure the active and passive ranges first, so the functional assessment has a
measurement to interpret. 4.7.1 covers active motion.

Ask what the patient cannot do, specifically, rather than whether the knee is
stiff.

Ask about the tasks that need progressively more flexion: sitting, stairs,
getting out of a car, cycling, kneeling and squatting.

Ask about tasks needing full extension: standing comfortably, walking without a
limp and lying flat.

Watch them perform the task that matters most to them, which is more informative
than any angle. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing.

### What a Positive Means

A range that measures adequately and does not allow the patient's necessary
tasks indicates either pain limiting the use of the range or a task needing more
than the measurement suggests. 4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

An extension deficit is functionally more significant than an equivalent flexion
deficit, because a knee that cannot straighten alters gait with every step.
4.4.1 covers normal gait.

A loss of deep flexion matters to specific patients far more than to others, so
a figure that is acceptable in one patient is disabling in another.

A patient whose occupation or religious practice requires kneeling has a
specific requirement that an angle does not convey, and recording it changes the
plan. 4.1.8 covers the record.

A range that is adequate for every task the patient needs is a normal functional
range, whatever the figure says. 4.7.1 covers the measurement.

### Key Learning Points

- The range needed for a task is specific, so an angle is interpretable only against the demand.
- An extension deficit matters more functionally than an equivalent flexion deficit.
- A knee that cannot straighten alters gait with every step.
- A figure acceptable in one patient is disabling in another.
- A range adequate for every needed task is normal, whatever the figure says.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected answer links the measurement to the task, and a candidate
who gives a figure and stops has answered half the question.

The expected points are the measured ranges, the specific tasks asked about, the
observation of the task that matters, and the record of the requirement.

Say that you would ask what the patient cannot do rather than whether the knee
feels stiff, since the first question gets a usable answer.

Say that an extension deficit matters more than a flexion deficit of the same
size, and say why. 4.4.1 covers normal gait.

Say that you would record the patient's specific requirement, because a
functional target is a better outcome measure than a number. 4.22.8 covers the
scores built on that idea.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that different tasks load the knee through different
arcs, so a restriction matters in proportion to the tasks it removes.
3.3.12 covers kinematics during stair climbing.

Extension deficits alter gait because the stance phase requires the knee to
approach full extension, so a small deficit has a consequence at every step.
3.14.2 covers the stance phase.

Flexion deficits remove specific tasks rather than altering a continuous
activity, which is why their significance varies so much between patients.
3.3.11 covers squatting.

Pain can remove the functional use of a range that is mechanically available,
which is why a measured range and a usable range differ. 4.7.6 covers the
painful arc.

### How Good It Is

The functional assessment correlates with the patient's experience better than
any measured range and is less reproducible between examiners, which is the
familiar trade off in this section.

Patient reported outcome scores capture the same ground more reproducibly and
less specifically, and the two are complementary. 4.22.8 covers their use.

It is the assessment that makes a range figure meaningful, which is a real
contribution and is often left out of a letter.

It cannot be compared between patients, only within one, which limits its use in
research and not its use in a clinic. 4.1.8 covers the record.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Different tasks load the knee through different arcs, so a restriction matters in proportion to the tasks it removes.
- Extension deficits alter gait, so a small deficit has a consequence at every step.
- Flexion deficits remove specific tasks, which is why their significance varies.
- Pain can remove the functional use of a mechanically available range.
- It cannot be compared between patients, only within one.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the functional range decides whether a measured restriction is
worth treating, which is the question a figure cannot answer.

A patient with a measured flexion deficit who needs none of the tasks it removes
does not need an operation for it, and offering one is treating a number.
4.7.4 covers the measurement.

A patient with a small extension deficit who limps has a functionally
significant restriction, and treating it is justified on a smaller figure than
would justify treating flexion. 4.4.1 covers normal gait.

In planning an arthroplasty or a release, the patient's specific requirement sets
the target, and the achievable target has to be discussed against it rather than
against the other knee. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

After surgery, a functional target reached is a better outcome than a range
figure achieved, and recording both makes the follow up meaningful. 4.7.10
covers post-operative range.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording a range and not asking what it prevents.

The second is treating a figure as the problem, which produces operations for
numbers.

The third is assuming an extension deficit and a flexion deficit of the same
size matter equally.

The fourth is promising a range rather than a function, which sets an
expectation the operation may not meet.

The fifth is comparing a patient's range with a population normal rather than
with their own requirement.

### Key Learning Points

- The functional range decides whether a measured restriction is worth treating.
- A flexion deficit removing no needed task does not justify an operation.
- A small extension deficit with a limp justifies treatment on a smaller figure.
- The patient's requirement sets the target, not the other knee.
- Promising a range rather than a function sets an expectation the operation may not meet.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the functional range is where a dissatisfied patient with a
good looking knee is finally understood.

A patient with a textbook range after a reconstruction who cannot do the one
thing they need is dissatisfied for a reason the measurements do not record, and
the referral is often framed as unexplained. 4.22.6 covers the score built for
that question.

A patient whose occupation requires deep flexion or kneeling has a requirement
that should have set the pre-operative conversation, and discovering it
afterwards is the recurring failure. 4.1.8 covers the record.

A patient with a small extension deficit and a persisting limp has a functional
problem worth treating, and it is often dismissed because the figure looks
acceptable. 4.4.7 covers post-operative gait.

In a multiply operated knee the achievable functional range may be well below
the patient's requirement, and establishing that before any further surgery is
the kindest part of the assessment. 4.10.12 covers revision assessment.

In medicolegal work the functional range is what a claim concerns and the
measured range is what the record contains, and bridging the two honestly is the
work of the opinion. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A textbook range with one necessary task missing explains the unexplained referral.
- An occupational requirement should set the pre-operative conversation.
- A small extension deficit with a limp is worth treating and is often dismissed.
- In a multiply operated knee the achievable range may be below the requirement.
- A claim concerns the functional range and the record contains the measured one.
