# Innervation

The nerve supply of the ligament and the receptors in it, which make it a
sensory organ as well as a restraint.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The anterior cruciate ligament is innervated. That is the fact this page
exists to establish, because a ligament is usually taught as a passive strap.

Nerve fibres reach it from the posterior articular branches of the tibial
nerve, which pierce the posterior capsule alongside the middle genicular
artery and run in the synovial sheath.

The fibres travel along the ligament in the subsynovial tissue rather than
within the collagen bundles, and they are concentrated near both insertions.

Conventional teaching has neural elements occupying only about 1 per cent of
the ligament's cross sectional area, which is a small proportion for a
structure with a sensory role.

### Relations

The nerves share the route of the artery: posterior capsule, notch, synovial
sheath. Stripping the sheath removes both.

The receptors sit in the subsynovial layer at each end of the ligament, where
deformation is greatest when the ligament is loaded.

Free nerve endings are distributed more widely than the encapsulated
receptors.

### Function

Two kinds of sensation. Encapsulated mechanoreceptors signal position and
movement; free nerve endings signal pain and respond to inflammation.

The mechanoreceptors contribute to joint position sense and to reflex muscle
activity, so the ligament is part of a control loop rather than only a
mechanical restraint. 2.7.10 covers that.

The free nerve endings explain why an intact but inflamed ligament can hurt
and why the notch is a source of pain.

### Clinical Relevance

Rupture denervates as well as destabilises, and the two happen together
because the route is shared with the blood supply.

That is part of why reconstruction restores mechanical stability faster than
it restores neuromuscular control, and why rehabilitation continues long after
the graft is secure.

A reconstructed ligament is reinnervated to some degree over time, and the
extent is debated. The anatomical point is that it starts with none.

### Key Learning Points

- Innervation comes from posterior articular branches of the tibial nerve through the posterior capsule.
- Fibres run in the subsynovial tissue and concentrate near both insertions.
- Conventional teaching gives neural elements as about 1 per cent of cross sectional area.
- Encapsulated receptors signal position and movement; free endings signal pain.
- Rupture denervates and destabilises together, because the route is shared with the artery.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Name the receptor types. Ruffini endings, slowly adapting, signalling joint
position and the direction of movement. Golgi type endings, high threshold and
slowly adapting, signalling extremes of movement and tension. Pacinian
corpuscles, rapidly adapting, signalling acceleration. Free nerve endings,
nociceptive and also responsive to inflammatory mediators.

They are not evenly distributed. Density is highest in the subsynovial tissue
near both insertions, and the femoral end is often described as the richer of
the two.

The receptors lie outside the collagen fascicles. They sense deformation of
the ligament transmitted through the surrounding tissue rather than strain
within a fibre.

### Relations

The posterior articular branches of the tibial nerve run with the middle
genicular artery through the posterior capsule. The obturator nerve
contributes to the capsule medially and the common peroneal recurrent branch
laterally, but the cruciates are tibial nerve territory.

The synovial sheath carries both vessels and nerves, so remnant preservation
preserves both and debridement removes both.

The fat pad is separately and richly innervated, which matters because it is a
competing source of anterior knee pain in the same region.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The vascular and neural anatomy of this ligament is one anatomy, not two. One
route, one sheath, one set of consequences.

Receptor density falls with age, alongside the fall in vascularity and
cellularity. The ligament of a fifty year old is a different sensory structure
from that of a twenty year old.

Reinnervation of a graft occurs from the surrounding synovium and fat pad
rather than from the original route, which is why it is partial and slow.

### Function

Position sense, movement sense and a reflex contribution to muscle activity.
The proposed reflex arc runs from the ligament's receptors to the hamstrings,
and is the basis of the protective reflex argument on 2.7.10.

Pain signalling through free nerve endings, which is why a partially torn
ligament hurts and a chronically absent one often does not.

The ligament is part of the knee's sensory apparatus, and 8.10 covers the
rehabilitation consequences.

### Clinical Relevance

Explain to a patient that an anterior cruciate rupture removes a sensor as
well as a strap, because it makes the length of rehabilitation intelligible
rather than arbitrary.

Remnant preservation at reconstruction has a proprioceptive rationale in
addition to a vascular one, and the cost is visualisation of the footprint.

Chronic cruciate deficiency is often painless, which follows from the free
nerve endings having gone with the ligament. Absence of pain is not evidence
of a stable knee.

The proportion figure on this page is a standard teaching value, registered
and unchecked. Histological series report receptor density very differently.

### Key Learning Points

- Four receptor types: Ruffini, Golgi type, Pacinian and free nerve endings.
- Density is highest near both insertions, often described as richer at the femoral end.
- Receptors lie outside the fascicles and sense transmitted deformation.
- Vascular and neural anatomy here are one anatomy with one route.
- Chronic deficiency is often painless, so absence of pain is not evidence of stability.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The sheath is the structure that carries the sensory function, and it is the
structure most readily sacrificed for a clear view of the footprint. That is
the trade in remnant preserving reconstruction, stated anatomically.

The fat pad, separately innervated and in the same region, is routinely
disturbed and is a confounder in any discussion of postoperative anterior knee
pain.

### Function

Mechanical reconstruction and sensory reconstruction are not the same thing
and do not happen on the same timescale. A graft is mechanically useful within
months and sensorily incomplete for longer, possibly permanently.

That gap is a reasonable explanation for why return to sport criteria based on
stability alone perform poorly, and 8.10 and Section 11 own that argument.

### Clinical Relevance

Use the sensory anatomy when counselling on timescales. A patient who
understands that a sensor is being regrown as well as a strap replaced accepts
a longer programme.

Remnant preservation is a defensible choice on vascular and neural grounds,
and the evidence for a functional difference is not settled. Section 7 owns
the comparison.

Neuromuscular rehabilitation addresses the deficit described here rather than
the graft, which is why it continues after the graft is mechanically secure.

No receptor density figure is quotable from here. The one proportion on this
page is conventional teaching and the series behind it disagree.

### Key Learning Points

- The sheath carries the sensory function and is the thing sacrificed for a clear footprint view.
- Mechanical and sensory recovery run on different timescales.
- The sensory gap is a reasonable explanation for stability based return to sport criteria performing poorly.
- Neuromuscular rehabilitation addresses the sensory deficit, not the graft.
