# MRI Anatomy

What a normal lateral meniscus looks like on magnetic resonance imaging, and
the four normal structures around it that are mistaken for tears.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

A normal lateral meniscus is uniformly dark on every sequence, like the
medial. The appearance conventions on 2.5.5 apply to both.

Two differences are visible. Its anterior and posterior horns are of similar
size, whereas the medial posterior horn is clearly larger than its anterior
horn. And it is more nearly circular, so it appears as a continuous band on
more sagittal slices.

On a coronal image through the mid joint, the lateral meniscus appears as a
dark wedge at the lateral margin, slightly thicker than its medial counterpart
at the same level.

### Relations

The popliteus tendon appears as a dark round structure immediately
posterolateral to the posterior horn, in its sheath. The gap between the two
is the popliteus hiatus and it is a normal finding.

The meniscofemoral ligaments are seen as small dark bands running from the
posterior horn towards the medial femoral condyle, in front of and behind the
posterior cruciate.

The transverse intermeniscal ligament crosses in front of both anterior horns.

The lateral collateral ligament is seen peripherally and does not attach to
the meniscus.

### Function

The imaging questions are the same as for the medial meniscus: intact or torn,
where, and whether the tear reaches a surface.

One extra question applies here. A lateral meniscus that is too large or
covers too much of the plateau is discoid, and that is assessed on the coronal
and sagittal images together.

### Clinical Relevance

Four normal structures around the lateral posterior horn can mimic a tear: the
popliteus tendon and hiatus, the two meniscofemoral ligaments, and the
transverse ligament anteriorly. Knowing them by name is most of what prevents
overcalling.

Criteria for calling a tear, for grading signal and for diagnosing a discoid
meniscus belong to 5.6 and 5.6.2.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal lateral meniscus is uniformly dark with horns of similar size.
- It appears as a continuous band on more sagittal slices, because it is nearly circular.
- The popliteus tendon and hiatus sit immediately posterolateral to the posterior horn and are normal.
- Four normal structures around the posterior horn mimic tears, and naming them prevents overcalling.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Count the bow ties here too, but expect more of them than on the medial side,
because the meniscus is wider relative to its plateau and more nearly
circular.

On sagittal images the posterior horn is seen with the popliteus tendon behind
it and the hiatus between. Moving medially, the meniscofemoral ligaments
appear alongside the posterior cruciate.

On coronal images follow the meniscus front to back as on the medial side, and
assess the ratio of meniscal width to plateau width, which is how discoid
shape is judged. The criteria belong to 5.6.

The anterior horn is seen close to the anterior cruciate footprint, and the
two can appear continuous on the slices where the root attaches.

### Relations

The popliteus hiatus is the structure most often reported as a tear. It is a
gap in the capsular attachment, it contains tendon and fluid, and it is
normal. Its position posterolateral to the posterior horn is the identifying
feature.

The meniscofemoral ligaments produce an oblique line adjacent to the posterior
horn, the pseudotear. Tracing the structure to the medial femoral condyle
identifies it.

The transverse intermeniscal ligament inserting on the anterior horn produces
the same pitfall anteriorly as it does medially.

The popliteomeniscal fascicles are thin and inconsistently seen. Their absence
on imaging is not a reliable sign of a fascicle tear, which is why
hypermobility is diagnosed arthroscopically.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

As on the medial side, vascularity is not directly assessable. The watershed
at the hiatus is anatomical rather than visible.

Peripheral enhancement after injury may suggest a vascular response, and the
zone is still assessed at arthroscopy.

Intrasubstance signal reflects water content and tissue change rather than
perfusion, and it increases with age in both menisci.

### Function

Sagittal images define which part of the ring is involved; coronal images
define radial extent, peripheral attachment and discoid shape.

The region that most needs good imaging is the posterolateral periphery,
because it is the region the arthroscope sees worst and the region with the
most normal structures to confuse.

Thin slices matter more here than on the medial side, for the same reason.

### Clinical Relevance

Learn the four pitfalls and trace each structure to its other end before
calling a tear. The hiatus to the popliteus muscle, the meniscofemoral
ligaments to the medial condyle, the transverse ligament to the opposite
anterior horn.

A discoid meniscus is a shape diagnosis made across both planes and is
commonly associated with abnormal peripheral attachment, which imaging
assesses poorly.

A lateral posterior root tear is harder to detect than a medial one, because
extrusion is held in check by the other tethers. Suspicion based on the
cruciate injury matters more than the imaging sign.

Signal grading, tear criteria and discoid classification all belong to 5.6 and
5.6.2. This page stops at the normal appearance, which is what the
architecture assigns it.

### Key Learning Points

- Expect more bow tie slices than on the medial side, because the meniscus is nearly circular.
- The hiatus is the structure most often reported as a tear, and its position identifies it.
- Trace each suspicious structure to its other end before calling a tear.
- Fascicle absence on imaging is not a reliable sign; hypermobility is an arthroscopic diagnosis.
- Lateral root tears are harder to detect because extrusion is held in check.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The posterolateral periphery is where imaging and arthroscopy disagree most
often, because imaging sees it better than the arthroscope does while also
containing the structures most likely to be misread.

That combination means the imaging report and the arthroscopic finding should
each be read in the light of the other rather than one overruling the other.

### Function

Imaging decides whether to operate and informs the plan; it does not decide
the operation on this side, because peripheral attachment and mobility are not
reliably assessable.

A plan for a lateral meniscal procedure should therefore include the
possibility of finding an attachment problem rather than a tear.

### Clinical Relevance

Before lateral meniscal surgery, assess shape, root integrity and the
posterolateral periphery, and plan to test mobility whatever the imaging says.

In a young patient with lateral symptoms and normal imaging, the differential
includes a hypermobile meniscus, and imaging will not settle it.

In discoid meniscus, expect the peripheral attachment to be abnormal more
often than imaging suggests, and plan stabilisation as a possibility rather
than a surprise.

No measurement appears on this page. Every discoid criterion and every signal
threshold is a measured value owned by Section 5.

### Key Learning Points

- Imaging sees the posterolateral periphery better than the arthroscope and also misreads it more.
- Peripheral attachment and mobility are not reliably assessable on imaging.
- Plan a lateral meniscal procedure to allow for an attachment problem rather than a tear.
- In discoid meniscus, expect abnormal peripheral attachment more often than imaging suggests.
