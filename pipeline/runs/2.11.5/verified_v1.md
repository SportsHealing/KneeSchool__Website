# Anterolateral Complex

Three structures, one function and a long argument about names. What the
anterolateral knee does, taken as a unit rather than as a dispute.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The anterolateral complex is the name given to the structures at the front and
outside of the knee that restrain internal rotation of the tibia, taken
together.

Three are usually included: the deep and capsulo-osseous layers of the
iliotibial band, the Kaplan fibres that anchor them to the femur, and the
anterolateral ligament. 2.11.1, 2.11.2 and 2.11.3 cover each.

They occupy the same region, from the distal lateral femur to the
anterolateral tibia between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head.

They are close enough together that a dissection can take two of them as one,
which is the root of the disagreement about how many structures there are.

The complex exists as a name because the function is agreed and the anatomy is
not.

### Relations

In front: the lateral retinaculum and the patellofemoral structures, which
2.12 covers.

Behind: the lateral collateral ligament and the posterolateral corner, which
2.10 covers.

Deep: the lateral meniscus and the joint.

Superficial: the iliotibial band, which is both part of the complex and the
layer covering it.

### Function

The complex restrains internal rotation of the tibia, particularly in flexion.

It works with the anterior cruciate ligament. The cruciate is the primary
restraint to anterior translation and rotation; this complex is secondary and
becomes important when the cruciate has failed.

The pivot shift is the movement both control, and 3.11.4 covers the test.

### Clinical Relevance

A pivot shift that persists after a sound cruciate reconstruction points here.

The complex is why lateral extra-articular procedures exist, and 7.30 covers
them.

The argument about whether the anterolateral ligament is a ligament has no
effect on what the region does. Function first, names second.

### Key Learning Points

- Three structures: the deep iliotibial band layers, the Kaplan fibres and the anterolateral ligament.
- They occupy one region from the distal lateral femur to the anterolateral tibia.
- They restrain internal rotation, particularly in flexion.
- They are secondary to the anterior cruciate and matter most when it has failed.
- A persistent pivot shift after cruciate reconstruction points here.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Set out the components and what each contributes. The capsulo-osseous layer is
the deepest and most posterior part of the iliotibial band and carries a large
share of the rotational restraint. The Kaplan fibres anchor the band to the
distal femur. The anterolateral ligament is the capsular thickening running to
the tibia with a meniscal attachment on the way.

They are not in series or in parallel in any simple sense. They share
attachments, overlap in layers, and load together.

Conventional teaching gives the whole complex a span from about 50 mm above
the lateral epicondyle to about 7 mm below the joint line, which is a region
rather than a structure.

Studies disagree on component boundaries and agree on the region. That pattern
is itself evidence about what kind of thing this is.

### Relations

The region occupies layers one and three laterally, in the scheme 2.10.4 uses,
with the deep iliotibial band layers in one and the anterolateral ligament in
three.

Gerdy's tubercle is the anterior bony reference and the fibular head the
posterior one. Everything in the complex attaches between or above them.

The lateral inferior genicular artery crosses the field near the tibial
attachments.

The lateral collateral ligament is not part of the complex. It runs to the
fibula and restrains varus, which 2.10.1 covers.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Supply across the region is from the lateral superior and lateral inferior
genicular arteries, with the superior serving the femoral attachments and the
inferior the tibial ones.

No component has a supply that distinguishes it from its neighbours, which is
another reason separating them anatomically has proved difficult.

Nerve supply is from the lateral articular branches and the recurrent
articular branch of the common peroneal nerve.

### Function

Sectioning studies agree on the direction and disagree on the share. Cutting
any component increases internal rotation in a cruciate deficient knee; which
one matters most depends on the order of cutting and the flexion angle tested.
3.11.5 carries that.

The complex contributes little in an intact knee and a great deal in a
cruciate deficient one, which defines it as a secondary restraint.

Its contribution rises with flexion and with internal rotation together, the
combination a pivot shift produces.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess the pivot shift grade rather than anterior translation alone when the
anterolateral side is in question, because that is the movement this complex
controls.

Expect a high grade pivot shift to indicate injury here as well as to the
cruciate.

Consider a lateral extra-articular procedure in a revision, in a high grade
pivot shift, or in generalised laxity. 3.11.8 covers the indications.

Avoid insisting on one component's primacy. The sectioning order decides the
answer, and that is a property of the experiment rather than of the knee.

### Key Learning Points

- The components share attachments, overlap in layers and load together.
- The region spans about 50 mm above the epicondyle to about 7 mm below the joint line.
- Cutting order and flexion angle decide which component looks most important.
- It contributes little in an intact knee and a great deal in a cruciate deficient one.
- Its contribution rises with flexion and internal rotation together.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

Treat the region as the unit of planning. Fixation on the femur sits at or
above the supracondylar ridge, proximal to the collateral ligament, and
fixation on the tibia sits between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head.
Those two points define every procedure in this field whatever it is called.

The region abuts the posterolateral corner behind it and the retinaculum in
front, so a construct here has neighbours on both sides.

The common peroneal nerve is not in this field. It lies behind the fibular
head and behind biceps, which is one of the few things that makes
anterolateral surgery simpler than posterolateral surgery.

### Function

Treat the complex as the secondary rotational restraint whose role is revealed
by cruciate failure. That framing is agreed across the literature and survives
every disagreement about components.

Its loading pattern explains the persistent pivot shift: a cruciate graft
restores translation and a complex that has been injured or divided does not
restore rotation.

Over-constraint is the failure mode. The region has little compliance, and a
stiff graft tensioned in extension removes rotation rather than restraining
it.

### Clinical Relevance

Grade the pivot shift and record the grade, because it is the one examination
finding that reflects this region.

Plan an anterolateral construct from the two reliable points, the
supracondylar ridge and the tibia between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular
head.

Consider the indication rather than the anatomy when deciding whether to add a
lateral procedure, since the anatomy supports it and the indication is what is
argued. 3.11.9 carries the controversies.

Tension in flexion with the tibia in neutral rotation, because tensioning in
extension is how over-constraint happens.

### Key Learning Points

- Two points define every procedure here: the supracondylar ridge and the tibia between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head.
- The peroneal nerve is not in this field, which makes it simpler than the posterolateral one.
- A cruciate graft restores translation; an injured complex still fails to restore rotation.
- Over-constraint is the failure mode, because the region has little compliance.
- The anatomy supports the procedure; the indication is what is argued.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

The complex is a case study in what anatomy is for. A century of disagreement
about whether there is a ligament there has not changed the operation, because
the operation targets a region and a function. Where a name does not change a
decision, the argument is academic in the precise sense.

Indication is the open question. The anatomy says there is something to
reconstruct; the evidence on who benefits is what 3.11.8 and 3.11.9 carry, and
it moves. A fellowship level position on this region is a position on the
evidence rather than on the dissection.

Over-constraint deserves more attention than it gets. The region is short and
stiff, and a graft here is stiffer still. A knee tensioned in extension with
the tibia externally rotated can be left with measurably less rotation than
the other side, and the patient reports that as a knee that does not feel like
theirs rather than as instability.

Revision is where this anatomy earns its place. A persistent pivot shift after
a technically sound graft has few explanations, and a divided or injured
anterolateral complex is the commonest of them. 2.11.2 covers how routinely it
is divided and how rarely that is recorded.

The patient with a Segond fragment is the one case where the anatomy is
demonstrated rather than assumed. 2.11.4 covers what the fragment carries, and
it is worth saying that this is the only direct evidence of the attachment
anyone sees outside a dissection room.

### Key Learning Points

- A century of naming disagreement has not changed the operation, because it targets a region.
- A fellowship position here is a position on the evidence, not on the dissection.
- Over-constraint presents as a knee that does not feel like the patient's own.
- A divided complex is the commonest explanation for a pivot shift after a sound graft.
- A Segond fragment is the only direct evidence of the attachment outside a dissection room.
