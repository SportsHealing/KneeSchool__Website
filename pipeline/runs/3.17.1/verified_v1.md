# ACL Landmark Studies

The studies that turned the anterior cruciate from a structure with a name
into a structure with measured behaviour, and what each one left unanswered.

## FRCS Level

### What the Question Was

Four questions drove the ACL literature in sequence, and the order matters
because each one only became askable once the one before it had an answer.

What is the ligament made of and how is it arranged. What motion does it
restrain, and how much of that restraint is its own. How much load does it
carry during real activity. And what does a reconstruction have to reproduce.

Anatomy came first because it is the only one answerable by dissection alone.
Loading during activity came last because it needed instrumentation that did
not exist earlier.

3.7.1 covers what the ligament does. This page covers how that came to be
known.

### What Was Done

Girgis and colleagues described the cruciate anatomy from cadaveric dissection
in the 1970s, including the bundle arrangement and the way tension shifts
between bundles through flexion.

Noyes worked on the mechanical properties of the ligament itself in the same
decade, including the effect of loading rate and of specimen age on failure
load and failure pattern.

Butler, Noyes and Grood ran the sequential sectioning study that produced the
primary and secondary restraint framework in the 1980s: load the knee, cut one
structure, load it again, and attribute the difference.

Markolf measured in situ force in the ligament directly rather than inferring
it from displacement, which made it possible to ask what a given surgical or
rehabilitation manoeuvre actually loads.

Beynnon and Fleming took that question into living knees with implanted strain
transducers, measuring ACL strain during the exercises patients were being
given.

### What It Showed

The ligament is not a single cord. It has bundles whose tension changes with
flexion angle, which is the anatomical basis for every bundle argument in
3.7.4.

The ACL is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation and supplies
most of that restraint by itself, with the secondary restraints contributing
in a way that depends on flexion angle.

Failure load falls with specimen age, and the figures quoted for a young
ligament and an elderly one differ enough that the two should never be used
interchangeably.

In vivo strain during open chain knee extension against resistance is
substantial near full extension, and closed chain loading produces less ACL
strain than the open chain equivalent at the same angles.

The pivot shift is not a single plane phenomenon, and a reconstruction that
controls anterior translation may leave rotation uncontrolled. 3.7.9 covers
the mechanics.

### What It Changed

The restraint framework is why examination is taught structure by structure
rather than as a global impression of looseness.

The age dependence of failure load changed graft selection arguments, because
a graft is being compared against a specific ligament rather than against the
ligament.

The in vivo strain work changed rehabilitation directly. The shift towards
closed chain exercise early after reconstruction rests on it.

Bundle anatomy drove two decades of tunnel position argument, and tunnel
position is now the part of the operation most strongly associated with
failure.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL literature answers four questions in sequence: structure, restraint, in vivo load, and what a reconstruction must reproduce.
- Sequential sectioning is the design that attributes a motion to a structure, and it is the basis of the primary and secondary restraint framework.
- Failure load figures are specimen specific; age changes them enough to change the argument.
- The shift to closed chain early rehabilitation came from in vivo strain measurement, not from outcome trials.
- Anterior control and rotational control are separate achievements, and a reconstruction can have one without the other.

## Fellowship Level

### What Was Done

Read the method before the number on every one of these studies, because the
method sets what the number can be used for.

A sectioning study gives causal attribution and no magnitude that transfers to
a patient, since the specimen has no muscle. 3.16.1 covers the limits in full.

A tensile test to failure gives a material property and says nothing about
behaviour in the physiological range, which is where the ligament actually
works.

An in vivo strain study gives physiology and loses control: the sample is
small, the transducer is invasive, and the activities are chosen for safety
rather than for risk.

A robotic study gives repeatability that a hand loaded rig cannot, which is
why force measurements from the 1990s onwards are more comparable than those
before. 3.16.2 covers the method.

### What It Showed

Where the literature is strong: the restraint hierarchy, the bundle
arrangement, and the relative strain of common rehabilitation exercises.

Where it is weak: the magnitude of load during the activities that actually
cause injury, because nobody instruments a cutting manoeuvre at injury
intensity in a living knee.

Where it is contested: how much rotational control an anatomic single bundle
reconstruction achieves, and whether adding a lateral extra-articular
procedure changes it in a way that matters clinically.

The injury mechanism literature is built from video analysis and from
modelling rather than from measurement, which is a weaker evidence base than
the restraint work it sits beside. 3.7.14 covers the mechanisms.

### What to Read Now

Read the sectioning studies for attribution and the robotic studies for
magnitude, and do not mix the two.

Read the in vivo strain work before giving a rehabilitation protocol, because
the protocol is where that evidence actually applies.

Treat any single figure for ACL failure load as meaningless without the
specimen age and the loading rate beside it.

Expect the rotational control question to stay open, and read new work on it
for the control condition rather than for the conclusion.

### Key Learning Points

- The method sets what a number can be used for, and these studies use five different methods.
- The restraint hierarchy is the best established finding in the chapter; in vivo injury load is the least established.
- A failure load figure without specimen age and loading rate beside it cannot be interpreted.
- Injury mechanism evidence is video and modelling, which is weaker than the laboratory work it is quoted alongside.

## Consultant Perspective

### What It Changed

The decision this literature actually informs is not whether to reconstruct
but what to reproduce, and the evidence supports a weighing rather than a
rule.

On one side, the bundle and tunnel work argues for reproducing the native
footprint as closely as the anatomy allows, and tunnel malposition is the
failure mode most strongly supported by the restraint literature.

On the other, the in vivo and outcome literature does not show that small
differences in tunnel position change what a patient reports, and the
technical complexity of chasing them carries its own risk.

Factor in the patient's age and physiology, since the failure load work says
the ligament being matched is not the same ligament at twenty and at fifty.
Factor in comorbidities that change healing and rehabilitation tolerance.
Factor in the demands they place on the knee, because a pivoting athlete and a
cyclist are asking different things of the same construct. Factor in their
psychological state, since fear of reinjury predicts return to sport more
strongly than laxity does. And elicit their own ideas, concerns and
expectations, because a patient who expects the knee to feel normal and a
patient who expects it to feel stable are measuring different outcomes.

Weigh those together rather than applying the laboratory finding directly, and
record which way the balance fell and why.

### What to Read Now

The honest summary for a trainee asking what to read is that the anatomy and
restraint work is settled enough to teach, and everything downstream of it is
argued.

Read the contested literature for its control group, because the rotational
control question has been answered differently by studies whose difference is
in the comparator rather than in the finding.

Say plainly, when teaching this, that the best established findings in ACL
biomechanics come from knees with no muscle, no pain and no healing, and that
this is a limit on the whole field rather than a flaw in individual papers.

### Key Learning Points

- The decision these studies inform is what a reconstruction should reproduce, and it is a balance rather than a rule.
- Tunnel position has the strongest mechanical support and the weakest patient reported signal, which is the tension to weigh.
- The five patient factors change the answer more than the laboratory evidence does.
- Read contested work for its comparator, since that is usually where the disagreement sits.
- The field's best evidence comes from specimens with no muscle, and teaching should say so.
