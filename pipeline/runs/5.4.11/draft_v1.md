# MPFL

The medial patellofemoral ligament, thin and superficial, which suits ultrasound
and rarely changes the plan.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The medial patellofemoral ligament runs from the medial femur to the medial
patellar border and restrains lateral patellar translation. 2.12.2 covers the
MPFL anatomy.

It is a thin superficial structure within easy reach of a high frequency probe.
5.2.7 covers the frequency selection.

It matters because it is the structure that fails in a patellar dislocation, and
its femoral attachment is where failure most often occurs. 5.12.5 covers MPFL
injury.

### How It Is Acquired

Scan transversely across the medial patellar border and follow the ligament
towards the femur. 2.12.2 covers the MPFL anatomy.

Identify the femoral attachment specifically, which is the commonest site of
failure. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

Keep the beam perpendicular, because the ligament is thin and anisotropic. 5.2.10
covers anisotropy.

Look at the adjacent retinaculum, which contributes to the same restraint. 2.12.4
covers the retinacula.

Apply a gentle lateral glide while scanning where the question is restraint.
5.4.14 covers patellar tracking.

### What Normal Looks Like

A thin bright band running from the medial patella towards the femur. 2.12.2
covers the MPFL anatomy.

A clean femoral attachment without fluid. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

An intact adjacent retinaculum. 2.12.4 covers the retinacula.

No excessive lateral patellar translation on a gentle glide. 5.4.14 covers
patellar tracking.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Thickening or discontinuity of the band. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

Fluid or disruption at the femoral attachment. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

An avulsed fragment from the medial patellar border. 5.3.14 covers avulsion
injuries.

Excessive lateral translation on a gentle glide, with the ligament not
restraining. 5.4.14 covers patellar tracking.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament restrains lateral patellar translation.
- It is thin and superficial, within reach of a high frequency probe.
- The femoral attachment is the commonest site of failure.
- The adjacent retinaculum contributes to the same restraint.
- A gentle lateral glide tests restraint during scanning.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the structure to name when asked what fails in a first patellar
dislocation.

The expected points are the transverse approach, the femoral attachment, the
perpendicular beam, the retinaculum, and the dynamic glide.

Say that the femoral attachment is where failure usually occurs. 5.12.5 covers
MPFL injury.

Say that the decision in instability rests on the bony measurements rather than on
this ligament. 5.16.4 covers the tuberosity to groove measurement.

Say that a magnetic resonance study gives the ligament and the bony anatomy
together. 5.12.1 covers trochlear dysplasia.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the ligament is the main passive restraint to
lateral translation near extension, so its failure is what allows a dislocation
rather than being a consequence of one. 3.12.5 covers the MPFL function.

The femoral attachment is where the strain concentrates, which is why it is the
usual failure site. 2.12.2 covers the MPFL anatomy.

The ligament's state does not by itself determine treatment, because recurrence
risk is driven by the bony anatomy the ultrasound cannot measure. 5.12.1 covers
trochlear dysplasia.

That makes this a structure ultrasound images well and a question it does not
answer. 5.16.4 covers the tuberosity to groove measurement.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound shows this ligament clearly and contributes little to the instability
decision.

Its best contribution is confirming an acute femoral sided disruption, which is a
structural finding available quickly. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

It is limited because the operative decision depends on trochlear shape, patellar
height and tuberosity position, none of which it measures. 5.16.4 covers the
tuberosity to groove measurement.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
translation or thickness measurements appear either: those are published
quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament is the main passive restraint to lateral translation near extension.
- Its failure allows a dislocation rather than following one.
- Strain concentrates at the femoral attachment.
- Recurrence risk is driven by bony anatomy ultrasound cannot measure.
- Imaged well, and the question is answered elsewhere.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this ligament's state is one input among several and not the
decisive one.

An acute femoral sided disruption confirms the mechanism, which is useful in a
first dislocation where the history is unclear. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

The recurrence decision rests on trochlear dysplasia, patellar height and
tuberosity position, which need cross sectional imaging. 5.12.1 covers trochlear
dysplasia.

A medial patellar avulsion fragment is a direct finding that can be fixed. 5.3.14
covers avulsion injuries.

A dynamic glide examination adds a functional observation that no static study
gives, and it is not a measurement. 5.4.14 covers patellar tracking.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating the ligament's appearance as the instability
assessment. 5.16.4 covers the tuberosity to groove measurement.

Anisotropy in the thin band is read as disruption. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

The retinaculum is confused with the ligament. 2.12.4 covers the retinacula.

A chronic appearance after a previous dislocation is read as an acute injury.
5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

A dynamic glide impression is reported as a measurement. 5.4.14 covers patellar
tracking.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament's appearance is treated as the instability assessment.
- Anisotropy in the thin band is read as disruption.
- The retinaculum is confused with the ligament.
- A chronic appearance is read as an acute injury.
- A glide impression is reported as a measurement.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this is a structure ultrasound sees well in a condition whose
decisions are made from bone.

Patellar instability surgery is planned from trochlear morphology, patellar
height and tuberosity position, all measured on cross sectional imaging, so the
ligament's appearance rarely alters the plan. 5.16.4 covers the tuberosity to
groove measurement.

The ligament is also commonly abnormal after a previous dislocation without
current symptoms, so an abnormal appearance carries little weight in the chronic
setting. 5.12.5 covers MPFL injury.

Dynamic patellar assessment is the question the clinic actually has, and
ultrasound can observe it without quantifying it, which is the same gap the
skyline view left. 5.4.14 covers patellar tracking.

Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging quantifies tracking and is not routine, so the
most relevant measurement remains unavailable in both modalities. 5.18.6 covers
dynamic magnetic resonance imaging.

The honest position is that this page describes a structure well and that the
decision lives in chapter 5.16. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

### Key Learning Points

- Instability surgery is planned from bony measurements.
- The ligament is commonly abnormal after a previous dislocation.
- Dynamic assessment can be observed without being quantified.
- Dynamic magnetic resonance imaging quantifies tracking and is not routine.
- The structure is described here and the decision lives elsewhere.
