# Effects of Root Tears

The functional meniscectomy. Detach one end of the ring and the whole meniscus
stops carrying load, with the tissue still in place.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A rope with one end loose carries nothing. Its length, thickness and condition
stop mattering the moment the anchor goes.

That is why a small lesion at an attachment can cost more than a large one in
the middle.

Functional loss and tissue loss are different quantities, and this is the
clearest case of them separating.

### At the Knee

A root tear detaches the meniscus from the tibia at one end. The ring is
intact and it has nothing to pull against. 3.6.9 covers roots.

Hoop tension cannot be generated, so load is no longer distributed. The
compartment behaves as though the meniscus had been removed. 3.6.2 covers hoop
stress.

Conventional teaching calls this a functional meniscectomy, which is the point
stated as a phrase.

The meniscus then extrudes, because the outward push is no longer resisted.
3.6.10 covers extrusion.

### What Changes It

Which root, since the lateral posterior meniscus has secondary attachments and
the medial does not. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Whether the tear is complete, since a partial detachment carries partial
tension.

Alignment, which sets the load the deficient compartment now carries. 3.5.6
covers it.

Time, since extrusion and cartilage change progress once the mechanism has
gone.

### Clinical Relevance

A root tear is a mechanical emergency in a way its size does not suggest,
which is why it has its own management. 6.34 covers root tears.

A report describing the meniscus as intact can coexist with complete
functional loss, which is worth knowing when reading one. 5.6 covers imaging.

Repair aims to restore the anchor, so position matters as much as strength.
Section 7 covers technique.

This single lesion is the best illustration in the knee of why mechanism
matters more than appearance.

### Key Learning Points

- A rope with one end loose carries nothing.
- A root tear leaves an intact ring with nothing to pull against.
- Conventional teaching calls it a functional meniscectomy.
- The meniscus extrudes once the outward push is no longer resisted.
- A meniscus described as intact can be completely non functional.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The mechanical consequence is a step change rather than a gradient. Partial
resection is a dose response; root detachment removes the mechanism at once.
3.5.9 covers the dose response.

That distinction matters for decision making. A dose response invites
judgement about how much; a step change does not.

The relevant comparison in any study is therefore the root tear against total
meniscectomy rather than against a partial resection.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching holds that cadaveric sectioning of a posterior meniscal
root produces contact area and pressure changes comparable to total
meniscectomy of that compartment.

Medial posterior root tears are commoner and behave worse, because the medial
meniscus has no meniscofemoral attachment to act as a second anchor.

Lateral posterior root tears are commonly associated with anterior cruciate
injury and are mechanically mitigated by the meniscofemoral ligaments. 2.6.3
covers them.

Extrusion follows, and conventional teaching treats extrusion as the imaging
sign that the root mechanism has failed. 3.6.10 covers it.

### What Changes It

Which root and whether a secondary attachment exists.

Completeness of the detachment and the gap from the footprint.

Alignment and body mass, which set the load the compartment now carries
unprotected.

Cartilage state at the time, which determines what the pressure rise acts on.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric sectioning with contact sensors is where the equivalence to
meniscectomy comes from, in paired specimens.

Coronal imaging demonstrates the tear and the extrusion, which is the clinical
measurement. 5.6 covers imaging.

Cohort studies measure the degenerative consequence over years.

No measurement confirms the hoop mechanism has failed in a patient, so
extrusion stands in for it.

### Clinical Relevance

Compare a root tear against total meniscectomy rather than against partial
resection.

Expect a medial posterior root tear to behave worse than a lateral one.

Look for a lateral root tear in anterior cruciate injury.

Use extrusion as the imaging sign that the mechanism has failed.

### Key Learning Points

- Root detachment is a step change, not a dose response.
- The comparison is total meniscectomy, not partial resection.
- Medial posterior root tears behave worse, lacking a meniscofemoral second anchor.
- Lateral root tears are commonly associated with anterior cruciate injury.
- Extrusion is the imaging sign that the root mechanism has failed.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat root repair as a joint preservation operation rather than a symptom
operation, because the mechanical case is about what happens next rather than
about pain now.

The repair has to restore the footprint centre to restore the direction of
tension. Conventional teaching holds that a non anatomic repair restores
contact mechanics less well. 3.6.9 covers root mechanics.

Alignment is the other half. A repaired root in an uncorrected varus limb is
being asked to carry a load that detached the original, which is why the two
are considered together. 3.13 covers alignment.

### What Changes It

Repair position relative to the native footprint centre.

Fixation and bone quality, which decide whether the repair holds under cyclic
load.

Alignment correction, which sets the load the repair must carry.

Cartilage state, which determines whether preservation is still the right aim.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric contact studies compare intact, torn, anatomically repaired and non
anatomically repaired states.

Cyclic loading measures displacement of the repair, which is the construct
losing the anchor position.

Postoperative imaging measures position, healing and residual extrusion.

Cohort outcome data measures whether joint preservation was achieved, over
years.

### Clinical Relevance

Repair anatomically to the footprint centre, since direction of tension
depends on it.

Assess and correct alignment alongside a root repair in a varus limb.

Measure residual extrusion postoperatively as the available proxy for restored
function.

Frame the operation to the patient as joint preservation rather than pain
relief.

### Key Learning Points

- Root repair is a joint preservation operation rather than a symptom operation.
- The footprint centre must be restored to restore the direction of tension.
- A repaired root in an uncorrected varus limb carries the load that detached the original.
- Cyclic displacement is the construct losing the anchor position.
- Residual extrusion is the available proxy for restored function.
