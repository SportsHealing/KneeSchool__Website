# Ballottement

The test for the tense knee, where fluid is read by how little the patella can
be moved.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Ballottement tests for a large or tense effusion by pressing the patella and
feeling it float, bounce or refuse to move at all.

It is the test at the opposite end of the volume range from the bulge sign. One
detects the smallest effusion, this one characterises the largest. 4.8.3 covers
the bulge sign.

Its real value is identifying a tense effusion, because tension rather than
volume is what makes an effusion painful and occasionally urgent. 4.8.7 covers
the acute haemarthrosis.

### How It Is Performed

Lay the patient supine with the knee extended and the quadriceps relaxed.

Place a hand above the patella to compress the suprapatellar pouch gently, which
loads the retropatellar space.

Press the patella downwards with the other hand and release, feeling for it
floating back.

Note how far the patella can be displaced. A patella that sinks and floats
indicates a large effusion; one that cannot be displaced at all indicates a tense
one.

Note whether the manoeuvre is painful, since a tense effusion is tender and that
is part of the finding.

### What a Positive Means

A patella that sinks through fluid and floats back indicates a large effusion.

A patella that cannot be displaced in a visibly swollen knee indicates a tense
effusion, which in an acute knee is a reason to consider aspiration both for
diagnosis and for relief. 4.8.7 covers it.

A tense effusion with warmth and an inability to move the knee raises infection,
and the pathway changes. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A tense effusion in an acutely injured knee is blood under pressure, which
narrows the diagnosis to a short list. 4.8.7 covers it.

A large but compliant effusion suggests a slower accumulation and a chronic
process. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

### Key Learning Points

- The test characterises a large or tense effusion by how far the patella can be displaced.
- It is at the opposite end of the volume range from the bulge sign.
- Tension rather than volume is what makes an effusion painful.
- A patella that cannot be displaced in a swollen knee indicates a tense effusion.
- A large compliant effusion suggests a slower accumulation and a chronic process.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this test is where the useful answer talks about tension rather than
volume, which is the distinction the question is usually probing.

The expected points are the extended relaxed knee, the compressed pouch, the
downward pressure and release, the degree of displacement, and the tenderness.

Say that tension is the clinically important variable and that it reflects the
rate of accumulation. 4.8.1 covers the physiology.

Say that a tense effusion in an acute knee may warrant aspiration, and say why
it is both diagnostic and relieving. 4.8.7 covers it.

Say that you would exclude infection in a tense warm knee before anything else.
4.6.2 covers temperature.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the capsule is compliant up to a volume and stiff
beyond it, so the same volume produces very different tensions depending on how
fast it accumulated. 4.8.1 covers normal fluid physiology.

A tense effusion holds the patella firmly against the femur, which is why it
cannot be displaced and why the test reads tension rather than volume.

Raised intra-articular pressure inhibits the quadriceps and limits the range, and
the effect is proportional to the pressure rather than to the volume.
4.8.2 covers the significance.

A rapidly accumulating effusion is usually blood, because arterial and
subchondral bleeding is faster than synovial production. 4.8.7 covers it.

### How Good It Is

Ballottement is reliable for a large or tense effusion and useless for a small
one, which makes it the complement of the bulge sign rather than a competitor.
4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Its finding is more clinically consequential than a volume estimate, because
tension is what drives pain and what prompts aspiration.

It is reproducible, since the finding is a clear mechanical impression rather
than a subtle visual one.

It cannot say what the fluid is, and in a tense knee that question matters more
than usual, which is the argument for aspiration. 4.8.7 covers it.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The capsule is compliant up to a volume and stiff beyond it.
- The same volume produces different tensions depending on how fast it accumulated.
- A tense effusion holds the patella against the femur, so the test reads tension.
- The quadriceps inhibition is proportional to the pressure rather than to the volume.
- A rapidly accumulating effusion is usually blood.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a tense effusion contributes to urgency and to the decision to
aspirate, which is a genuine and immediate contribution.

A tense effusion in an acutely injured knee narrows the differential and makes
aspiration reasonable for diagnosis and for relief. 4.8.7 covers it.

A tense warm effusion raises infection, and the pathway that follows is urgent
and different. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A tense effusion in a knee listed for an operation is a reason to defer, because
the examination is unreliable and the quadriceps is inhibited. 4.8.2 covers the
significance.

A large but compliant effusion in a chronic knee contributes to the diagnosis
rather than the urgency, and the examination's job is to establish which kind it
is. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is using this test alone and recording a dry knee when there
is a small effusion. 4.8.3 covers the right test.

The second is reading an undisplaceable patella as a normal finding rather than
as a tense effusion.

The third is attributing a tense warm knee to an injury without excluding
infection.

The fourth is treating a tense effusion's quadriceps inhibition as weakness.
4.7.8 covers the lag.

The fifth is recording tense or not rather than a size and a tension, which
loses half the finding. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### Key Learning Points

- A tense effusion in an acutely injured knee makes aspiration reasonable.
- A tense warm effusion raises infection and an urgent pathway.
- A tense effusion is a reason to defer an operation.
- An undisplaceable patella in a swollen knee is a tense effusion, not a normal finding.
- Recording tense or not loses half the finding.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice a tense effusion is usually an acute problem and a large
compliant one is usually a chronic problem, and the examination separates them in
seconds.

A patient with a tense effusion after a reconstruction has either bleeding or
infection, and both need addressing rather than observing. 4.6.2 covers
temperature.

A patient with a large compliant effusion and a normal mechanical examination has
a synovial process, and the chronicity is in the compliance rather than in the
history. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

A patient with a tense effusion and a locked knee has two findings that together
raise a displaced tear with bleeding, and the urgency comes from the
combination. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

In a knee with a scarred capsule the compliance is reduced, so a modest volume
can be tense and the usual relationship does not hold. 4.2.30 covers previous
surgery.

In medicolegal work the recorded tension of an effusion at presentation is more
informative than its recorded size, and it is almost never documented.
4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A tense effusion is usually acute and a large compliant one usually chronic.
- A tense effusion after a reconstruction is bleeding or infection.
- A large compliant effusion with a normal mechanical examination is a synovial process.
- A tense effusion with a locked knee raises a displaced tear with bleeding.
- A scarred capsule means a modest volume can be tense.
