# Bursa

Small fluid filled cushions that let tendons and skin slide over bone without
rubbing.

## For Young Learners

### Structure and Location

A bursa is a small flat sac with a little fluid inside. Think of a thin water
balloon with barely any water in it, placed between two things that need to
slide past each other.

There are quite a few around the knee. The best known sits right in front of
the kneecap, just under the skin. Another sits below it, over the top of the
shin bone, and one more sits deeper down.

### Function

The job is to stop rubbing. Skin slides over the kneecap every time you bend
your knee. A tendon slides over bone every time a muscle pulls. Without a
cushion in between, those surfaces would wear against each other.

If you kneel for a long time, the bursa at the front gets squashed repeatedly
and can fill with fluid. It swells into a soft lump you can see on the front
of the kneecap. The joint itself is untouched, which is why the knee usually
still bends normally.

### Key Learning Points

- A bursa is a small flat fluid filled sac that reduces rubbing.
- Several sit around the knee, including one just in front of the kneecap.
- They let skin and tendons slide over bone smoothly.
- Swelling in a bursa sits outside the joint, so the knee usually still bends.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Structure and Location

Bursae are thin sacs lined with a membrane similar to the joint lining,
containing a small amount of fluid. More than a dozen have been described
around the knee; a handful matter in practice.

The prepatellar bursa sits between the skin and the front of the kneecap. The
superficial infrapatellar bursa lies in front of the patellar tendon, and the
deep infrapatellar bursa behind it. The pes anserine bursa lies on the inner
side just below the joint, under the tendons that attach there.

The large pouch above the kneecap began as a bursa and usually connects with
the joint itself in adults, so it behaves as part of the joint cavity.

### Function

Each one reduces friction between structures that move against each other:
skin over bone, tendon over bone, or tendon over tendon. They are positioned
exactly where that movement occurs.

### Clinical Relevance

A bursa can become irritated by repeated pressure or by a direct blow, and
then fills with fluid. The prepatellar bursa is the classic example,
historically associated with trades involving kneeling.

The practical distinction worth understanding is between swelling in a bursa
and swelling in the joint. Bursal swelling is localised, sits in front of the
kneecap or over the tendon, and usually leaves knee movement comfortable.
Joint swelling is diffuse, fills the whole front of the knee, and limits
bending.

A hot, red, tender bursa can be infected, particularly after a graze or a
puncture, and that needs prompt assessment.

Individual bursal conditions and their treatment are covered in the conditions
section.

### Key Learning Points

- Bursae are thin fluid filled sacs that reduce friction at points of movement.
- The prepatellar bursa lies between skin and kneecap and is the one most often affected.
- The pouch above the kneecap connects with the joint in most adults.
- Bursal swelling is localised and usually leaves knee movement comfortable.
- Joint swelling is diffuse and limits bending, which distinguishes the two.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** I have a soft lump on the front of my kneecap. What is it?
Most often a swollen prepatellar bursa, usually from kneeling or a knock. It
sits outside the joint, which is why the knee itself still bends.

**Q.** How do I tell bursa swelling from joint swelling?
Bursal swelling is a localised lump and movement stays comfortable. Joint
swelling is spread across the whole front of the knee and makes bending tight.

**Q.** Is it dangerous?
Usually not. A bursa that is hot, red and very tender, especially after a
graze, can be infected and should be assessed promptly.

**Q.** Will it come back?
It can, if the cause continues. Kneeling pads and changing how long you kneel
at a time are the usual preventive measures.

See a doctor promptly if a swollen area is hot, red and very tender,
particularly after a graze or a cut, or if you develop a fever.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

Bursae are synovium lined sacs interposed at sites of friction. Around the
knee the clinically relevant ones are the prepatellar, between skin and
patella; the superficial infrapatellar, anterior to the patellar tendon; the
deep infrapatellar, between the tendon and the tibia; the pes anserine, deep
to the conjoined insertion of sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus; and the
semimembranosus and medial gastrocnemius bursa posteromedially.

The suprapatellar bursa communicates with the joint cavity in the large
majority of adults and is therefore functionally part of it.

### Function

Each reduces shear between adjacent moving structures. Their lining resembles
synovium and responds to irritation in the same way, with increased fluid
production.

### Clinical Relevance

Bursitis arises from repetitive pressure, direct trauma, infection or
inflammatory arthropathy. Prepatellar bursitis is the classic occupational
example.

The clinical discrimination that matters is bursal from intra-articular
swelling. Bursal swelling is well circumscribed, anterior or superficial, and
range of movement is typically preserved. An effusion is diffuse, obliterates
the parapatellar hollows and restricts flexion.

Septic bursitis is relatively common in superficial bursae because of their
subcutaneous position and proximity to skin breaches, and is distinct from
septic arthritis, which involves the joint itself. The distinction changes
both urgency and treatment.

A popliteal cyst arises from the semimembranosus and medial gastrocnemius
bursa and commonly communicates with the joint, so it typically reflects
intra-articular pathology rather than being a primary problem.

Management of each is covered in the conditions section.

### Key Learning Points

- Bursae are synovium lined sacs at sites of friction, responding to irritation with fluid.
- The suprapatellar bursa communicates with the joint in most adults.
- Bursal swelling is circumscribed with preserved movement; an effusion is diffuse and restricts flexion.
- Septic bursitis is distinct from septic arthritis and differs in urgency and treatment.
- A popliteal cyst usually communicates with the joint and reflects intra-articular pathology.
