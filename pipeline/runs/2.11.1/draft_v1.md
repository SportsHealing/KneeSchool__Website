# Iliotibial Band

A sheet of fascia from the hip to the tibia that becomes a ligament at the
knee, and the only structure here that crosses two joints.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The iliotibial band is a thickening of the fascia lata on the outer side of
the thigh. It runs from the iliac crest to the tibia, crossing both the hip
and the knee.

Tensor fasciae latae and part of gluteus maximus insert into it proximally, so
it carries muscle pull as well as acting as a passive restraint.

At the knee it attaches to Gerdy's tubercle on the anterolateral tibia. 2.2.4
covers that tubercle.

It is more than a surface structure. Deep fibres run from the band to the
lateral femoral condyle, and those are the Kaplan fibres that 2.11.2 covers.

A further expansion, the iliopatellar band, runs forwards to the patella and
forms part of the lateral retinaculum. 2.12.4 covers that.

### Relations

Superficial to it: skin and subcutaneous fat. It is the outermost structure of
the lateral knee and is palpable along its whole course.

Deep to it: the lateral femoral condyle, with a small bursa between the two
near the epicondyle.

Behind it: biceps femoris, with the common peroneal nerve behind that again.
2.10.5 and 2.10.6 cover them.

In front: the lateral retinaculum, which it blends with rather than meets.

### Function

It stabilises the hip in single leg stance, which is its function above the
knee.

At the knee it restrains internal rotation of the tibia and contributes to
varus restraint.

Its line of pull moves as the knee flexes. In extension it lies in front of
the axis of rotation and helps extend; past about 30 degrees it lies behind
and helps flex. That shift is why the band behaves differently at different
angles.

### Clinical Relevance

Because it crosses two joints, a problem at the knee can arise from the hip.
That is an anatomical fact with consequences for assessment.

The deep fibres to the femur are what make it a rotational restraint rather
than only a passive strap, and 2.11.2 covers them.

It is the one lateral structure that can be followed by hand along its whole
length, which makes it the simplest landmark on this side of the knee.

### Key Learning Points

- It runs from the iliac crest to Gerdy's tubercle, crossing the hip and the knee.
- Tensor fasciae latae and part of gluteus maximus insert into it.
- Deep fibres run from the band to the lateral femoral condyle as the Kaplan fibres.
- It restrains internal rotation of the tibia and contributes to varus restraint.
- Its line of pull crosses the flexion axis at about 30 degrees.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe it in three parts at the knee: the superficial layer running to
Gerdy's tubercle, the deep layer attaching to the distal femur, and the
capsulo-osseous layer running posteriorly and distally to the tibia behind
Gerdy's tubercle.

The capsulo-osseous layer is the one most often left out. It is the deepest
and most posterior part and it behaves as a ligament rather than as fascia.

Conventional teaching gives the band a width at the knee of the order of 25 mm
superficially, narrowing as it approaches its tibial attachment.

It is continuous with the lateral intermuscular septum, which anchors it to
the linea aspera along the length of the femur.

### Relations

It is the whole of layer one on the lateral side, in the three layer scheme
2.10.4 uses.

The deep and capsulo-osseous layers lie against the capsule and against the
lateral femoral condyle, so the band's deep surface is in contact with layer
three.

A bursa separates the band from the lateral epicondyle, which is where
friction problems arise.

In front it blends with the lateral retinaculum and the iliopatellar band, so
there is no clear anterior border.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Supply is from the lateral superior genicular artery at the knee and from
perforating branches of the profunda femoris along the thigh.

The band itself is relatively avascular in its mid substance, which is
consistent with a structure that carries load rather than one that contracts.

Nerve supply is from the lateral articular branches and from cutaneous nerves
in the overlying fascia. The band is well supplied with nociceptors near the
epicondyle.

### Function

The superficial layer anchors the band to the tibia and carries the tension
that tensor fasciae latae and gluteus maximus generate.

The deep and capsulo-osseous layers restrain internal rotation of the tibia,
and the capsulo-osseous layer carries most of that. 3.11.1 covers the
proportions.

The change of lever arm through flexion means the band acts as an extensor
near extension and a flexor in deeper flexion, which 3.11.1 also covers.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess the hip when the lateral knee is painful, because the band crosses both
joints and tension at one end is felt at the other.

Expect the band to contribute to rotational control as well as to coronal
stability. That is what makes it relevant to anterior cruciate surgery at all.

Preserve the capsulo-osseous layer where an approach allows it, since it
carries the rotational restraint the superficial layer does not.

Identify Gerdy's tubercle before any anterolateral work. It is the fixed
landmark and 2.2.4 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Three parts at the knee: superficial, deep and capsulo-osseous.
- The capsulo-osseous layer is the deepest and carries most of the rotational restraint.
- Conventional teaching gives a superficial width at the knee of about 25 mm.
- It is the whole of layer one on the lateral side.
- A bursa separates it from the lateral epicondyle.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The band's continuity with the intermuscular septum means it is anchored to
the femur along a length rather than at a point. Any procedure that tensions
it is tensioning a structure attached to the whole femur.

Its deep layers and the anterolateral ligament occupy the same region, which
is part of why the two have been described as the same structure by different
authors. 2.11.3 and 2.11.5 cover that.

Its anterior blend with the retinaculum means a lateral release crosses the
band's expansion, so the two procedures are not as separate as their names
suggest.

### Function

Treat the band as the main passive restraint to internal rotation at the knee,
with the anterolateral ligament and the capsule contributing. That ordering is
the current reading and 3.11.5 carries the evidence.

Its two joint course makes it the one knee structure whose tension can be
altered by something above the knee, which matters when a lateral
extra-articular procedure is being considered.

Over-tensioning it constrains rotation rather than simply restraining it,
because the band has little compliance in its deep layers.

### Clinical Relevance

Consider the band's deep layers rather than its surface when a rotational
problem is being addressed. The superficial layer is the easiest to reach and
the least relevant to rotation.

Assess hip abductor function in a knee with lateral symptoms, because the
band's tension is set above the knee.

Plan any lateral extra-articular procedure around the capsulo-osseous layer
and the Kaplan fibres, which 2.11.2 covers, and 7.30 carries the technique.

Avoid over-tensioning. A band with little compliance tightened too far does
not restrain rotation, it removes it.

### Key Learning Points

- The band is anchored to the femur along a length, through the intermuscular septum.
- Its deep layers and the anterolateral ligament occupy the same region.
- A lateral release crosses the band's anterior expansion, so the procedures overlap.
- It is the main passive restraint to internal rotation at the knee.
- Over-tensioning removes rotation rather than restraining it.
