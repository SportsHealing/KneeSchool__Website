# Why Clinical Examination Matters

Examination is not a cheaper scan. It answers a question imaging cannot ask,
and knowing which question that is makes the rest of this section usable.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

An examination of the knee tests what the joint does. Imaging shows what its
tissues look like. Those are different questions and they have different
answers in the same patient.

A torn structure on a scan may be doing its job. An intact structure on a scan
may be failing. Examination is how you find out which.

It also tests something no scan reaches: what this person can tolerate, how
they move, and what they are guarding against.

That is why examination survived the arrival of imaging rather than being
replaced by it.

### How It Is Performed

The sequence is always look, feel, move, then test, and the order is not
arbitrary.

Looking before touching tells you where the knee is held and what it will not
do, which changes how you then touch it.

Feeling before moving localises the problem to a structure while the patient
is still relaxed.

Special tests come last, because every one of them is uncomfortable and an
uncomfortable patient guards. A guarded knee reads normal when it is not.
4.1.4 covers the full sequence.

### What a Positive Means

A positive test means the finding was present, not that the diagnosis is made.

Each test interrogates a motion and a structure, and a positive licenses a
conclusion about that motion and no more.

Findings combine. One positive test with a matching history is worth more than
three positive tests that do not agree with each other or with what the
patient describes.

### Key Learning Points

- Examination tests what the joint does; imaging shows what its tissues look like.
- A torn structure on a scan may be functioning and an intact one may be failing.
- Look, feel, move, test, in that order, because special tests hurt and a guarded knee reads normal.
- A positive test licenses a conclusion about one motion, not a diagnosis.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

In examination terms a positive finding shifts a probability rather than
settling a question, which is the framing an examiner is listening for.

A knee is assessed by building a picture: a mechanism, a symptom pattern, a
set of findings, and the degree to which they agree.

Disagreement between those four is information rather than a nuisance. A
patient describing instability with no laxity on examination has something
going on that neither the history nor the test explains alone.

### How Good It Is

Knee examination is better than its reputation for some questions and worse
for others, and the pattern is consistent enough to learn.

It is strong for cruciate integrity. The Lachman test is the most reliable
single manoeuvre in the knee and outperforms the anterior drawer
substantially. 4.10.4 covers it.

It is weak for meniscal tears. The standard meniscal tests are specific rather
than sensitive, so a positive is informative and a negative is close to
useless. 4.9 covers them.

It is moderate for cartilage and poor for the source of anterior knee pain,
where no test reliably localises the problem.

No figures appear anywhere in Section 4 for these claims, under decision 023,
because the comparison is standard teaching and a quoted percentage written
from memory is not.

### Key Learning Points

- A positive finding shifts a probability rather than settling a question.
- Examination is strong for cruciate integrity and weak for meniscal tears, and that pattern holds across the literature.
- A specific test with a positive result is informative; the same test with a negative result often is not.
- Disagreement between history, mechanism and findings is information, not noise.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At this level the question is not whether examination works but what it is
being asked to do in a particular decision, and the answer changes its value.

Examination is at its most valuable where imaging is ambiguous and a decision
depends on function: a degenerate knee with a scan full of findings, a
post-operative knee, a knee whose symptoms do not match its images.

It is at its least valuable where the lesion is structural, the imaging is
definitive and the decision follows from the anatomy.

A test's accuracy also depends on who is performing it and on when. Laxity
tests read differently in the acute knee, under anaesthetic, and at three
weeks, and the same test in the same knee gives different answers at those
three points. 4.20 covers the post-operative version of the problem.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error in knee practice is not a missed finding. It is treating
an image as the answer to a question only the examination asked.

A degenerate meniscal tear is present on a large proportion of asymptomatic
middle aged knees, so finding one on a scan does not make it the cause of this
patient's pain. 4.9.11 covers the consequence.

The reverse error is less common and more costly. A normal scan in a patient
describing mechanical symptoms does not exclude a cause, and the examination
is what distinguishes them.

The third error is institutional rather than individual. An examination done
badly is worse than one not done at all, because a recorded normal finding is
believed by everyone downstream and is rarely repeated.

### Key Learning Points

- Examination is most valuable where imaging is ambiguous and a decision depends on function.
- The same laxity test gives different answers acutely, under anaesthetic and at three weeks.
- Treating an image as the answer to an examination question is the commonest error in knee practice.
- A degenerate meniscal tear on a scan does not make it the cause of the pain.
- A badly done examination is worse than none, because a recorded normal is believed and rarely repeated.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the examination's role changes again. It is now mostly
being used to decide whether a previous conclusion was right.

A revision assessment is an examination problem rather than an imaging one,
because the images will be full of the previous operation and the question is
what the knee now does.

Three patterns recur in referred patients. Rotational laxity missed because
only anterior translation was tested. A posterolateral corner injury missed
because the dial test was not done at two flexion angles. And a patellofemoral
cause attributed to a tibiofemoral finding that happened to be visible on a
scan.

All three are failures of examination completeness rather than of technique,
which is why this section is organised by region and insists on a sequence.

The honest limit is that examination under anaesthetic remains the most
informative single assessment of laxity available, and it requires an
anaesthetic, which means the decision to use it is itself a judgement rather
than a step in a protocol.

### Key Learning Points

- A revision assessment is an examination problem, because the images are full of the previous operation.
- Rotational laxity is missed when only anterior translation is tested.
- A posterolateral injury is missed when the dial test is done at one flexion angle.
- These are failures of completeness rather than of technique, which is why the sequence matters.
- Examination under anaesthetic is the most informative laxity assessment and costs an anaesthetic to obtain.
