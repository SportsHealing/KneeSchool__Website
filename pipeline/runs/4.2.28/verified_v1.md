# Tennis

Repeated acceleration, deceleration and direction change on a hard surface,
and a population that is often older than the other court sports.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A tennis history tests the combination of repeated deceleration and direction
change, which loads the knee in both the sagittal and the rotational planes.
3.15.4 covers the mechanics.

It also tests age and surface, because tennis is played across a wider age
range than most court sports and the surface changes the sliding and therefore
the loading.

The presentations split between overuse in the older recreational player and
acute injury in the younger competitive one.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the level, the frequency and the surface: hard court, clay, grass or
indoor.

Ask whether the problem began during a specific shot or point, or built up
over weeks.

Ask about the serve, since the knee is loaded in deep flexion and then
extended explosively.

Ask about lunging and sliding, because a slide on clay unloads the knee
rotationally and a hard court does not.

Ask about footwear, which on a hard court is a genuine variable.

### What a Positive Means

An acute twist during a direction change with immediate swelling is a cruciate
or meniscal injury as in any pivoting sport. 4.2.18 covers twisting injuries.

Anterior pain during serving and lunging, building over weeks, is
patellofemoral or patellar tendon overload. 4.16.3 covers one version.

Medial joint line pain in an older player with stiffness raises degenerative
change plus a degenerate meniscal tear. 4.9.11 covers the combination.

Posterior pain during a lunge raises a hamstring origin or a cyst rather than
the joint. 4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa.

A sudden calf or posterior knee pain during a push off is a muscle or tendon
injury rather than a joint one.

### Key Learning Points

- Tennis loads the knee in both the sagittal and rotational planes repeatedly.
- The surface changes sliding and therefore loading: clay unloads rotationally, hard courts do not.
- Older recreational players present with overuse; younger competitive ones with acute injury.
- Anterior pain during serving and lunging is patellofemoral or patellar tendon overload.
- A sudden posterior pain on push off is muscle or tendon rather than joint.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning connects the stroke to the load.

The serve loads the knee in deep flexion and then in explosive extension,
which is a patellofemoral and extensor mechanism load. 3.12.3 covers contact
pressures.

A lunge loads the knee in flexion with rotation, which is a meniscal load.

A direction change on a planted foot loads the cruciate and the anterolateral
structures, as in any pivoting sport.

The older recreational player's knee is commonly degenerate before the tennis,
which means a minor event can produce a significant symptom and the history
will sound trivial.

### How Good It Is

The tennis history performs well for separating overuse from acute injury,
which is the main division in this population.

The stroke at which the symptom occurs is reasonably specific and is reliably
recalled, because players think in strokes.

It performs poorly for structural diagnosis in the degenerate knee, where the
trivial event and the significant symptom give an uninformative combination.

Surface and footwear are modifiable and the history is the only route to them.

### Key Learning Points

- The serve is a patellofemoral and extensor mechanism load; the lunge is a meniscal one.
- Players think in strokes, so the stroke at which it happens is reliably recalled.
- In the degenerate knee a trivial event with a significant symptom is uninformative.
- Surface and footwear are modifiable and only the history reaches them.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the tennis history matters mostly for the older recreational
player, who is the commonest presentation.

A degenerate knee with a meniscal tear in a player who wants to keep playing
is the clearest application of the meniscectomy trial evidence, and the honest
answer is usually non-operative first. 4.9.11 covers the point.

The exception is a genuine mechanical block, which remains a surgical
indication whatever the degenerative background. 4.2.5 covers locking.

In a younger competitive player the decisions are the same as for any pivoting
athlete, and the return target is set by the demands rather than the sport.
4.21.8 covers return to play.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is arthroscopy for a degenerate meniscal tear in a tennis
player whose symptom is pain, because the sport makes the demand feel like an
indication.

The second is dismissing an older player's demand as recreational, which
under-treats a patient for whom the sport may be their main physical and
social activity.

The third is attributing posterior or calf symptoms to the knee.

And a tennis player with anterior pain and no injury should have their serve
and training load considered before their joint surfaces.

### Key Learning Points

- A degenerate tear in a player whose symptom is pain is usually non-operative first.
- A genuine mechanical block remains surgical whatever the degenerative background.
- The sport can make a demand feel like an indication, which is the error to guard against.
- Dismissing an older player's demand as recreational under-treats them.
- Consider the serve and the training load before the joint surfaces.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the tennis player presents after an arthroscopy that did
not help, and the history establishes whether the symptom was ever mechanical.

A player whose pain was load related and diffuse before the operation and is
unchanged after it had degenerative pain, and the next step is load
management, injection or eventually arthroplasty rather than a further
arthroscopy.

A player with a mechanical symptom that persists raises a retained fragment, a
new tear or an unaddressed chondral lesion. 4.17.5 covers cartilage defects.

An older player considering arthroplasty who wants to return to tennis needs
an explicit conversation about what the implant tolerates, and the history of
their intended play is what makes that concrete. 4.20.8 covers assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Pain unchanged after arthroscopy was degenerative, and the next step is not another arthroscopy.
- A persisting mechanical symptom raises a retained fragment, a new tear or a chondral lesion.
- A player wanting to return to tennis after arthroplasty needs an explicit conversation.
- Their intended level of play is what makes that conversation concrete.
