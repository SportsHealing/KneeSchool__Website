# J Sign

An abrupt lateral movement of the patella in terminal extension, named for the
shape of the path it traces.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The J sign is an abrupt lateral deviation of the patella as the knee reaches
full extension, so the patella's path traces a J rather than a straight line.

It indicates that the patella is leaving the trochlear groove and being pulled
laterally by the quadriceps once the bone no longer constrains it. 2.12.1
covers the trochlea.

It is the clearest and most specific clinical sign of patellar maltracking, and
it is read during active extension rather than passively. 4.15.1 covers
tracking.

### How It Is Performed

Sit the patient on the edge of the couch with the knees flexed and the feet
hanging. 4.3.4 covers the sitting assessment.

Watch the patella from directly in front as the patient extends the knee slowly
and fully.

Look specifically at the last part of the arc, since that is where the sign
occurs.

Note whether the deviation is abrupt or a gradual drift, because the abruptness
is what makes it the J sign rather than a mild tracking abnormality.

Repeat as the patient flexes from full extension, where the patella should
reduce abruptly back into the groove.

### What a Positive Means

An abrupt lateral deviation in terminal extension is a positive J sign and
indicates substantial maltracking.

It usually indicates trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, or a deficient medial
restraint, and often more than one of those. 4.15.8 covers dysplasia.

A positive sign in a patient with instability episodes is a structural finding
supporting the diagnosis. 4.2.22 covers the dislocation event.

A positive sign in a patient whose complaint is pain rather than instability
still indicates maltracking, and whether it explains the pain is a separate
question. 4.15.9 covers patellofemoral pain.

A gradual lateral drift without an abrupt component is a milder tracking
abnormality and should be recorded as that rather than as a J sign. 4.15.1
covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- The patella traces a J because it leaves the groove and is pulled laterally.
- The abruptness is what distinguishes it from a mild tracking abnormality.
- It usually indicates dysplasia, patella alta, or a deficient medial restraint.
- It is read during active extension, not passively.
- Whether it explains a pain complaint is a separate question.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the J sign is the answer when asked for a clinical sign of
maltracking, and knowing what it implies structurally is the follow up.

The expected points are the sitting position, the view from the front, the
terminal part of the arc, the abruptness, and the reduction on flexion.

Say what the sign implies: that the patella is unconstrained in extension, which
points at the trochlea, the patellar height or the medial restraint. 4.15.8
covers dysplasia.

Say that you would assess the patellar height, since patella alta keeps the
patella out of the groove through more of the range. 4.6.5 covers patellar
tenderness.

Say that you would measure the rotational alignment, since malrotation
contributes and changes the operation. 4.5.6 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the trochlea's depth constrains the patella
through flexion, so in extension, where the patella sits above the groove, only
the soft tissues hold it. 2.12.1 covers the trochlea.

A dysplastic trochlea is shallow proximally, so the patella engages later and
leaves earlier, which widens the arc in which it is unconstrained. 3.12.4
covers trochlear function.

Patella alta raises the patella so it engages later, with the same effect
through a different mechanism. 2.4.1 covers the patellar anatomy.

A deficient medial patellofemoral ligament removes the restraint that resists
the quadriceps' lateral pull in that arc. 2.12.2 covers the ligament.

### How Good It Is

The J sign is the most specific clinical sign in the patellofemoral
examination, because an abrupt reproducible deviation is hard to produce in a
normal knee.

It is insensitive. Plenty of knees with significant maltracking and instability
do not show it, so a negative sign excludes little.

It is more reproducible than most patellofemoral findings, because the
observation is binary rather than graded.

It cannot distinguish its causes, which imaging does, and in this chapter the
imaging genuinely leads. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The trochlea constrains the patella through flexion; in extension only the soft tissues do.
- A dysplastic trochlea is shallow proximally, so the patella engages later and leaves earlier.
- Patella alta produces the same effect through a different mechanism.
- It is the most specific patellofemoral sign and is insensitive.
- It cannot distinguish its causes, which imaging does.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a J sign contributes to the case for bony surgery rather than
soft tissue surgery, which is the central patellofemoral decision.

A J sign with trochlear dysplasia and instability is the combination in which a
medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction alone is least likely to be
enough. 4.15.7 covers the ligament.

A J sign with patella alta contributes to the case for distalising the tubercle,
which is a different operation decided largely on imaging. 4.6.7 covers the
tubercle.

A J sign in a patient with pain and no instability is a finding to record and
not an indication, and operating on it is a recognised way to make a painful
knee worse. 4.15.9 covers patellofemoral pain.

After surgery the sign's persistence is the clearest clinical evidence that the
maltracking was not corrected. 4.20.6 covers that assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is calling a gradual lateral drift a J sign.

The second is assessing it passively or supine.

The third is reading it as an indication in a patient whose complaint is pain.

The fourth is attributing it to the medial restraint alone, when dysplasia and
patellar height are the commoner structural contributors.

The fifth is not imaging the trochlea, which is where the operative decision is
actually made.

### Key Learning Points

- A J sign contributes to the case for bony rather than soft tissue surgery.
- With dysplasia and instability, a ligament reconstruction alone is least likely to be enough.
- A J sign in a painful knee with no instability is not an indication.
- Its persistence after surgery is the clearest evidence the maltracking was not corrected.
- Calling a gradual drift a J sign is the commonest error.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice a persisting J sign after patellofemoral surgery is the
finding that explains the operation that did not work.

A patient with a medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction and a persisting
J sign had a soft tissue operation for a bony problem, and the revision is a
bony one. 4.15.8 covers the assessment that would have shown it.

A patient whose J sign has gone and whose instability persists has a different
problem, and that is worth distrusting as a sign that the instability was not
patellar at all. 4.2.2 covers instability as a symptom.

A patient with a J sign and generalised hypermobility has a soft tissue
contribution that no bony correction fully addresses, and that belongs in the
consent. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

In a knee with a previous tubercle osteotomy the sign's persistence may reflect
an incomplete correction rather than a failed soft tissue repair, and the
pre-operative imaging separates them. 4.6.7 covers the tubercle.

In medicolegal work a recorded J sign is a useful objective observation, and it
is one of the few patellofemoral findings specific enough to carry weight.
4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A persisting J sign after ligament reconstruction means a soft tissue operation for a bony problem.
- A resolved J sign with persisting instability suggests the instability was not patellar.
- Generalised hypermobility adds a contribution no bony correction fully addresses.
- After a tubercle osteotomy, persistence may be an incomplete correction.
- It is one of the few patellofemoral findings specific enough to carry medicolegal weight.
