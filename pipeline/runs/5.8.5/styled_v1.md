# PCL Reconstruction

Several operations reconstruct different things, so without the technique the
report describes a construct and cannot judge it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A reconstruction replaces the posterior cruciate with a graft fixed in bone at
both ends. 3.8.8 covers the PCL reconstruction biomechanics.

The techniques differ in where the tibial end is fixed and in how many bundles
are reconstructed, and the appearances differ accordingly. 3.8.10 covers the
inlay techniques.

It matters because an appearance that is correct for one technique is wrong for
another, so a report written without the technique can describe a normal
construct as a failure. 5.20.7 covers reporting post-operative knees.

### How It Is Acquired

Establish the technique before reading, from the operation note rather than from
the images. 5.20.7 covers reporting post-operative knees.

Identify both fixation points and the path between them. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel
assessment.

Read the graft along its whole length, since a graft can be intact at its ends
and abnormal in the middle. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Read the tibial fixation specifically, because the two families of technique put
it in different places. 3.8.10 covers the inlay techniques.

Note the interval since surgery, because graft signal changes with time. 5.7.10
covers graft maturation.

### What Normal Looks Like

A continuous graft between its fixation points with a course appropriate to the
technique used. 2.8.6 covers the surgical anatomy.

Tunnels or a bone block in the expected position for that technique. 5.7.12
covers the tunnel assessment.

Graft signal that varies with the interval since surgery rather than staying
uniformly dark. 5.7.10 covers graft maturation.

Fixation hardware in place without surrounding bone change. 5.5.6 covers the
bones.

A graft that is taut in its expected path, judged as an appearance and not
against a figure. 3.8.8 covers the PCL reconstruction biomechanics.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Discontinuity of the graft, which is unambiguous failure. 5.7.11 covers graft
failure.

A graft that is continuous and lax in its path, which is failure of function
without failure of substance. 3.8.2 covers the posterior stability.

Tunnel widening or lucency around fixation. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Fluid tracking along the graft or around its fixation. 5.2.17 covers STIR
imaging.

A construct that does not match the technique described, which is a reason to
check the operation note rather than to report a complication. 5.20.7 covers
reporting post-operative knees.

### Key Learning Points

- The graft is fixed in bone at both ends.
- The techniques differ in tibial fixation and in bundle number.
- An appearance correct for one technique is wrong for another.
- Graft signal changes with the interval since surgery.
- A continuous lax graft is a failure of function without failure of substance.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer asks for the technique before describing the
images.

The expected points are the technique families, the fixation points, the graft's
whole length, the interval, and the laxity being clinical.

Say that the tibial fixation distinguishes the two main approaches. 3.8.10 covers
the inlay techniques.

Say that a double bundle construct has two graft limbs and is read as two. 3.8.9
covers the double bundle reconstruction.

Say that posterior laxity after reconstruction is measured on examination. 4.11.2
covers the posterior drawer test.

### How Good It Is

The modality shows the construct well and judges its success poorly, which is the
same position the post-operative chapter reaches for every procedure.

Its best contribution is the graft's continuity and its path, both of which are
clearly resolved. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Its second contribution is the fixation and the bone around it, which is where a
mechanical problem shows. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

It is limited because success is the absence of posterior translation and that is
not visible on an unloaded study. 3.8.6 covers the posterior drawer mechanics.

It is limited most of all by missing information rather than by physics: without
the technique, the report has no expected appearance to compare against. 5.17.1
covers the post-operative ACL reconstruction.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no tunnel
position, tunnel diameter, graft dimension, translation distance or interval
appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between
studies and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The construct is shown well and its success judged poorly.
- Continuity and path are clearly resolved.
- The fixation and surrounding bone show mechanical problems.
- Success is the absence of translation and is not visible unloaded.
- Without the technique there is no expected appearance to compare against.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a revision is considered on laxity, and the scan's job is to
say what there is to work with.

The graft's state, the tunnel positions and the bone stock around the fixation
decide whether a revision is a single stage procedure. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel
assessment.

A corner injury left unaddressed at the index operation is a recognised reason
these grafts stretch out, and it is visible on the same study. 5.10.3 covers PLC
injuries.

Alignment left uncorrected does the same, which is the point the medial and
lateral chapters both reach. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

An examination under anaesthesia reports the result that no imaging appearance
reports. 4.14.6 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading the construct against the native ligament. 5.8.1
covers the normal posterior cruciate.

A maturing graft's signal is reported as failure. 5.7.10 covers graft maturation.

An inlay construct is reported as abnormal because a tunnel is expected. 3.8.10
covers the inlay techniques.

One limb of a double bundle graft is reported as a partial tear of a single one.
3.8.9 covers the double bundle reconstruction.

The corner and the alignment are not assessed, so the reason for a stretched
graft is not found. 5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

### Key Learning Points

- The construct is read against the native ligament.
- A maturing graft's signal is reported as failure.
- An inlay construct is reported as abnormal for having no tunnel.
- One limb of a double bundle graft is read as a partial tear.
- The corner and the alignment are not assessed.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this page reaches the same closing position as the medial and
lateral chapters, and it reaches it harder because the techniques here differ
more.

Two knees with the same diagnosis and the same outcome can carry constructs that
look nothing alike, so an expected appearance exists only once the technique is
known. 5.20.7 covers reporting post-operative knees.

The operation note is therefore part of the study rather than background to it,
and a report written without it should say so rather than describe the construct
as though it had one. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The pre-operative study is the only record of which structures were injured, so
it is read alongside the post-operative one when a graft has failed. 5.8.3 covers
the complete PCL tears.

The useful report names the technique it assumed, the graft's continuity and
path, each fixation point, the corner, and the alignment. 5.10.6 covers
post-operative PLC imaging.

### Key Learning Points

- Two good outcomes can carry constructs that look nothing alike.
- An expected appearance exists only once the technique is known.
- The operation note is part of the study rather than background.
- A report written without it should say so.
- The pre-operative study records which structures were injured.
