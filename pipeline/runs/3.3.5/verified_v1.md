# Screw Home Mechanism

The knee rotates as it straightens, and that rotation locks it. Standing
upright costs almost no muscle work because of it.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A screw advances as it turns. The knee does something comparable through
terminal extension: the rotation and the extension are one movement rather
than two.

The rotation is obligatory. It happens because of the shape of the surfaces
and the length of the ligaments, not because a muscle produces it.

Its purpose is to lock. A locked joint can hold a load with very little muscle
activity, which is why standing still is not tiring.

### At the Knee

Through roughly the last 30 degrees of extension the tibia rotates externally
relative to the femur. Conventional teaching gives about 5 to 10 degrees of
rotation.

In closed chain the same relationship appears as internal rotation of the
femur on a fixed tibia.

Three features produce it. The medial femoral condyle has a longer articular
surface than the lateral, the anterior cruciate tightens through extension,
and the collateral and posterior structures tighten with it.

At the end of the movement the joint surfaces are maximally congruent, the
ligaments are taut, and the knee has no useful rotation left. This is the
position of maximal stability.

### What Changes It

The anterior cruciate, since its tightening is part of the mechanism and a
deficient knee loses part of it.

The shape of the medial femoral condyle, which varies between individuals.

Anything preventing full extension, because the mechanism occupies the last
part of the range and a knee that cannot reach it never locks.

Soft tissue tension generally, which is why a swollen or contracted knee loses
the position.

### Clinical Relevance

A knee held in slight flexion cannot lock, so the quadriceps must work
continuously to hold a load. That is one mechanical reason a flexion
contracture is tiring.

Restoring full extension restores the locking position, which is why extension
is protected so carefully after injury and surgery.

The locked knee is the position a limb is most stable in and least mobile in,
which is the trade the mechanism makes.

Unlocking is an active step rather than a passive release. 3.3.6 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Extension and external rotation are one obligatory movement, not two.
- Conventional teaching gives 5 to 10 degrees of rotation through the last 30 degrees.
- Condylar asymmetry, cruciate tightening and ligament tension together produce it.
- The locked knee holds load with almost no muscle work.
- A knee that cannot fully extend never locks, so the quadriceps works continuously.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Describe it as a consequence of constraint rather than as a mechanism with a
purpose. Given these surfaces and these ligament lengths, the rotation is the
only way the joint can reach full extension.

The term screw home is conventional and slightly misleading, because nothing
threads. The useful content is that terminal extension is coupled to external
tibial rotation.

The end position is usually called the position of maximal congruence or the
terminal locked position, and it is the one position in which the knee has
essentially no axial rotation available.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching attributes the rotation chiefly to the greater
anteroposterior length of the medial femoral condylar articular surface, which
obliges the lateral side to finish its travel first.

The anterior cruciate ligament tightens through terminal extension and
contributes to the coupled rotation rather than only limiting translation. 3.7
covers it.

The iliotibial band and the posterior capsule tighten in extension and add to
the terminal constraint.

The patella sits at its most proximal trochlear position in extension, with
the smallest contact area, which 3.12 covers.

### What Changes It

Anterior cruciate deficiency, which alters terminal rotation and is one reason
a deficient knee feels different in extension rather than only in pivot.

Condylar morphology, which varies and is part of why normal terminal rotation
has a range rather than a value.

A fixed flexion deformity, which removes the part of the range the mechanism
needs.

Previous surgery that changes extension, including a graft placed so that it
limits terminal extension.

### How It Is Measured

Fluoroscopic and model based tracking through terminal extension measures the
coupled rotation in vivo, over a small range where measurement error is
proportionally large.

Cadaveric work established the relationship and measures it without muscle,
which is acceptable here because the mechanism is passive.

Clinical assessment detects loss of full extension reliably and the coupled
rotation not at all, so the mechanism is inferred rather than measured in
clinic.

Conventional teaching's figure of 5 to 10 degrees comes from a small range
measurement and should be held loosely.

### Clinical Relevance

Protect and restore full extension after any knee injury or operation, because
the locking position depends on the last few degrees.

Expect a knee that cannot fully extend to fatigue the quadriceps in standing,
and treat the extension rather than the muscle.

Suspect a graft or a cyclops lesion when terminal extension is lost after
reconstruction.

Avoid quoting terminal rotation as a precise figure, since the published range
is wide relative to the quantity.

### Key Learning Points

- The rotation is a consequence of constraint, not a mechanism with an aim.
- Conventional teaching credits the longer medial femoral condylar surface.
- The anterior cruciate tightens through terminal extension and contributes to the rotation.
- Full extension is the one position with essentially no axial rotation available.
- The coupled rotation is inferred in clinic rather than measured.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat terminal extension as a design and a technical target. An arthroplasty
that cannot reach full extension has lost the locking position, and the
patient pays for it in standing endurance rather than in range.

The coupled rotation constrains component rotation in replacement, because an
implant that cannot rotate as it extends will either not extend or will rotate
the whole tibia.

In reconstruction the same coupling explains why a graft placed too anteriorly
limits terminal extension. Conventional teaching describes this as
impingement, and the mechanical account is that the construct cannot
accommodate the coupled motion.

### What Changes It

Component geometry and insert conformity in replacement, which set whether
coupled rotation is possible.

Posterior capsular release and osteophyte removal, which decide whether the
last degrees of extension are available at all.

Graft tunnel position, particularly on the femoral side, which changes the
angle at which the graft becomes the limit.

Tibial slope, which shifts the whole flexion extension arc and therefore where
the mechanism sits in it.

### How It Is Measured

Intraoperative assessment of full passive extension is the practical
measurement, and it is made in an anaesthetised knee that will swell.

Fluoroscopic kinematic study of replaced knees measures whether coupled
terminal rotation survived the operation, in selected series performing
prescribed tasks.

Lateral radiography measures component position and sagittal alignment rather
than the coupled motion itself.

Loss of extension is detected clinically with certainty and attributed to a
cause only with imaging and examination together.

### Clinical Relevance

Achieve full extension intraoperatively rather than accepting a few degrees,
since the locking position has no partial version.

Place the femoral tunnel to allow terminal extension, and check it on the
table.

Investigate lost extension after reconstruction rather than attributing it to
stiffness.

Consider component rotation when a replaced knee loses terminal extension
without mechanical obstruction.

### Key Learning Points

- An arthroplasty without full extension has lost the locking position.
- The coupled rotation constrains component rotation in replacement.
- A graft placed too anteriorly limits terminal extension because it blocks coupled motion.
- Full extension has no partial version; achieve it on the table.
- Lost extension after reconstruction is investigated, not accepted as stiffness.
