# Contributions at Different Flexion Angles

The medial side divides its work by angle. That division is why the
examination has a sequence and why a single apportionment figure means
nothing.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

If two structures are tight at different joint positions, each is the
restraint in its own part of the range.

Testing a joint in the position where one structure is tight isolates that
structure. Testing where both are tight tests neither.

So an examination sequence that moves through angles is a way of taking the
structures one at a time.

### At the Knee

Near full extension the posterior oblique ligament and the posteromedial
capsule are taut and contribute substantially to valgus restraint. 3.9.5
covers the posterior oblique ligament.

At 20 to 30 degrees those structures have slackened and the superficial medial
collateral ligament is the clear primary restraint. 3.9.3 covers it.

In deeper flexion the superficial ligament's proximal tibial division carries
more and the distal division less, by conventional teaching.

Rotation is restrained by the deep layer and the corner, with the corner's
contribution greatest near extension. 3.9.4 covers the deep layer.

### What Changes It

The flexion angle itself, which is the whole point.

Which structures are intact.

Load, which adds lateral compartment compression.

Muscle activity, which contributes at every angle.

### Clinical Relevance

Valgus testing at 20 to 30 degrees isolates the superficial ligament, and in
full extension it tests the corner as well. Section 4 covers the tests.

Opening in extension but not at 30 degrees is a corner pattern. Opening at
both is a larger injury.

Any apportionment figure quoted without a flexion angle is uninterpretable.

The same logic governs the cruciates and the lateral side, so learning it once
serves everywhere. 3.3.8 covers changes through flexion.

### Key Learning Points

- Structures tight at different positions are each the restraint in their own range.
- Testing where one structure is tight isolates it.
- Near extension the corner contributes substantially to valgus restraint.
- At 20 to 30 degrees the superficial ligament is the clear primary.
- An apportionment figure without a flexion angle is uninterpretable.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

This is the medial side's version of a general principle: restraint is angle
dependent and the handovers between structures define the examination. 3.2.10
covers stability through range.

Reciprocal tightening is the mechanism. One structure tightens as another
slackens, which gives continuous coverage with structures that are
individually effective over part of the arc.

The practical consequence is that an examination is a sampling strategy: a
small number of angles chosen to catch each structure where it is the limit.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the superficial medial collateral ligament its
greatest share of valgus restraint at around 20 to 30 degrees and a reduced
share at full extension.

The posterior oblique ligament and the posteromedial capsule have their
greatest share at or near full extension.

Within the superficial ligament, conventional teaching has the proximal tibial
division tight in flexion and the distal division nearer extension. 3.9.3
covers it.

For external rotation the corner's share is greatest near extension and the
deep layer contributes through more of the arc. 3.9.2 covers rotation.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, as the organising variable.

Which structures are intact and the sectioning order in any study quoted.

Applied load direction, since valgus and rotation distribute differently.

Axial load, which recruits bony contact.

### How It Is Measured

Sequential sectioning at several flexion angles, with valgus and rotational
loads, is the method behind every figure here.

Order dependence is the standing limitation, and it is larger where structures
are adjacent capsular layers. 3.2.9 covers that.

Valgus stress radiography at two angles measures the functional consequence
clinically.

Figures differ between studies by angle, load and protocol, which is expected
rather than contradictory.

### Clinical Relevance

Examine at full extension and at 20 to 30 degrees deliberately, as a sampling
strategy.

Record the angle with any laxity finding.

Read differences between published figures as protocol differences.

Expect order dependence to be worse where the structures are adjacent capsular
layers.

### Key Learning Points

- Restraint is angle dependent and the handovers define the examination.
- Reciprocal tightening gives continuous coverage from partial structures.
- An examination is a sampling strategy at chosen angles.
- Conventional teaching: superficial ligament greatest at 20 to 30 degrees, corner at extension.
- Differences between published figures are protocol differences.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the angle dependence as the thing that makes a medial reconstruction a
two limb operation and the thing that makes tensioning angles matter. 3.9.8
covers the construct.

A single limb tensioned at one angle cannot reproduce two reciprocal patterns,
which is a geometric statement rather than a surgical opinion.

It also sets the postoperative assessment: measuring at one angle tests one
limb, so the result has to be measured at both. 3.9.1 covers valgus stability.

### What Changes It

Attachment positions, which set each limb's pattern.

Tensioning angle for each limb.

Which native structures survived and at what length.

Alignment, which loads the whole medial side. 3.13 covers alignment.

### How It Is Measured

Valgus stress radiography at full extension and at 20 to 30 degrees measures
both limbs' territory.

Robotic testing of constructs at several angles is where the tensioning
recommendations come from.

Range of motion detects over-constraint of either limb.

No measurement attributes a residual laxity to a specific limb.

### Clinical Relevance

Measure the postoperative result at both angles, not one.

Tension each limb at its own angle.

Expect a single limb construct to leave one angle less controlled.

Accept that a residual laxity cannot be attributed to a specific limb.

### Key Learning Points

- Angle dependence makes medial reconstruction a two limb operation.
- One limb at one angle cannot reproduce two reciprocal patterns.
- The result has to be measured at both angles.
- Over-constraint of either limb shows as lost range.
- No measurement attributes a residual laxity to a specific limb.
