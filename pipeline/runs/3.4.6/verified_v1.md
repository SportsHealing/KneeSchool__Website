# External Moments

The moments the world applies to the knee. One of them, the adduction moment,
is the most clinically useful number in knee biomechanics.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

An external moment is one applied to the limb from outside it: by gravity, by
the ground, by a load being carried, or by another person.

It is called external because the body did not produce it. The body's job is
to resist it, which it does with an internal moment. 3.4.7 covers that side.

Naming the external moment is the convention in the literature, so a reported
knee moment usually describes what the world is doing rather than what the
muscles are doing.

### At the Knee

An external flexion moment tends to bend the knee and is resisted by the
quadriceps. It is largest in early stance and on stairs.

An external extension moment tends to straighten the knee and is resisted by
the hamstrings and the posterior structures.

An external adduction moment tends to angulate the knee into varus and loads
the medial compartment. It is produced whenever the ground reaction force line
passes medial to the knee, which in normal walking it does for most of stance.

An external abduction moment does the reverse and loads the lateral
compartment. It is the moment applied in a valgus collapse.

### What Changes It

Alignment. A varus knee sits further lateral to the force line, which
lengthens the arm and raises the adduction moment.

Body weight, which scales the force producing every external moment.

Walking speed, which raises the peaks.

Where the foot is placed and how the trunk leans, both of which move the force
line.

### Clinical Relevance

The adduction moment is the mechanical reason medial compartment
osteoarthritis is commoner than lateral.

A walking stick held in the opposite hand reduces the adduction moment, which
is a mechanical effect rather than a confidence one.

An osteotomy works by moving the force line so the moment falls, which is why
the correction target matters more than the appearance. 3.13 covers alignment.

Carrying a load on one side changes the moment at the knee on the other, which
is worth asking about in an occupational history.

### Key Learning Points

- An external moment is applied from outside the body, by gravity, the ground or a load.
- The literature names the external moment by convention.
- An external adduction moment loads the medial compartment.
- A varus knee has a longer adduction moment arm, so the moment is larger.
- A stick in the opposite hand reduces the adduction moment mechanically.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

An external moment is the cross product of the ground reaction force and the
vector from the knee to the force's point of application, so it changes with
both the force and the geometry.

It is reported normalised to body mass, and sometimes to mass and height,
which makes studies comparable and conceals absolute load.

Peak values and impulse are both reported. The impulse, the moment integrated
over the stance phase, is the better description of cumulative exposure.

### At the Knee

The external knee adduction moment peaks twice in stance in most people,
mirroring the two peaks of the vertical ground reaction force. 3.4.2 covers
that curve.

Conventional teaching associates a larger adduction moment with faster
radiographic progression of medial compartment osteoarthritis, and the
association is stronger for progression than for incidence.

The external flexion moment in early stance sets the eccentric quadriceps
demand, and a patient who avoids it walks with a reduced stance flexion wave.
3.3.9 covers walking.

Transverse plane external moments are small in walking and substantial in
cutting, where they combine with the coronal moment. 3.3.14 covers cutting.

### What Changes It

Mechanical axis alignment, which is the dominant term and is surgically
modifiable.

Foot progression angle. Conventional teaching holds that walking with the feet
turned out reduces the late stance adduction moment.

Lateral trunk lean towards the stance limb, which moves the centre of mass
over the knee and reduces the moment.

A cane or a lateral wedge, which redirect or offload part of the force.

### How It Is Measured

Inverse dynamics from force plate and motion capture gives the moment and is a
research measurement.

The hip knee ankle angle on a long leg standing film is the clinical proxy and
correlates moderately with the dynamic moment.

Wearable and insole systems estimate it with a model between the sensor and
the number.

Reported effect sizes for gait modification are small, and the mechanism is
not in doubt while the clinical benefit remains modest.

### Clinical Relevance

Measure alignment when the adduction moment is the clinical question, since it
is the available proxy.

Offer a cane in the opposite hand for medial compartment pain, on mechanical
grounds.

Describe gait modification honestly as a small mechanical change with a sound
rationale.

Prefer impulse over peak when the question is cumulative exposure rather than
a single step.

### Key Learning Points

- An external moment depends on both the force and the geometry.
- Normalising to body mass makes studies comparable and hides absolute load.
- Impulse describes cumulative exposure better than a peak does.
- A larger adduction moment is associated with faster medial progression.
- Toe out gait and lateral trunk lean both reduce the moment, by conventional teaching.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the adduction moment as the target of every mechanical treatment for
medial compartment disease, and treat alignment as the lever that moves it
furthest.

The dose response is the useful framing. Osteotomy produces a large change,
bracing a moderate one, gait modification and a wedge a small one, and the
invasiveness follows the same order.

The association with progression is observational. No randomised evidence
establishes that lowering the moment by a stated amount changes an
individual's outcome, which is the honest limit of the argument.

### What Changes It

Correction magnitude and the resulting mechanical axis, which is the dominant
surgical variable.

Body mass, which scales the moment and often exceeds the mechanical correction
in magnitude.

Brace design and compliance, since an unworn brace applies no moment.

Compartment and ligament state, which decide whether unloading the medial side
is sufficient or the knee needs more.

### How It Is Measured

Gait analysis quantifies the moment and the change a treatment produces, in a
laboratory and over a few strides.

Long leg radiography measures the static correction achieved and is what is
available after surgery.

Registry and cohort data measure survivorship and conversion to arthroplasty,
which is the outcome rather than the mechanism.

There is no agreed moment threshold for intervention, so the indication stays
clinical and radiographic.

### Clinical Relevance

Match the intervention's mechanical dose to the patient's deformity and
demand.

Address body mass alongside correction, since it can exceed the surgical
effect.

State the observational nature of the progression association when
counselling.

Plan correction to a mechanical axis target and confirm it radiographically.

### Key Learning Points

- Alignment is the lever that moves the adduction moment furthest.
- Dose response: osteotomy large, bracing moderate, gait modification small.
- The association with progression is observational, with no randomised threshold.
- Body mass can exceed the surgical effect in magnitude.
- No agreed moment threshold defines the indication.
