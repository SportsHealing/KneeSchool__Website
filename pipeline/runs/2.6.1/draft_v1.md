# Gross Anatomy

The shape of the lateral meniscus and why it is the mobile one: more circular,
less tethered, and sitting on a convex surface.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The lateral meniscus is a fibrocartilaginous ring on the lateral tibial
plateau. Seen from above it is almost a closed O rather than the medial
meniscus's open C, because its two horns attach close together.

It covers more of its plateau than the medial meniscus covers of its own,
conventionally given as about 75 per cent of the lateral articular surface.

In cross section it is the same wedge as the medial: thick peripherally, thin
at the free edge, concave above and flat below. Its body is of fairly even
width around the ring rather than broadening posteriorly.

### Relations

Above: the lateral femoral condyle. Below: the lateral tibial plateau, which
is convex in the sagittal plane, so the meniscus is supplying more congruence
here than on the medial side.

Peripherally: the capsule, but with an important gap. At the popliteus hiatus
the tendon crosses between the meniscus and the capsule, so the meniscus has
no capsular attachment over that length.

Posteriorly: the popliteomeniscal fascicles tether the posterior part to the
popliteus tendon, and the meniscofemoral ligaments run from the posterior horn
to the medial femoral condyle. 2.6.2 and 2.6.3 cover both.

Anteriorly: the transverse intermeniscal ligament joins its anterior horn to
the medial meniscus.

### Function

The same hoop mechanism as the medial meniscus, described on 2.5.4.
Compression of the wedge is resisted by circumferential fibres anchored at the
horns.

The lateral meniscus does more congruence work, because the surface under it
is convex rather than concave. Take it away and the lateral compartment loses
more than the medial compartment loses without its own.

It is also far more mobile. The horns are close together, the capsular
attachment is interrupted at the hiatus, and the coronary ligaments are
longer. It translates with the femoral condyle rather than being loaded in
place.

### Clinical Relevance

Mobility protects it. The lateral meniscus is torn less often than the medial
in the intact knee, because it moves out of the way.

Losing it costs more. Lateral meniscectomy is associated with faster and more
severe cartilage loss than medial meniscectomy, and the convex plateau
underneath is the reason.

It is the meniscus that can be congenitally discoid, a variant in which the
meniscus covers most or all of the plateau. Conventional teaching puts the
prevalence at a few per cent, with wide variation between populations.

### Key Learning Points

- The lateral meniscus is nearly a closed ring and covers about 75 per cent of its plateau.
- The capsular attachment is interrupted at the popliteus hiatus.
- It sits on a convex surface, so it supplies more congruence than the medial meniscus does.
- It is more mobile and torn less often, but losing it costs more.
- Discoid variants occur on this side, at a few per cent with wide population variation.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Compare it to the medial meniscus feature by feature. The horns attach close
together rather than far apart, so the ring is nearly closed. The body is of
even width rather than broadening posteriorly. The coronary ligaments are
longer, so the periphery is less tightly held. And the capsular attachment is
absent at the popliteus hiatus.

The posterior horn has two further attachments the medial side does not: the
popliteomeniscal fascicles to the popliteus tendon, and one or both
meniscofemoral ligaments to the medial femoral condyle.

The anterior horn attaches in the anterior intercondylar area close to and
often overlapping the anterior cruciate tibial footprint. The posterior horn
attaches immediately in front of the posterior cruciate's attachment and
lateral to the medial meniscus's posterior root. 2.6.4 covers both.

### Relations

The popliteus hiatus is the defining relation. The tendon passes from its
sulcus on the lateral femoral condyle, through a gap in the capsule, to the
muscle belly. Over that length the meniscus is held not by capsule but by the
fascicles.

That gap is also a route: a tear at the posterolateral periphery can extend
into a region with no capsular attachment and therefore no tissue to repair
to.

Laterally, outside the capsule, lie the lateral collateral ligament and the
posterolateral corner structures, which do not attach to this meniscus. That
is the opposite of the medial arrangement and is the second major difference
between the two sides.

The inferior lateral genicular artery runs circumferentially just outside the
peripheral border and supplies the perimeniscal plexus on this side.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The supply is the same arrangement as described on 2.5.3, derived here from
the lateral genicular arteries, with the same peripheral penetration and the
same three zones.

The hiatus region is a relative watershed, because the capsular tissue that
carries the plexus is absent there. Tears in that region behave as if more
central than their position suggests.

Innervation follows the vessels, with mechanoreceptors at the horns. The
popliteomeniscal fascicles carry receptors and are proposed as part of the
dynamic control of meniscal position.

### Function

Load sharing follows the same hoop mechanism, with the extra demand of a
convex tibial surface beneath. The lateral meniscus is doing more of the
congruence work and is therefore less dispensable, which is the opposite of
what its lower tear rate suggests.

Mobility is the second function. The posterior horn translates posteriorly
with the femoral condyle through flexion, and the fascicles and the popliteus
control that movement rather than preventing it.

The meniscofemoral ligaments pull the posterior horn forwards as the knee
extends, which is a second mechanism for keeping the meniscus under the
condyle. 2.6.3 covers them.

### Clinical Relevance

Lateral meniscectomy has worse long term consequences than medial, and the
convex plateau is the anatomical explanation. That asymmetry should change the
threshold for resection on this side.

A tear at the popliteus hiatus may be unrepairable because there is nothing to
repair to, and recognising the position matters more than measuring the tear.

Discoid lateral meniscus is a variant of shape with an associated instability
of attachment, and the Wrisberg type lacks the posterior capsular attachment
entirely. Classification and management belong to Sections 5, 6 and 10.

Because the anterior horn attachment can overlap the anterior cruciate
footprint, cruciate tunnel placement can injure it, in the same way as the
medial anterior root.

### Key Learning Points

- The horns attach close together, the coronary ligaments are longer, and the capsule is absent at the hiatus.
- The lateral collateral and posterolateral structures do not attach to this meniscus, unlike the medial arrangement.
- The hiatus is a vascular watershed, so tears there behave as more central than they look.
- Lateral meniscectomy has worse consequences than medial, because the plateau beneath is convex.
- The anterior horn attachment can overlap the anterior cruciate footprint.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The posterolateral periphery is the region where the usual rules do not apply.
There is no capsular attachment at the hiatus, the vascular tissue is
interrupted, and the fascicles are the only tether. A tear there is assessed
on what is left to fix rather than on its distance from a margin.

The popliteus tendon is in the way of instruments approaching the
posterolateral periphery, which is both a hazard and a landmark.

### Function

The lateral meniscus is mobile by design, so stability of attachment and
freedom of movement are both normal findings and the line between them is a
judgement. A hypermobile lateral meniscus is a recognised entity and 2.6.2
covers the anatomy.

Because the compartment depends on the meniscus for congruence, restoring the
meniscus matters more here. That is the anatomical case for lateral allograft
transplantation being considered earlier than medial.

### Clinical Relevance

Preserve lateral meniscal tissue more aggressively than medial. The anatomy,
not the evidence, is the primary argument: the compartment has no concavity of
its own.

In a young patient after lateral meniscectomy, allograft transplantation is a
legitimate consideration and Section 7 owns the indications.

At arthroscopy, assess lateral meniscal mobility deliberately rather than
incidentally. Excessive translation with an intact meniscus points at the
fascicles.

The coverage proportion and the discoid prevalence on this page are standard
teaching values, registered and unchecked. The discoid figure in particular
varies several fold between populations.

### Key Learning Points

- At the hiatus a tear is assessed on what is left to fix rather than on distance from a margin.
- Mobility is normal by design, so the line between mobile and hypermobile is a judgement.
- The lateral compartment has no concavity of its own, which is the primary argument for preservation.
- Assess lateral meniscal mobility deliberately at arthroscopy.
