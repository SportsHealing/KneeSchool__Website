# Kinematics During Running

Running is not fast walking. There is no moment when both feet are down, so
the knee absorbs a whole body landing on every step.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Walking always has one foot on the ground and has two periods of double
support. Running has neither, and it has a flight phase when neither foot is
down.

That single difference changes the task. Every step is a landing, and the limb
has to absorb the whole body's descent on its own.

Stance is shorter and the motion faster, so the same range has to be covered
in less time.

### At the Knee

The knee contacts the ground already flexed. Conventional teaching gives
roughly 20 degrees at initial contact in running, against near extension in
walking.

Stance flexion then increases to roughly 40 to 45 degrees, which is more than
double the walking wave and under a larger load.

Swing flexion is much greater. Conventional teaching gives roughly 90 to 130
degrees depending on speed, since the heel rises towards the buttock as speed
increases.

Stance occupies a smaller share of the cycle as speed rises, so the absorbing
work is compressed into less time.

### What Changes It

Speed, which increases both the swing flexion peak and the rate at which
stance flexion occurs.

Footstrike pattern, which changes the knee's share of the absorbing work
relative to the ankle.

Fatigue, which reduces the stance flexion and therefore the absorption.

Surface and gradient, since downhill running increases the absorbing demand.

### Clinical Relevance

Running loads the knee in flexion repeatedly, which is why a patient can walk
without pain and run with it.

Returning to running is a separate milestone from returning to walking,
because it is a separate task rather than a faster one.

A patient who cannot reach the swing flexion range will change their stride
rather than stop, which moves the demand elsewhere.

Sport specific application belongs to 3.15, and running mechanics as a cause
of injury to 3.1.5.

### Key Learning Points

- Running has a flight phase and no double support; every step is a landing.
- Conventional teaching gives about 20 degrees of knee flexion at initial contact.
- Stance flexion reaches roughly 40 to 45 degrees, more than double the walking wave.
- Swing flexion reaches roughly 90 to 130 degrees depending on speed.
- Returning to running is a separate milestone from returning to walking.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Treat running as a repeated absorb and return cycle. The limb absorbs kinetic
energy in early stance and returns part of it in late stance, and the knee
contributes to both.

Absorption is eccentric work and return is partly elastic, stored in tendon
rather than produced by muscle. 3.4.8 covers energy absorption.

The kinematic signature of absorption is flexion under load, so a knee that
flexes less in stance is absorbing less and passing more elsewhere.

### At the Knee

Initial contact flexion of roughly 20 degrees means the knee is already off
its locking position and depends on muscle and ligament rather than on
congruence.

Peak stance flexion of roughly 40 to 45 degrees places the knee in mid
flexion, where rotation and translation are greatest. 3.3.8 covers the zones.

Axial rotation and coronal motion remain small in the sagittal plane dominated
task, and they increase when running is combined with direction change. 3.3.14
covers cutting.

The patellofemoral joint sees its load rise sharply with stance flexion, since
the compressive force scales with both quadriceps force and flexion angle.
3.12 covers it.

### What Changes It

Speed and stride length, which set the flexion peak and the absorbing rate.

Footstrike pattern, which shifts work between the ankle and the knee.

Quadriceps and gluteal function, which decide whether the absorbing flexion is
controlled.

Fatigue, which is measurable as reduced flexion and altered coronal motion
late in a run.

### How It Is Measured

Treadmill based optical capture with an instrumented belt is the usual method
and measures treadmill running, which differs slightly from overground.

Overground running with embedded force plates gives fewer strides and more
fidelity.

High speed video with two dimensional analysis is used in practice and
measures sagittal angles adequately while confounding the coronal ones with
rotation.

Soft tissue artefact grows with speed, so the fast transverse plane quantities
are the least reliable in the whole kinematic literature.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess running separately from walking when a patient's symptoms are activity
specific.

Expect patellofemoral symptoms to appear in running before walking, on the
loading argument rather than on a structural one.

Read two dimensional video coronal measurements cautiously, since rotation
confounds them.

Consider fatigue when symptoms appear late in an activity rather than at the
start.

### Key Learning Points

- Running is a repeated absorb and return cycle; the knee contributes to both.
- Flexion under load is the kinematic signature of absorption.
- Initial contact flexion means the knee starts off its locking position.
- Peak stance flexion places the knee in mid flexion, where motion is greatest.
- Soft tissue artefact grows with speed, so fast transverse plane data is least reliable.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat return to running as a mechanical decision rather than a time based one.
The task requires flexion under load in mid flexion, which is where a
reconstruction is least protected by congruence.

Repeated loading is the variable that distinguishes running from most clinical
testing. A construct adequate for one cycle may not be adequate for several
thousand per session.

Patellofemoral and cartilage consequences follow from the loading rather than
from the kinematics alone, and they are the reason running advice differs
after a cartilage procedure. 3.5 covers contact mechanics.

### What Changes It

Graft maturity and fixation, which change what repeated mid flexion loading
costs.

Cartilage and meniscal status, which set the surface's tolerance of the load.

Alignment, which concentrates the repeated load in one compartment.

Body mass and training volume, which scale the total number of loading cycles.

### How It Is Measured

Criterion based return to sport testing measures hop, strength and symmetry,
which are performance proxies rather than joint kinematics.

Laboratory running analysis measures the kinematics in a few strides and
cannot measure cumulative load.

Wearable load monitoring measures cycle counts and is the closest available
measure of cumulative exposure.

No measurement predicts an individual's tolerance, which is the honest
position in counselling.

### Clinical Relevance

Stage return to running by criteria rather than by time after surgery.

Account for cumulative cycles rather than peak load when advising after a
cartilage procedure.

Correct alignment where repeated single compartment loading is the problem.

State the uncertainty about individual tolerance rather than offering a
figure.

### Key Learning Points

- Return to running is a mechanical decision, not a time based one.
- Repeated loading distinguishes running from any single cycle clinical test.
- Cumulative cycles, not peak load, drive the cartilage consequence.
- Performance tests are proxies; they do not measure joint kinematics.
- No measurement predicts an individual's tolerance.
