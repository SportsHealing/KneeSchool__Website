# OCD

Osteochondritis dissecans in the growing knee, where the examination is looking
for a lesion it cannot feel in a patient who may not localise it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

In a child or adolescent the lesion presents as vague activity related knee
pain, sometimes an effusion after sport, and occasionally a mechanical symptom.
4.17.4 covers the adult presentation.

The examination is deciding whether the pain is articular, where the loaded
compartment is, and whether anything suggests the fragment has separated.
4.17.2 covers mechanical symptoms.

It matters that the paediatric version behaves differently from the adult one,
because growth gives a stable lesion a chance to heal. 4.19.1 covers the growth
plate assessment.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history: the age, the sport, how long the pain has been present, and
whether it is worse during or after activity. 4.2.11 covers aggravating
factors.

Ask specifically about catching, locking and giving way, since those are the
clinical markers of an unstable fragment. 4.2.5 covers locking.

Assess the effusion, because a recurrent effusion after sport is often the only
objective finding. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Palpate the femoral condyles with the knee flexed, since the commonest site
only becomes accessible in flexion. 4.6.1 covers systematic palpation.

Measure the range and note any block or any pain at a particular angle. 4.7.6
covers the painful arc.

Assess the gait, since a child with a condylar lesion may walk with the limb
externally rotated. 4.4.1 covers normal gait.

Examine the other knee, because the lesion is bilateral in a proportion of
cases. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

### What a Positive Means

Activity related pain with a recurrent effusion and tenderness over a femoral
condyle in flexion is the characteristic picture.

A mechanical symptom with those findings suggests the fragment is unstable or
separated, which changes the urgency. 4.17.2 covers mechanical symptoms.

A block to full extension suggests a loose fragment within the joint. 4.5.4
covers the flexion deformity.

Tenderness in flexion over the lateral aspect of the medial condyle fits the
commonest site, and a normal joint line argues against a meniscal cause. 4.6.4
covers joint line tenderness.

A normal examination in a child with activity related pain is common and does
not exclude the lesion. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

### Key Learning Points

- The presentation is vague activity related pain rather than a clear mechanical story.
- Catching, locking and giving way are the clinical markers of instability.
- The commonest site is only accessible with the knee flexed.
- A block to extension suggests a loose fragment.
- A normal examination does not exclude the lesion.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the adolescent with activity related knee pain and the
expected answer reaches skeletal maturity and fragment stability quickly.

The expected points are the age and maturity, the activity history, the
mechanical questions, the effusion, the condylar palpation in flexion, the
range, the gait and the other knee.

Say that you would palpate the condyles in flexion and give the reason. 4.6.1
covers systematic palpation.

Say that the examination raises the diagnosis and that the imaging grades the
lesion. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

Say that you would examine both knees, since the condition can be bilateral.
4.19.6 covers developmental variants.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the lesion starts in the subchondral bone, so the
overlying cartilage loses its support and can separate with it. 3.5.1 covers
contact areas.

A lesion that stays attached can revascularise while growth continues, which is
why skeletal maturity governs the prognosis. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate
assessment.

A separated fragment becomes a loose body and produces the mechanical symptoms,
so the history is the clinical proxy for stability. 4.17.2 covers mechanical
symptoms.

The commonest site is loaded in flexion rather than extension, which is why the
pain is activity related and why the arc localises it. 3.5.4 covers the effects
of flexion.

### How Good It Is

The examination raises the diagnosis in an adolescent with the characteristic
history and cannot grade the lesion.

Its most useful contribution is the mechanical history, because stability is the
decision the treatment turns on. 4.17.2 covers mechanical symptoms.

It is insensitive for a stable lesion, which may produce only an effusion and
vague pain.

Magnetic resonance imaging grades stability and radiographs show the lesion, so
the examination's role is the referral. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The lesion starts in the subchondral bone and the cartilage loses its support.
- An attached lesion can revascularise while growth continues.
- A separated fragment becomes a loose body and produces mechanical symptoms.
- The commonest site is loaded in flexion, which is why the pain is activity related.
- The examination's contribution is the mechanical history and the referral.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the lesion is sorted by skeletal maturity and by stability,
and the examination establishes the first and contributes to the second.

A stable lesion in a skeletally immature knee is observed and protected, and the
examination's job is confirming that no mechanical symptom is present. 4.17.2
covers mechanical symptoms.

An unstable or detached lesion moves towards intervention, and the mechanical
history is what raises it. 4.20.5 covers the cartilage surgery assessment.

A lesion in a near mature knee behaves like the adult version, so the maturity
assessment changes the expected course rather than the diagnosis. 4.17.4 covers
the adult version.

Alignment is assessed, because a malaligned limb loads the affected compartment
and changes the plan. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is attributing an adolescent's knee pain to growth or to
overuse without examining for an effusion. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Condylar tenderness is missed when the knee is palpated in extension. 4.6.1
covers systematic palpation.

A mechanical symptom reported by a child is unreliable, and the parent's account
and the patient's differ. 4.2.5 covers locking.

An effusion is attributed to activity when it is the only sign of an articular
lesion. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

A bilateral finding is read as a normal variant because it is symmetrical.
4.19.6 covers developmental variants.

### Key Learning Points

- Attributing adolescent knee pain to growth without examining for an effusion is the commonest error.
- Condylar tenderness is missed when the knee is palpated in extension.
- A child's report of a mechanical symptom is unreliable.
- An effusion may be the only sign of an articular lesion.
- A symmetrical bilateral finding is not thereby normal.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the examination cannot answer the question the treatment
turns on, which is whether the fragment is stable.

The mechanical history is the clinical proxy for stability and it is an
imperfect one, because a stable lesion can catch and an unstable one can be
silent. 4.17.2 covers mechanical symptoms.

Skeletal maturity governs the prognosis and the clinical assessment of maturity
is approximate, so the imaging carries that question too. 4.19.1 covers the
growth plate assessment.

A healed lesion can leave a residual contour abnormality that remains tender
without being active, and the examination does not separate the two. 4.17.5
covers articular cartilage defects.

Pain from the lesion and pain from the altered loading around it are not
separable clinically, and only the second responds to alignment correction.
3.5.6 covers the effects of malalignment.

Repeated examination over time is more informative than any single assessment
here, which makes the record the useful instrument rather than the findings.
4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

### Key Learning Points

- The mechanical history is an imperfect proxy for fragment stability.
- Clinical assessment of skeletal maturity is approximate.
- A healed lesion can remain tender without being active.
- Pain from the lesion and from the altered loading are not separable clinically.
- Repeated examination over time beats any single assessment.
