# Hoop Stress

The idea the whole chapter rests on. Squeezing a wedge-shaped ring makes it
try to spread outwards, and the ring holds itself together in tension.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A barrel is held together by iron hoops. The staves push outwards and the
hoops resist in tension, which is the only reason the barrel holds.

A ring squeezed from above tries to get wider. If it cannot get wider, the
material around the ring is put into tension instead.

That tension running around the circumference is hoop stress. It is a tensile
stress produced by a compressive load, which is the part that takes some
getting used to.

### At the Knee

The meniscus is a wedge: thick at the outside, thin at the inside. Loading it
from above pushes it outwards and downwards.

It cannot escape outwards, because it is attached at both ends and tethered at
its rim. So the compression becomes circumferential tension.

The roots carry that tension into bone. Without them the ring has nothing to
pull against. 3.6.9 covers roots.

That is how the meniscus spreads load. It converts a vertical push into a
tension it carries around and out, instead of letting the condyle sink into
the plateau.

### What Changes It

Whether the ring is continuous. A tear across it interrupts the tension path.
3.6.12 covers radial tears.

Whether the roots are attached. A detached root leaves a complete ring that
carries nothing. 3.6.11 covers root tears.

Whether the meniscus is in the joint. An extruded meniscus is outside the
load. 3.6.10 covers extrusion.

Tissue quality, since tension needs material strong enough to carry it.

### Clinical Relevance

This is why a radial tear matters more than a longitudinal one of the same
length. One cuts the hoop and the other does not.

It is why a root tear behaves like a meniscectomy, and why the ring looking
intact on imaging is not reassuring. 5.6 covers imaging.

It is why repair aims to restore a tension path rather than to close a gap.
Section 7 covers technique.

Every mechanical statement in this chapter traces back to this one sentence.

### Key Learning Points

- Hoop stress is a tensile stress produced by a compressive load.
- A barrel's hoops hold it together in tension while the staves push outwards.
- The meniscal wedge is pushed outwards and the ring resists in tension.
- The roots carry that tension into bone.
- A radial tear cuts the hoop; a longitudinal tear of the same length may not.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

State it as a load path. Axial compression on the wedge produces a radially
outward force, which is resisted by circumferential tension in the deep fibre
bundles, which is transmitted through the roots into the tibia.

Each step in that chain is a point of failure, and each has its own clinical
lesion: a radial tear, an extrusion, a root avulsion.

The term hoop stress is conventional. Circumferential tensile stress is the
precise description, and the two are used interchangeably in the literature.

### At the Knee

The deep circumferential fibre bundles are the structural element carrying the
tension, oriented along the length of the meniscus. 3.6.3 covers the
architecture.

Radial tie fibres cross them and resist longitudinal splitting, which is a
different mechanical job. 3.6.4 covers them.

The wedge cross section converts compression to radial displacement, which is
why a flattened or degenerate meniscus converts less of it.

Conventional teaching holds that the peripheral third carries most of the hoop
tension, which is the mechanical reason peripheral tissue is preserved
preferentially.

### What Changes It

Fibre continuity, which a radial tear interrupts in proportion to its depth.

Root attachment position and integrity, since tension needs an anchor in the
right place.

Peripheral rim integrity and the coronary attachments, which prevent the
meniscus escaping outwards.

Tissue stiffness, which falls with degeneration and with repeated loading.

### How It Is Measured

Strain gauges and optical strain measurement on cadaveric menisci under load
measure the circumferential strain directly.

Root load cells measure the tension arriving at the attachment, which is the
hoop tension's destination.

Contact pressure studies measure the consequence of losing it rather than the
tension itself.

Finite element modelling computes the stress distribution within the meniscus
and depends on its material assumptions.

### Clinical Relevance

Preserve the peripheral third preferentially, since it carries most of the
tension.

Treat a radial tear's depth as the mechanical variable rather than its length.

Read a root avulsion as a complete failure of the tension path.

Expect a flattened or degenerate meniscus to convert less compression into
tension.

### Key Learning Points

- The load path is compression, radial displacement, circumferential tension, root, bone.
- Each step has its own clinical lesion.
- The deep circumferential bundles carry the tension; radial tie fibres do a different job.
- Conventional teaching gives the peripheral third most of the hoop tension.
- A radial tear's depth is the mechanical variable, not its length.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat hoop tension as the quantity every meniscal operation is judged against.
A repair restores it or does not; a resection reduces it in proportion; a
transplant restores part of it.

The framing makes some disagreements disappear. Whether to repair a particular
tear is a question about whether tension can be restored, which is answerable
from the pattern rather than from preference.

It also explains the failures. A repair that heals without restoring the fibre
path leaves a meniscus that looks healed and carries less, which is consistent
with the gap between healing rates and clinical outcomes. 6.42 covers failed
repair.

### What Changes It

Tear pattern and depth, which decide whether the fibre path can be
reconstituted.

Repair construct and its orientation, since a suture across the fibres
restores the path and one along them does not. 3.6.13 covers repair.

Root repair position and tension, which restore the anchor. 3.6.9 covers
roots.

Alignment and load, which determine the tension the restored path must carry.
3.13 covers alignment.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric strain and contact studies compare constructs for restored tension
and restored contact area, which are related and not identical.

Cyclic loading in a rig measures gap formation, which is the construct losing
the tension path under repeated load.

Clinical healing on imaging or second look measures continuity rather than
tension.

No measurement confirms restored hoop tension in a patient, which is why
outcome data carries the argument.

### Clinical Relevance

Orientate a repair construct across the circumferential fibres, not along
them.

Decide repairability from whether the tension path can be restored.

Expect healed appearance and restored tension to differ, and judge by outcome.

Repair a root to its anatomic position, since tension depends on where the
anchor sits.

### Key Learning Points

- Hoop tension is the quantity every meniscal operation is judged against.
- Repairability is a question about whether tension can be restored.
- A repair can heal without restoring the fibre path.
- Cyclic gap formation is the construct losing the tension path.
- No measurement confirms restored hoop tension in a patient.
