# Tunnel View

The intercondylar notch projected open, which is where loose bodies and the
posterior condyle hide.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A tunnel view projects through the intercondylar notch with the knee flexed, so
the notch is seen open rather than foreshortened. 5.2.2 covers the image
formation.

That brings the notch walls, the posterior parts of the condyles and the
intercondylar region into view. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

It matters because a loose body, an osteochondral lesion or a notch osteophyte
can be invisible on the standard views and visible here. 5.11.5 covers
osteochondral lesions.

### How It Is Acquired

Flex the knee and angle the beam so the notch is projected along its own axis.
5.2.5 covers magnification.

Keep the flexion and beam angle consistent, since both change which part of the
condyle is profiled. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Look specifically in the notch and at the posterior condylar surfaces, which is
what the view is for. 5.11.6 covers OCD.

Assess the notch width, since notch narrowing accompanies arthritis and has been
associated with cruciate injury. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

Pair it with the standard views rather than using it alone. 5.3.1 covers the
anteroposterior view.

### What Normal Looks Like

An open intercondylar notch with smooth walls and no bony fragment within it.

Smooth posterior condylar articular surfaces. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

A notch of proportionate width relative to the condyles. 5.7.1 covers the normal
cruciate.

No separate ossific density in the joint. 5.14.6 covers synovial chondromatosis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A loose ossific body within the notch or the posterior joint. 5.14.6 covers
synovial chondromatosis.

A lucent or fragmented area of the condyle, typically the lateral aspect of the
medial condyle. 5.11.6 covers OCD.

Notch osteophyte narrowing the notch. 5.3.18 covers osteophytes.

An avulsed bony fragment at the cruciate attachment. 5.3.14 covers avulsion
injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- The notch is projected open rather than foreshortened.
- Loose bodies and posterior condylar lesions become visible.
- Flexion and beam angle decide which condyle surface is profiled.
- Osteochondritis dissecans has a characteristic site.
- The view supplements the standard views rather than replacing them.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the view to request when a loose body or an osteochondral
lesion is suspected and the standard films are normal.

The expected points are the flexion and beam angle, the consistency, the notch
and posterior condyles, the notch width, and the pairing.

Say that this view finds what the standard views foreshorten. 5.3.1 covers the
anteroposterior view.

Say that the classic osteochondritis dissecans site is seen here. 5.11.6 covers
OCD.

Say that magnetic resonance imaging characterises what this view detects. 5.11.5
covers osteochondral lesions.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the notch is a recess, so a beam crossing it
obliquely superimposes its walls and anything inside it on the condyles. 5.2.2
covers the image formation.

Projecting along the notch axis removes that superimposition, which is why the
same fragment appears here and not on the anteroposterior view. 5.2.5 covers
magnification.

The posterior condyle contacts the tibia in flexion, so a lesion there is loaded
in flexion and is both clinically relevant and radiographically hidden on an
extended view. 3.5.4 covers the effects of flexion.

Notch narrowing in arthritis is osteophyte, and its association with cruciate
injury is a separate and weaker claim. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

### How Good It Is

The tunnel view detects what the standard views conceal and characterises
nothing.

Its best contribution is a loose body or an osteochondral fragment that would
otherwise be missed, which is a detection role. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral
lesions.

It is limited to detection, since staging an osteochondral lesion needs magnetic
resonance imaging. 5.11.6 covers OCD.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no notch
width index or beam angle appears either: those are published protocol
quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- A beam crossing the notch obliquely superimposes its contents on the condyles.
- Projecting along the notch axis removes that superimposition.
- The posterior condyle is loaded in flexion and hidden on an extended view.
- Notch narrowing in arthritis is osteophyte.
- The view detects and does not characterise.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this view is worth requesting when the question is a fragment
and the standard films are unremarkable.

A locking knee with normal standard films is the clearest indication, because a
loose body has a mechanical answer. 5.6.6 covers bucket handle tears.

A suspected osteochondritis dissecans lesion in a young patient is detected here
and staged elsewhere. 5.11.6 covers OCD.

Notch osteophyte limiting extension is a finding with a surgical answer. 5.3.18
covers osteophytes.

A normal tunnel view does not exclude a cartilaginous loose body, which is
invisible on any radiograph. 5.14.6 covers synovial chondromatosis.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is excluding a loose body because the radiographs are
normal, when a cartilaginous body is radiolucent. 5.14.6 covers synovial
chondromatosis.

A normal standard series is accepted without the tunnel view being considered.
5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

An osteochondral lesion is staged from this view. 5.11.6 covers OCD.

A fabella or a normal ossicle is read as a loose body. 5.3.12 covers patellar
fractures.

Notch narrowing is interpreted as cruciate risk on weak grounds. 5.7.1 covers
the normal cruciate.

### Key Learning Points

- A cartilaginous loose body is radiolucent and this view cannot show it.
- A normal standard series is accepted without considering this view.
- An osteochondral lesion is staged from a plain film.
- A normal ossicle is read as a loose body.
- Notch narrowing is over-interpreted as cruciate risk.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the tunnel view is a cheap film with a narrow indication that
is both under-requested and over-interpreted.

Its detection advantage is real and its characterisation ability is nil, so a
positive finding always leads to another study and a negative finding excludes
less than it appears to. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral lesions.

The notch width index and its association with cruciate injury has a literature
that predates routine magnetic resonance imaging, and the association has not
translated into a usable risk measure. 5.21.2 covers the cruciate imaging
literature.

Magnetic resonance imaging detects everything this view detects and more, so the
view survives on cost and availability rather than on capability. 5.1.5 covers
the cost effectiveness of imaging.

Where a cross sectional study is already planned, this view adds nothing, and
requesting it reflexively is the commoner waste. 5.1.4 covers imaging pathways.

The defensible position is that this is a useful first line film where cross
sectional imaging is not immediately available, and redundant where it is. 5.1.3
covers choosing the correct investigation.

### Key Learning Points

- Real detection advantage and no characterisation ability.
- A positive leads to another study and a negative excludes less than it seems.
- The notch width literature predates routine magnetic resonance imaging.
- The view survives on cost and availability rather than capability.
- It is redundant where cross sectional imaging is already planned.
