# Jumper's Knee

Tendinopathy at the patellar tendon's origin, which is a clinical diagnosis and
is treated by load rather than by an operation.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Jumper's knee is tendinopathy of the patellar tendon at its origin on the lower
pole of the patella, in a patient whose sport involves repeated jumping or
deceleration.

Examining for it means localising tenderness precisely to the lower pole and
reproducing the pain with a loaded task, which is how the diagnosis is made.
4.16.2 covers the tendon.

It matters because it presents as anterior knee pain in an athlete and is
frequently investigated as something intra-articular. 4.15.9 covers the
alternative.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history: pain at the front of the knee that comes on with jumping or
landing, worse at the start of activity, and worse after rather than during in
the early stages. 4.2.11 covers aggravating factors.

Palpate the lower pole of the patella with the knee extended and relaxed, where
the tenderness is focal and reproducible. 4.6.6 covers tendon tenderness.

Repeat with the knee flexed and the tendon tensioned, where the tenderness
typically reduces.

Ask the patient to perform a single leg decline squat or a single leg squat, and
note whether it reproduces the pain. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing.

Test resisted extension and note whether that reproduces it too.

Assess the quadriceps and the proximal control, since both contribute to the
load the tendon carries. 4.4.10 covers functional screening.

### What a Positive Means

Focal tenderness at the lower pole, reduced when the tendon is tensioned, with
pain reproduced by a loaded single leg task, is the characteristic pattern.

Pain that is worse at the start of activity and eases as it continues, in the
early stages, is a tendinopathy pattern rather than a joint one. 4.2.11 covers
it.

Diffuse tenderness along the whole tendon raises a different problem, and a
partial tear or an enthesopathy are both possibilities. 4.16.2 covers the
tendon.

Tenderness at the tubercle rather than the lower pole is an insertional problem,
and in an adolescent an apophysitis. 4.16.4 covers it.

A normal examination in an athlete with the characteristic history still
supports the diagnosis, because the finding depends on the stage. 4.2.9 covers
onset.

### Key Learning Points

- The tenderness is focal at the lower pole and reduces when the tendon is tensioned.
- Pain worse at the start of activity and easing as it continues is a tendinopathy pattern.
- A loaded single leg task reproduces it, which is part of the diagnosis.
- Diffuse tenderness raises a partial tear or an enthesopathy instead.
- Tenderness at the tubercle is an insertional problem, not this one.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the answer when asked about anterior knee pain in a jumping
athlete, and the precise localisation is what separates it from a guess.

The expected points are the history pattern, the focal tenderness at the lower
pole, the two tendon positions, the loaded single leg task, and the proximal
assessment.

Say that the diagnosis is clinical and that imaging stages it rather than makes
it, which is unusual in this section. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

Say that the treatment is load management and that surgery is a late and
uncommon option, which is the clinically important consequence.

Say that you would assess the proximal control and the ankle, since both change
the load the tendon takes in landing. 4.4.5 covers the proximal pattern.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the enthesis carries the highest stress in the
tendon during eccentric loading, which is why the pathology sits at the origin
and why jumping sports produce it. 2.4.3 covers patellar biomechanics.

Deceleration and landing load the tendon eccentrically, which is a higher load
than concentric extension, and that is why the single leg decline squat
reproduces the pain so reliably. 3.14.8 covers landing.

The pathology is degenerative rather than inflammatory in the established
stages, which is the basis for load based treatment rather than
anti-inflammatory treatment.

Proximal weakness increases the load transmitted through the knee in landing,
which is why hip and trunk control are part of the assessment and the treatment.
3.12.7 covers dynamic valgus.

### How Good It Is

The clinical diagnosis is reliable when the tenderness is focal and a loaded
task reproduces the pain, which makes the examination the reference standard
here.

It is less reliable early, when the tenderness can be subtle and the pain only
appears after activity.

Imaging stages the tendon and does not change the early management, which is why
the examination leads. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

It cannot distinguish tendinopathy from a partial tear, and in a tendon that has
failed to respond that distinction becomes relevant. 4.16.2 covers it.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The enthesis carries the highest stress during eccentric loading, which is why the pathology sits there.
- Deceleration and landing load the tendon eccentrically, which is why the decline squat reproduces the pain.
- The established pathology is degenerative rather than inflammatory.
- Proximal weakness increases the load transmitted in landing.
- The examination is the reference standard, and imaging stages rather than diagnoses.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this diagnosis is one where the examination's main job is to
prevent an operation on the joint.

An athlete with focal lower pole tenderness and a reproducible loaded pain needs
load management and a programme, and an arthroscopy for it achieves nothing.
4.15.9 covers the parallel.

A tendon that has failed a properly delivered programme over months is the group
in which surgery is discussed, and the imaging and the duration carry that
decision rather than the examination.

A patient whose symptoms are bilateral and insertional raises an enthesopathy,
which moves the specialty rather than the operation. 4.2.14 covers inflammatory
pain.

In a patient being considered for a bone patellar tendon bone graft, a
tendinopathic tendon is an argument for a different graft. 4.20.1 covers that
assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is investigating the joint rather than palpating the tendon.

The second is recording anterior knee tenderness rather than the exact position.

The third is treating it as an inflammatory problem in its established stages.

The fourth is omitting the proximal assessment, which is where the modifiable
contributor usually is. 4.4.10 covers screening.

The fifth is offering surgery before a programme has actually been delivered, as
opposed to recommended. 4.2.34 covers previous rehabilitation.

### Key Learning Points

- The examination's main job here is preventing an operation on the joint.
- A tendon that has failed a properly delivered programme over months is the surgical group.
- Bilateral insertional symptoms raise an enthesopathy and move the specialty.
- A tendinopathic tendon argues for a different graft choice.
- Offering surgery before a programme has been delivered, rather than recommended, is an error.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this diagnosis arrives either late or wrongly attached, and
the examination sorts both.

An athlete with a normal arthroscopy and persisting anterior pain, tender at the
lower pole, had the diagnosis available at the first consultation. 4.16.2
covers the palpation.

An athlete whose programme has failed may not have had a programme: establishing
what was actually done, at what load and for how long, is the first step rather
than imaging. 4.2.34 covers previous rehabilitation.

A patient with a previous graft harvest and lower pole pain has donor site pain,
which is a different problem with a different prognosis. 4.20.1 covers that
assessment.

An elite athlete's management belongs with their own staff, and the surgeon's
role is the decision about surgery rather than the load programme. 4.23.2
covers those patients.

In medicolegal work this diagnosis is difficult to attribute to a single event,
because it is an overload condition, and saying so plainly is the honest
position. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal arthroscopy with lower pole tenderness had the diagnosis available at the first consultation.
- An athlete whose programme failed may not have had a programme.
- Donor site pain after a graft harvest is a different problem with a different prognosis.
- In an elite athlete the surgeon's role is the surgical decision rather than the programme.
- As an overload condition it is hard to attribute to a single event.
