# Meniscal Root Tears

The tear that examines worst and matters most. Why the standard tests miss it
and what does find it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A meniscal root tear is a detachment of the meniscus at its tibial attachment
rather than a tear through its body.

That matters mechanically rather than just anatomically: the root anchors the
circumferential fibres, so detaching it releases hoop tension and the meniscus
stops distributing load. 3.6.9 covers root biomechanics.

Examining for it is difficult, because the standard provocative tests trap a
mobile fragment and a detached root has no fragment to trap.

So this page is largely about why the examination underperforms and what to
rely on instead.

### How It Is Performed

Palpate the posterior joint line specifically, with the knee flexed, which is
where the root sits and where the standard examination stops short.

Ask about pain in deep flexion and on rising from a squat, which is when the
posterior horn is loaded. 4.9.6 covers the squat test.

Look for an early loss of joint space or a new varus alignment, because a root
tear can produce rapid degenerative change and extrusion. 4.5.2 covers varus
alignment.

Assess for an effusion and for a loss of extension, which are both less common
than with a body tear.

Take the mechanism history carefully: a deep squat, a forced flexion, or in
older patients no injury at all. 4.2.18 covers twisting injuries.

### What a Positive Means

Posterior joint line tenderness with pain in deep flexion, in a patient with a
squatting or forced flexion mechanism, is the clinical picture.

An absence of mechanical symptoms does not argue against a root tear, which is
the opposite of the body tear pattern.

A new or progressing varus deformity in a patient with recent medial pain
raises a medial root tear with extrusion and is an important and
time-sensitive picture.

A normal examination is common and does not exclude the diagnosis, which is
why this page exists.

The diagnosis is usually made on imaging, and the examination's job is to
raise the question.

### Key Learning Points

- A root tear detaches the meniscus at its tibial attachment and releases hoop tension.
- The standard tests trap a fragment, and a detached root has no fragment to trap.
- Palpate the posterior joint line specifically, which the standard examination stops short of.
- An absence of mechanical symptoms does not argue against a root tear.
- A normal examination is common and does not exclude the diagnosis.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning is that hoop tension is what makes the meniscus work, so losing
the root is mechanically closer to losing the meniscus than to tearing it.
3.6.2 covers hoop stress.

That is why a root tear produces extrusion, load concentration and rapid
degenerative change, and why it is treated more aggressively than a comparable
body tear.

It is also why the examination findings are those of a functionally
meniscus-deficient knee rather than those of a torn meniscus. 4.9.10 covers
deficiency.

In an older patient a root tear frequently presents with new medial pain and a
rapidly progressing varus, and the differential includes a subchondral
insufficiency fracture, which presents similarly and needs separating.

In a younger patient it usually accompanies a cruciate injury, because the
mechanism produces both. 4.10.2 covers acute cruciate injury.

### How Good It Is

The clinical examination performs poorly for root tears, worse than for any
other lesion in this chapter, and stating that plainly is more useful than
listing tests.

The reasons are mechanical: no mobile fragment to trap, frequently no
mechanical symptoms, and tenderness in a posterior location the routine
examination palpates least well.

Posterior joint line tenderness and deep flexion pain are the most useful
findings available, and neither is specific.

The history and the imaging carry the diagnosis, and the examination's
contribution is to raise the question in a patient whose presentation would
otherwise be called degenerative.

- Losing the root is mechanically closer to losing the meniscus than to tearing it.

- The findings are those of a functionally deficient meniscus rather than a torn one.
- In older patients a subchondral insufficiency fracture presents similarly and needs separating.
- The examination performs worse here than for any other lesion in this chapter.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a root tear changes the operation, so recognising it matters
more than the examination's weakness would suggest.

A repairable root in a knee with preserved cartilage and acceptable alignment
is a repair rather than a resection, and resecting it accelerates the
degeneration it causes. 4.20.3 covers root repair assessment.

A root tear in a varus knee with established degeneration raises the question
of realignment alongside or instead of any meniscal procedure. 4.18.3 covers
varus disease.

The examination's contribution is the alignment assessment and the progression
history rather than any provocative test, which is an unusual conclusion for a
chapter of tests.

A new varus with recent medial pain should prompt urgent imaging rather than a
course of physiotherapy.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is labelling a root tear presentation as degenerative
disease, because the patient is older and the radiographs show change.

The second is resecting a repairable root, which converts a reparable
mechanical problem into a permanent one.

The third is relying on the provocative tests, which perform badly here, and
recording a negative examination as reassuring.

And missing the alignment. A root tear with extrusion and progressive varus is
a different problem from a root tear in a neutral knee, and only the alignment
assessment separates them. 4.5.1 covers mechanical axis assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- A root tear changes the operation, so recognising it matters despite the weak examination.
- Resecting a repairable root accelerates the degeneration the tear causes.
- A root tear in a varus knee raises realignment alongside or instead of a meniscal procedure.
- The examination's contribution here is alignment and progression rather than any test.
- New varus with recent medial pain warrants urgent imaging rather than physiotherapy.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the root tear appears as a rapidly deteriorating knee in
a patient who was told they had arthritis, or as a failed repair.

The first is the more common and the more costly, because a window for repair
existed and was missed, and the history of how quickly the knee changed is
what identifies it.

A failed root repair presents with the same mechanical picture as the original
tear, and distinguishing failure from progression of the cartilage damage is
difficult clinically and usually requires imaging. 4.20.3 covers the
assessment.

A root repair in a malaligned knee that was not realigned is a recognised
reason for failure, and the alignment is the part of the assessment that
answers it.

The honest conclusion of this page is that the examination raises the question
and almost never answers it, which is unusual in this chapter and should be
taught as such.

### Key Learning Points

- The common referral is a rapidly deteriorating knee labelled as arthritis.
- How quickly the knee changed is what identifies it.
- A failed repair looks like the original tear, and imaging usually separates them.
- A root repair in a knee that was not realigned is a recognised reason for failure.
- The examination raises the question here and almost never answers it.
