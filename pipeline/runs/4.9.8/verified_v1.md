# Mechanical Locking

The one finding in this chapter that stands alone. How to confirm a block and
what to do about it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Mechanical locking is a physical block to extension caused by something
interposed in the joint. Confirming it is the most important thing this
chapter does.

It matters because it is the one meniscal finding that is a surgical
indication on its own, and because the thing causing it is often repairable
now and not later.

4.2.5 covers the history. This page covers confirming it on examination.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the patient to straighten the knee fully themselves, and compare with the
other side.

Then attempt passive extension gently, supporting the heel and letting gravity
assist, and feel for the endpoint.

Characterise the endpoint. A hard, abrupt endpoint with a springy resistance
is a block. A soft endpoint that creeps further with gentle sustained pressure
is not. 4.7.5 covers end feel.

Measure and record the loss of extension in degrees, and record the endpoint
character beside it. 4.1.8 covers the record.

Do not force extension. A block that is forced can be made worse, and the
examination is complete once the endpoint is characterised.

### What a Positive Means

A fixed loss of extension with a hard springy endpoint, in a knee with a
matching history, is a mechanical block and the commonest cause is a displaced
meniscal fragment.

A bucket handle tear is the classic pattern, and in a young knee it is
frequently repairable if addressed promptly.

A loose body gives a block at variable positions rather than a fixed loss of
extension. 4.17.3 covers osteochondral lesions.

A torn cruciate stump can block extension after an acute injury, which is a
recognised and less common cause.

A soft endpoint with a loss of extension is pain, effusion or a developing
contracture rather than a block. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

### Key Learning Points

- A block is a physical obstruction to extension, and confirming it is this chapter's most important job.
- It is the one meniscal finding that is a surgical indication on its own.
- Characterise the endpoint: hard and springy is a block, soft and creeping is not.
- Record the loss in degrees and the endpoint character beside it.
- Do not force extension, because a forced block can be made worse.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected sequence is active extension, comparison, gentle
passive extension, endpoint characterisation, measurement, and a statement
that you would not force it.

The endpoint is the mark. A candidate who records a loss of extension without
characterising the endpoint has not distinguished a block from a contracture.

Say that you would examine the other knee, because a patient with
physiological hyperextension has a loss of extension relative to themselves
that is easy to miss. 4.7.3 covers hyperextension.

Say what you would do next, which for a confirmed block in a young knee is
urgent rather than routine.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning is mechanical: a fragment displaced into the intercondylar
region physically prevents the femur completing its roll and glide into
extension. 3.3.2 covers roll and glide.

That produces a hard endpoint, because bone is meeting an interposed structure
rather than a tightening capsule.

A contracture produces a soft endpoint, because the limiting structure is
elastic.

Pain inhibition produces a variable endpoint that changes with reassurance and
with analgesia, and re-examining after analgesia is a legitimate step.

An effusion produces a mild loss of extension that resolves as the effusion
does.

### How Good It Is

A confirmed mechanical block is the most specific finding in this chapter by a
wide margin, and it is the only one that acts alone.

Its sensitivity for a meniscal tear is low, because most tears do not lock, so
it is a finding to confirm rather than a test to screen with.

Examiner agreement on the endpoint is better than agreement on the provocative
tests, because a hard endpoint is a more discrete observation than a click.

The acute knee is the exception: guarding and effusion make the endpoint hard
to characterise, and re-examination after a few days is more reliable than
persisting.

No figures appear, under decision 023, and the claim is the ranking against
the other findings in this chapter.

### Key Learning Points

- A fragment physically prevents the femur completing its roll into extension.
- A block gives a hard endpoint; a contracture gives a soft one; pain gives a variable one.
- Re-examining after analgesia is a legitimate step.
- It is the most specific finding in this chapter and the only one that acts alone.
- Its sensitivity is low, so it is a finding to confirm rather than a test to screen with.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a confirmed block changes the pathway from elective to
urgent, and it is the one place in this chapter where the examination alone
justifies an operation.

It is also the exception to the arthroscopic meniscectomy trial evidence.
Those trials recruited knees with mechanical symptoms rather than knees that
were locked, and applying them to a locked knee misreads them. 3.17.3 covers
the point.

The urgency is about repairability rather than about the block. A displaced
peripheral tear in a young knee may be repairable now, and a delay converts a
repair into a resection. 4.2.9 covers onset.

In an older degenerate knee a block is still an indication and the operation
is more often a resection, so the urgency is lower and the symptom relief is
the aim.

A knee with a soft endpoint and a loss of extension does not need urgent
surgery and frequently needs rehabilitation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting a reported locking without confirming a
block, which sends a knee to arthroscopy on a history alone. 4.2.5 covers the
history.

The second is the reverse: dismissing a genuine block in a young knee because
the imaging was reported as a small tear.

The third is failing to compare with the other side in a hyperextending
patient, where a ten degree loss relative to their own normal is a real block
that reads as full extension on its own. 4.7.3 covers hyperextension.

And forcing extension to prove the point, which can displace a reducible
fragment further and is never necessary.

### Key Learning Points

- A confirmed block changes the pathway from elective to urgent.
- It is the exception to the meniscectomy trial evidence, which recruited symptoms not blocks.
- The urgency is about repairability: delay converts a repair into a resection.
- In a degenerate knee a block is still an indication, with lower urgency and symptom relief as the aim.
- Compare with the other side, because a hyperextending patient's block reads as full extension.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

A block after knee surgery has its own differential and the meniscus is not at
the top of it.

After a cruciate reconstruction, a cyclops lesion produces a block near
extension and is frequently recorded as stiffness instead, which delays its
treatment. 4.20.1 covers the assessment.

After any arthroscopy, a retained fragment or a loose body can block, and the
history of whether the knee was ever straight after the operation separates
the two.

After an arthroplasty, a block raises impingement, a retained osteophyte,
cement or a component issue rather than a meniscal cause. 4.20.8 covers the
assessment.

In a multiply operated knee the distinction between a block and arthrofibrosis
is the one that matters most, because the treatments differ completely, and
the endpoint character is the main clinical discriminator available.

No figure here supports these claims, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- A block after surgery has its own differential and the meniscus is not at the top of it.
- A cyclops lesion blocks near extension and is frequently recorded as stiffness.
- Whether the knee was ever straight after the operation separates a retained fragment from a contracture.
- After arthroplasty a block raises impingement, osteophyte, cement or component issues.
- The endpoint character is the main clinical discriminator between a block and arthrofibrosis.
