# Tibial Tubercle Position

Where the patellar tendon attaches sets the direction and the lever arm of the
distal pull, and it is the one variable surgery can change precisely.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A rope's effect depends on where it is tied as well as how hard it is pulled.

Move the attachment sideways and the direction of pull changes. Move it
further from the joint and the lever arm changes.

So one attachment point controls two separate things, and a change to it
affects both.

### At the Knee

The patellar tendon runs from the bottom of the patella to the tibial
tubercle, a bump on the front of the shin. 2.2.5 covers the tubercle.

If the tubercle sits further outwards, the tendon pulls the patella outwards
harder. 3.12.6 covers the Q angle.

If the tendon is long, or the tubercle sits low, the patella rides high and
enters the groove later. 3.12.1 covers engagement.

A high patella is called patella alta, and it is one of the four factors in
instability. 3.12.8 covers them.

### What Changes It

The position the tubercle grew in, which is fixed in adults.

Tendon length, which sets how high the patella sits.

Rotation of the shin bone, which moves the tubercle round the front of the
leg. 3.12.11 covers it.

Surgery that moves the tubercle deliberately. 7.53 covers the operation.

### Clinical Relevance

This is the one thing in the joint that an operation can move precisely, which
is why it attracts attention.

Moving it inwards reduces the outward pull. Moving it downwards lowers a high
patella. 7.53 covers the operation.

Moving it in the wrong direction or too far causes problems of its own.
3.12.10 covers the wear that follows.

5.12.2 covers how patellar height is measured.

### Key Learning Points

- A rope's effect depends on where it is tied as well as how hard it is pulled.
- A more lateral tubercle pulls the patella laterally harder.
- A long tendon or a low tubercle gives a high riding patella.
- Patella alta is one of the four instability factors.
- The tubercle is the one thing surgery can move precisely.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The tubercle sets two independent quantities: the transverse offset of the
distal pull, and the height at which the patella sits.

They are independent mechanically and are often moved together surgically,
which is a common source of overcorrection.

Describing a knee therefore needs both a transverse index and a height ratio,
not one number.

### At the Knee

Transverse offset is measured as the tubercle to groove distance, with quoted
abnormal values above about 20 millimetres and a soft threshold. 5.12.4 covers
it.

Patellar height is measured by ratios of tendon length to patellar length or
to tibial landmarks, and the ratios do not agree with each other. 5.12.2
covers them.

A high patella delays engagement, which lengthens the arc in which only the
medial soft tissue restrains. 3.12.5 covers the ligament.

A lateral tubercle raises the lateral vector throughout the arc rather than
only near extension.

The two therefore produce different clinical pictures: alta gives instability
near extension, lateralisation gives it more broadly. 3.12.8 covers the
factors.

### What Changes It

Developmental tubercle position and tendon length, which are fixed in the
adult.

Tibial torsion, which rotates the tubercle laterally without changing the
bone's own position. 3.12.11 covers it.

Femoral version, which raises the same index from the other end of the joint.

Knee flexion during imaging, which reduces the measured tubercle to groove
distance.

Previous tubercle surgery, which changes both quantities at once. 7.53 covers
it.

### How It Is Measured

Tubercle to groove distance on computed tomography or magnetic resonance
imaging, with the knee position affecting the result. 5.12.4 covers the
technique.

Patellar tendon to trochlear groove distance, which excludes tubercle rotation
from the measurement.

Patellar height ratios on a lateral radiograph or on sagittal imaging, which
disagree systematically. 5.12.2 covers them.

Cadaveric transfer studies, which measure the pressure and displacement effect
of moving the tubercle a set distance. 3.16.1 covers them.

No index reports the transverse offset and the height together, so both must
be stated.

### Clinical Relevance

Report the tubercle to groove distance and a named height ratio together,
because they describe different problems.

Record the knee position at imaging, since the transverse index depends on it.

Expect medialisation to reduce the lateral vector and distalisation to advance
engagement, and keep the two decisions separate. 7.53 covers the operation.

Avoid treating a single borderline index as an indication, because both
thresholds are soft. 3.12.8 covers the pattern.

### Key Learning Points

- The tubercle sets transverse offset and patellar height independently.
- They are often moved together surgically, which causes overcorrection.
- Abnormal tubercle to groove distance is quoted above about 20 millimetres, with a soft threshold.
- Height ratios disagree systematically, so the ratio must be named.
- Alta gives instability near extension; lateralisation gives it more broadly.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Tubercle transfer is the most mechanically direct operation in this joint,
which is why its limits are instructive.

Medialisation reduces the lateral vector and raises medial facet pressure. The
second effect is why overcorrection produces medial pain and medial wear.
3.12.10 covers the wear.

Anteriorisation reduces contact pressure by changing the lever arm and the
resultant, and its effect is largest in the flexion range where the joint is
most loaded. 3.12.3 covers pressure.

Distalisation advances engagement and shortens the soft tissue only window,
which is a different mechanism from medialisation. 3.12.1 covers engagement.

Cadaveric work supports each effect in isolation. The clinical results are
more modest, which is what happens when one term in a multifactorial problem
is corrected precisely. 6.153 covers the controversies.

### What Changes It

The direction and the magnitude of transfer, which are separate decisions and
are often taken as one.

Whether the underlying abnormality is tubercle position or femoral version,
because only the first is addressed here. 3.12.11 covers version.

Trochlear morphology, which limits what a vector correction can achieve.
3.12.4 covers it.

Cartilage status, because transfer moves load onto whatever surface receives
it. 3.12.10 covers the patterns.

Skeletal maturity, which rules out tubercle osteotomy in a growing knee. 7.94
covers paediatric practice.

### How It Is Measured

Preoperative tubercle to groove distance and a named height ratio, acquired at
a recorded knee position. 5.12.4 covers the technique.

Rotational profile imaging where the index is raised, to establish the source
before correcting the result. 3.12.11 covers it.

Intraoperative measurement of transfer distance, which is the only quantity
controlled directly.

Postoperative imaging, which confirms the geometric change and not the
pressure change.

No clinical measurement confirms that the intended pressure redistribution
occurred, which is a gap between the rationale and the verification.

### Clinical Relevance

Establish whether a raised index comes from the tubercle or from femoral
version before transferring the tubercle. 3.12.11 covers the distinction.

Decide direction and magnitude separately, since medialisation and
distalisation correct different mechanisms.

Limit medialisation, because medial facet overload is the characteristic
complication. 3.12.10 covers it.

State that the pressure redistribution is inferred rather than measured after
surgery.

Take the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations into the decision,
because a precise correction of one term does not by itself make the operation
right for this patient.

### Key Learning Points

- Tubercle transfer is the most mechanically direct operation in this joint.
- Medialisation reduces the lateral vector and raises medial facet pressure.
- Distalisation advances engagement, which is a different mechanism from medialisation.
- Cadaveric work supports each effect; clinical results are more modest.
- No measurement confirms the intended pressure redistribution after surgery.
