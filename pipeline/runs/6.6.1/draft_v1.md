# Epidemiology

The observation is solid and every explanation offered for it is weaker than the
observation itself.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

In the same sports, with the same training, girls and women tear this ligament
more often than boys and men do. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

That is a counted difference rather than an opinion. Why it happens is still
being argued about. 6.6.2 covers hormonal factors.

### Who Gets It

The sports where it shows up most are the ones with cutting, pivoting and
landing from a jump: football, netball, basketball, handball, skiing. 6.1.5
covers the mechanisms of injury.

It is not a difference in toughness or in effort, which is worth saying because
that is what young athletes are often told. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Key Learning Points

- In the same sports, female athletes tear the ACL more often.
- The difference is counted rather than claimed.
- Cutting, pivoting and landing sports show it most.
- The reason is still argued about.
- It is not a difference in toughness or effort.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

The sex difference in ACL injury rate is one of the better established findings
in sports injury, and the explanation for it is one of the weaker bodies of
evidence in this chapter. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Both halves of that sentence matter. The risk is real, and the mechanism being
offered to you may be presented with more confidence than it has earned. 6.6.2
covers hormonal factors.

### Who Gets It

The comparison only means something when the sport and the amount of play are
the same on both sides. Comparing a football squad with a swimming squad
measures the sport. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

Most of these injuries happen without contact, on a landing or a turn, which is
the reason prevention is possible at all. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Four groups of explanation are offered: anatomy, hormones, neuromuscular control
and the way sports are coached and resourced. 3.7.15 covers female ACL
biomechanics.

The neuromuscular group is the one that can be trained, which is why the
prevention page is the useful end of this chapter. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- The sex difference is well established.
- The explanations for it are not equally well established.
- A comparison must match the sport and the amount of play.
- Most of these injuries happen without contact.
- Neuromuscular control is the part that can be trained.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Does this mean my daughter should not play?
No. The risk belongs to the activity and so does the benefit, and the useful
response is training that reduces the risk rather than withdrawal from the
sport.

**Q.** Is it because of the shape of the female pelvis?
That is one of the anatomical explanations and it is not settled. Anatomical
factors cannot be changed, which is part of why attention moved to the factors
that can be.

**Q.** Can the risk be reduced?
At squad level there is evidence that structured neuromuscular training reduces
injuries. What that means for one individual player is a weaker claim, and the
prevention page is careful about the difference.

**Q.** Why is the explanation so unsettled when the difference is so clear?
Because counting injuries is straightforward and isolating a cause among
anatomy, hormones, control and coaching is not. The four candidates all vary
together in real athletes.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

The relevant quantity is incidence per athlete exposure rather than a count of
cases, because a count reflects participation. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

An athlete exposure is one athlete in one training session or match, and it is
the denominator that makes a sex comparison interpretable. 6.2.1 covers
non-contact injury.

### Who Gets It

This page carries no incidence ratio, because none has been verified in this
environment and an invented ratio would be worse than an absent one. 6.1.3
covers the epidemiology.

What can be stated is the shape: the difference appears in sport-matched
cohorts, it is largest in cutting and pivoting sports, and it is concentrated
in non-contact mechanisms. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

A reviewer with the literature can place the ratios into this section without
restructuring the page. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### Causes and Risk Factors

The candidate factors divide into non-modifiable and modifiable, and that
division is more useful clinically than the anatomical and hormonal headings.
3.7.15 covers female ACL biomechanics.

Non-modifiable candidates include intercondylar notch geometry, tibial slope,
generalised laxity and limb alignment. 3.7.12 covers ACL loading during
landing.

Modifiable candidates include landing mechanics, trunk control, hamstring to
quadriceps strength balance and training exposure. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular
factors.

Hormonal candidates sit awkwardly between the two, since the cycle is not
modifiable and its pharmacological modification is a separate question. 6.6.2
covers hormonal factors.

### Key Learning Points

- The interpretable quantity is incidence per athlete exposure.
- A case count reflects participation rather than risk.
- This page states the shape of the difference and no verified ratio.
- Dividing factors into modifiable and non-modifiable is the useful split.
- Hormonal factors sit awkwardly between the two.

## MRCS Level

### Who Gets It

In a viva the expected answer gives the denominator before the difference.

Say incidence per athlete exposure, sport-matched cohorts, and the

Say that the difference is established and the mechanism is contested, because

### Causes and Risk Factors

Group the factors as non-modifiable, modifiable and environmental, and name two
in each. 3.7.15 covers female ACL biomechanics.

The environmental group is the one candidates forget: coaching quality, playing
surface, footwear, training load and the resources a squad has. 6.6.4 covers

### Complications and Prognosis

The epidemiological difference does not stop at first injury. Rates of
contralateral and graft rupture are also reported as higher in young female
athletes, which matters for counselling about return to sport. 6.8.1 covers
graft failure.

Age interacts with sex here more strongly than either acts alone, and the young
pivoting athlete is where both converge. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature
patients.

### Key Learning Points

- Give the denominator before the difference.
- The difference is established and the mechanism is contested.
- Group factors as non-modifiable, modifiable and environmental.
- Candidates forget the environmental group.
- The difference extends to second injury, not just first.

## FRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

At examination level the candidate is asked what they would tell a young female
athlete and her parents at the first consultation.

The defensible answer separates what is known from what is offered. The elevated
rate is known. Which factor drives it is not, and a confident single cause
delivered in clinic becomes a belief the family then acts on. 6.6.2 covers
hormonal factors.

Second injury risk is the part of the conversation that gets left out, and it is
the part most relevant to a return to pivoting sport at seventeen. 6.8.1 covers
graft failure.

### Management Overview

Nothing on this page changes an operation. It changes the counselling and the
rehabilitation requirement before release to sport. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Release criteria matter more in this group precisely because the re-injury rate
is higher, so a time based release is harder to defend here than anywhere else
in the chapter. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is how much of the sex difference survives adjustment
for training exposure and coaching resource. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The second is whether the reported difference in graft failure reflects biology
or a difference in return to sport behaviour. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The third is whether screening can identify the individual at risk rather than
describe the group. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the group that arrives is the one that has already had the
second injury, and the record almost never says what the first return to sport
was based on.

A note saying returned to play at nine months tells the next surgeon nothing
about whether criteria were met or a season started. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The habit worth keeping is to record the release decision with its basis:
strength symmetry, hop test performance, the sport, the level, and whether the
athlete was released or returned on their own. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular
factors.

The second habit is to record training exposure in hours, because it is the one
variable in this chapter that is both measurable and routinely absent. 6.6.4
covers prevention.

Neither is in any standard operation note template, which is why both have to be
done deliberately. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- The arriving group has already had the second injury.
- Returned to play at nine months records nothing useful.
- Record the release decision together with its basis.
- Record training exposure in hours.
- Neither is in a standard template, so both need doing deliberately.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that this page's content is socially loaded, and a
family hears a statement about risk in a young woman's sport as a statement
about whether she should be playing it. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The position that holds up is that the risk is a reason to train differently
rather than a reason to withdraw, and that the benefits of the sport are not
part of the risk calculation unless somebody states them. 6.6.3 covers
neuromuscular factors.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which here includes
skeletal maturity and sets the second injury risk. Their medical comorbidities.
The demands they put on the knee, which in this group are set by a season, a
squad and a coach rather than by the patient alone. Their psychological state,
because fear of a second injury and determination to return both distort the
history. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which in this chapter
usually arrive as a cause already chosen from the internet.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and ask who decides when she
plays. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Families hear a risk statement as a verdict on the sport.
- The risk is a reason to train differently rather than to withdraw.
- The benefits of the sport are not in the calculation unless stated.
- Demand in this group is set by a season, a squad and a coach.
- Ask who decides when she plays.
