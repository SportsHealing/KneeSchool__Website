# Contact Injury

The force came from outside, so the structures it passed through are predictable
from its direction.

## For Young Learners

### Causes and Risk Factors

Some ACL tears do happen from a tackle or a collision. A blow to the outside of
the knee pushes the joint inwards while the foot stays planted. 6.1.5 covers the
mechanisms of injury.

Because the force comes from a known direction, it is possible to work out which
parts of the knee it went through. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### How It Presents

The knee swells as it does with any ACL tear, and there is usually bruising on
the outside where the blow landed. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

More than one thing is often injured at once, so these knees tend to be sorer
and more obviously wrong than a twisting injury. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Some ACL tears come from a tackle or collision.
- A blow to the outside pushes the knee inwards.
- The direction of the force says what it passed through.
- More than one structure is often injured at once.
- These knees tend to be more obviously wrong than twisting injuries.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Causes and Risk Factors

A valgus blow, meaning a force to the outside of the knee driving it inwards, is
the usual contact pattern. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

The force passes through the structures on the inside of the knee before
reaching the cruciate, which is why those are commonly injured together. 6.2.4
covers combined injuries.

Higher energy injuries, such as a fall from height or a road traffic collision,
produce more damage and more urgency. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

### How It Presents

Swelling within hours is the same signal as in any ACL injury. 6.2.5 covers
acute haemarthrosis.

Pain and tenderness on the inner side of the knee, in addition to the swelling,
suggests the medial structures were involved. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

A knee that feels loose in more than one direction needs assessing promptly
rather than at the next convenient appointment. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- A valgus blow is the usual contact pattern.
- The force reaches the inner structures before the cruciate.
- Those are commonly injured together.
- Higher energy injuries bring more damage and more urgency.
- Looseness in more than one direction needs prompt assessment.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is a contact injury worse than a twisting one?
Often, in the sense that more structures are involved. The cruciate injury
itself is not different; what changes the outlook is what else was damaged at
the same time.

**Q.** Why does the inside of my knee hurt when the ligament that tore is in the
middle?
Because a blow from the outside loads the inner structures first. The medial
ligament frequently takes part of the force, and its tenderness is a separate
finding from the cruciate.

**Q.** Does this change the treatment?
It can. A combined injury is managed as a pattern rather than as a single torn
ligament, and the order and timing of treatment depend on which structures are
involved.

**Q.** Should I be seen more urgently?
If the knee feels unstable in several directions, or if there is numbness,
coldness or a foot that will not move properly, then yes, urgently.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Causes and Risk Factors

The classical contact mechanism is a valgus force applied to the lateral aspect
of the knee with the foot planted. 3.7.14 covers the ACL injury mechanisms.

The force is resisted by the medial structures first, so a medial collateral
injury commonly accompanies the cruciate one. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

Varus and rotational contact forces are less common and raise the posterolateral
structures instead. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Energy matters: a sporting tackle and a vehicle impact sit at different ends of
a range, and the second brings fracture and neurovascular questions. 6.2.3
covers hyperextension injury.

### How It Presents

Haemarthrosis is present as in any acute cruciate injury. 6.2.5 covers acute
haemarthrosis.

Localised tenderness along a collateral ligament is the additional finding, and
it is sought deliberately rather than noticed. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

Multi directional laxity, where it can be elicited, changes the diagnosis from a
single ligament injury to a pattern. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Examination

Test the collaterals as well as the cruciates, because the mechanism predicts
them. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Examine the limb neurologically and vascularly in any high energy contact
injury, before anything else is arranged. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

### Key Learning Points

- A valgus force to the lateral knee with a planted foot is classical.
- The medial structures resist it first.
- Varus and rotational contact raise the posterolateral structures.
- Energy separates a tackle from a vehicle impact.
- The limb is examined neurologically and vascularly at high energy.

## MRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

In a viva a contact mechanism should move the answer towards a pattern before
it moves towards the cruciate.

The expected points are the direction of force, the structures in its path, the
energy, the predicted combination, and the neurovascular question at high
energy. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Say that a contact mechanism raises the prior probability of multi ligament
injury materially. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

Say that multi ligament injury is a different clinical problem with different
urgency, not a more severe version of the same one. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension
injury.

### Examination

The examination is directed by the force's direction and then widened, because
the structures beyond its path are where the quiet injuries are. 4.10.9 covers
the rotational instability.

A guarded acute knee hides collateral laxity as readily as rotational
instability, so the examination is repeated. 4.10.10 covers the examination
under anaesthesia.

### Investigations and Imaging

The bruise pattern differs from the non-contact pattern and is read as such.
6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Medial structures, the posterolateral corner and the menisci are each assessed
rather than assumed from the cruciate finding. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

### Key Learning Points

- Move the answer to a pattern before moving it to the cruciate.
- Contact raises the chance of multi ligament injury materially.
- Multi ligament injury is a different problem, not a worse one.
- Guarding hides collateral laxity as readily as rotation.
- The bruise pattern differs and is read as such.

## FRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

At examination level a contact mechanism is a prompt to discuss the multi
ligament knee, and a candidate who stays on the cruciate has missed the
question.

The direction of force, the energy and the examination findings together define
which structures to reconstruct and in what order. 6.2.4 covers combined
injuries.

A medial injury alongside a cruciate one is often managed by allowing the medial
side to heal before reconstructing the cruciate, which is a timing decision
rather than a technical one. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

A lateral or posterolateral injury alongside changes that calculus, because
those structures heal less reliably. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is the optimal timing of cruciate reconstruction after a
healed collateral injury. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

The second is whether a contact mechanism independently predicts a worse outcome
once the associated injuries are accounted for. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

The third is how much the energy of the injury, as distinct from the structures
involved, should change the plan. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- A contact mechanism is a prompt to discuss the multi ligament knee.
- Force direction, energy and examination define the reconstruction order.
- A medial injury is usually a timing decision rather than a technical one.
- Lateral and posterolateral structures heal less reliably.
- Whether contact independently worsens outcome is unresolved.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the contact injury is where the chapter stops being about a
ligament.

The cruciate is often the least consequential structure in a high energy
contact knee, and treating it as the diagnosis delays the assessment that
matters. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

Stiffness is the characteristic complication of these knees, because more
tissue is injured and more of it is operated on, which makes the timing argument
sharper here than in the isolated injury. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A collateral injury that healed in a lengthened position leaves laxity that no
cruciate reconstruction corrects, and it is found at the examination under
anaesthesia rather than on the scan. 4.10.10 covers the examination under
anaesthesia.

The useful habit is to let the mechanism set the expected pattern, then
deliberately look for what the pattern does not predict. 6.4.4 covers ALL
injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- The cruciate is often the least consequential structure here.
- Stiffness is the characteristic complication.
- More injured tissue sharpens the timing argument.
- A collateral healed long leaves laxity no cruciate graft corrects.
- Let the mechanism set the pattern, then look past it.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question in a contact injury is sequencing, and it is decided
before any technical question is reached.

Which structures are reconstructed, in what order, and in how many operations is
the whole of the decision, and the evidence supporting any particular sequence
is thin because these injuries are uncommon and heterogeneous. 6.2.4 covers
combined injuries.

A single stage reconstruction of everything shortens the overall course and
raises the stiffness risk. Staging reduces that and asks the patient for a
second recovery. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The five things decide it rather than the literature. The patient's age and
physiology, which set healing and tolerance of a long rehabilitation. Their
medical comorbidities, because a high energy injury often arrives with others.
The demands they put on the knee, which decide how much laxity is acceptable.
Their psychological state, because two recoveries ask more than one. And their
own ideas, concerns and expectations, including what they can take time away
from.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

### Key Learning Points

- Sequencing is decided before any technical question.
- Evidence for a particular sequence is thin because the injuries are uncommon.
- Single stage shortens the course and raises the stiffness risk.
- Staging reduces that and asks for a second recovery.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide it.
