# Segond Fractures

A fleck of bone at the lateral tibial margin, which is worth more than its size.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A Segond fracture is an avulsion fragment from the lateral margin of the tibial
plateau. 2.11.4 covers the Segond lesion.

It arises from the anterolateral structures of the knee being pulled off under
rotational load. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

It matters because the fragment is small and its association with a cruciate tear
is strong, so a tiny radiographic finding predicts a major soft tissue injury.
5.7.3 covers complete cruciate tears.

### How It Is Acquired

Look at the lateral tibial margin on the anteroposterior view, just below the
joint line. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

Recognise it as a thin vertically oriented fleck parallel to the tibial cortex.
5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Assess the cruciate deliberately once it is seen, because the association is the
point. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Assess the posterolateral and anterolateral structures, which is where the
fragment came from. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

Proceed to a magnetic resonance study, since the radiograph has done its job by
raising the question. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A thin ossific fleck lateral to the tibial plateau margin, parallel to the
cortex.

A corresponding cortical irregularity at the donor site. 5.3.14 covers avulsion
injuries.

Associated findings of a cruciate injury: marrow signal, translation and an
effusion on the cross sectional study. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

Anterolateral soft tissue swelling on the same film. 2.11.3 covers the
anterolateral ligament.

### Key Learning Points

- The fragment comes from the lateral tibial plateau margin.
- It is avulsed by the anterolateral structures under rotational load.
- It is a thin fleck parallel to the tibial cortex.
- Its association with a cruciate tear is strong.
- A tiny finding predicts a major soft tissue injury.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the fracture to name when asked what a small lateral tibial
fleck means.

The expected points are the location, the appearance, the cruciate association,
the anterolateral and posterolateral structures, and the magnetic resonance
study.

Say that the fragment implies a significant rotational injury. 5.7.4 covers the
acute cruciate injury.

Say that its size is irrelevant to the injury's severity. 5.3.14 covers avulsion
injuries.

Say that it contributes to the argument for an anterolateral procedure alongside
a reconstruction. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the anterolateral structures restrain internal
rotation, so the same pivoting mechanism that tears the cruciate loads them, and
the fragment records that load. 3.7.7 covers rotational stability.

That is why the fragment is a marker of mechanism rather than a lesion needing
treatment in itself. 3.7.14 covers the injury mechanisms.

Its presence supports the argument that the anterolateral complex contributes to
rotational control, which is the question an extra-articular augmentation
addresses. 2.11.5 covers the anterolateral complex.

The fragment is cortical and thin, so it is the kind of finding a reader not
looking for it will pass over. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

### How Good It Is

The Segond fracture is a small finding with a high implication, and the radiograph
is where it is best seen.

Its best contribution is that a plain film raises a specific soft tissue question
nobody would otherwise have asked. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

It is limited by being absent in most cruciate injuries, so its absence excludes
nothing. 5.7.3 covers complete cruciate tears.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and the
comparison is the claim: the fragment is more informative than its size suggests,
which is a comparison rather than a number.

### Key Learning Points

- The anterolateral structures restrain internal rotation.
- The pivoting mechanism that tears the cruciate loads them.
- The fragment is a marker of mechanism rather than a lesion to treat.
- It supports the anterolateral complex's role in rotational control.
- Its absence excludes nothing.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this fragment contributes to the anterolateral augmentation
decision.

A cruciate tear with a Segond fracture is a rotational injury with documented
anterolateral involvement, which is part of the case for an extra-articular
procedure. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

It is a marker rather than an indication, so it contributes to a decision made on
several factors. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

Its presence on the injury films is worth recording, because it may not be visible
later. 5.7.5 covers chronic cruciate injury.

The associated posterolateral corner must be assessed separately, since the two
are not the same injury. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not seeing it, because it is small and at the edge of the
film. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

It is reported as a non-specific cortical irregularity. 5.20.8 covers common
reporting errors.

Its absence is taken to exclude anterolateral injury. 2.11.3 covers the
anterolateral ligament.

It is treated as a fracture needing fixation. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

The cruciate is not assessed after it is found. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate
injury.

### Key Learning Points

- It is missed because it is small and peripheral.
- It is reported as a non-specific cortical irregularity.
- Its absence is taken to exclude anterolateral injury.
- It is treated as a fracture needing fixation.
- The cruciate goes unassessed after it is found.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this fragment sits at the centre of an unsettled question
about the anterolateral complex.

The structure it avulses has been described in several ways and its existence as a
discrete ligament has been debated, so the fragment's anatomical origin is
better established than the structure's identity. 2.11.5 covers the anterolateral
complex.

Whether an extra-articular augmentation should be added to a cruciate
reconstruction is an active question, and this fragment is one of the inputs
rather than an answer. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

The fragment is visible on the injury radiograph and frequently not on later
films, so a patient presenting late has lost the finding, which argues for
keeping the original films. 5.7.5 covers chronic cruciate injury.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows the anterolateral structures directly and
inconsistently, which is why a bony marker retains value in a cross sectional
era. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

The position worth holding is that this is a well established radiographic sign
of a contested anatomical structure, and both halves of that should be said.
2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

### Key Learning Points

- The avulsed structure's identity is less settled than the fragment's origin.
- Extra-articular augmentation is an active question and this is one input.
- The fragment is often absent on later films, so the injury films matter.
- Cross sectional imaging shows the anterolateral structures inconsistently.
- A well established sign of a contested structure.
