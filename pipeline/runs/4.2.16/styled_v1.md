# Non-contact Injury

The commonest mechanism of serious knee injury, and the one where the
patient's account is most diagnostic.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A non-contact injury is one where nothing struck the knee. The patient
produced the forces that injured it.

Asking about this tests whether the injury was generated by the patient's own
movement, which points strongly towards the cruciate and the menisci rather
than towards the collaterals.

The majority of anterior cruciate ruptures happen this way, which is why this
page opens the injury history section. 3.7.14 covers the mechanisms.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether anything or anybody hit the knee. A patient will often describe a
tackle that happened nearby rather than to them.

Ask what the foot was doing: planted, turning, or landing.

Ask what the body was doing: decelerating, changing direction, or landing from
a jump.

Ask whether they heard or felt a pop, whether they fell, and whether they
could carry on.

Ask about swelling timing, which is the single most discriminating follow-up.
4.2.3 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

A planted foot with a sudden change of direction, a pop, immediate swelling
and inability to continue is an anterior cruciate rupture.

Landing from a jump with the knee collapsing inwards is the same injury by a
different route, and is the classic mechanism in female athletes. 3.7.15
covers it.

Deceleration with a twist and pain but the ability to continue, with slower
swelling, fits a meniscal tear.

Hyperextension during a kick or a missed step raises the cruciate and the
posterior structures. 4.2.19 covers hyperextension.

A patella that moved laterally during a twist on a flexed knee is a
dislocation event, which patients usually describe clearly. 4.2.22 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- A non-contact injury is one where the patient produced the forces themselves.
- Most anterior cruciate ruptures happen this way.
- Ask what the foot was doing and what the body was doing separately.
- Planted foot, change of direction, pop, immediate swelling, could not continue: a cruciate.
- Swelling timing is the most discriminating follow-up question.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning connects the mechanism to the loading, which Section 3
establishes.

A planted foot with internal rotation of the femur on the tibia loads the
anterior cruciate and the anterolateral structures together, which is why
isolated cruciate injuries are less common than the teaching implies.

Valgus collapse during landing adds the medial structures to the same event.
4.12.7 covers separating medial meniscus from collateral injury.

A deep squat or a forced flexion with rotation loads the posterior meniscal
attachments, which is a root tear mechanism. 4.9.9 covers root tears.

The combination that should always be sought is a cruciate injury with a
meniscal injury, because the mechanism produces both and the second is the one
whose repair has a window.

### How Good It Is

The non-contact mechanism is one of the better recalled parts of the knee
history, because it happened during a deliberate movement the patient was
concentrating on.

It performs well for identifying which structures were loaded and poorly for
grading what happened to them.

The pop is reasonably discriminating when present and absent in a substantial
proportion of genuine ruptures, so its absence excludes little.

The inability to continue is among the more reliable features, because it is a
behaviour rather than a sensation.

### Key Learning Points

- A planted, rotating knee loads the cruciate and the anterolateral structures together.
- Valgus collapse on landing adds the medial structures to the same event.
- Forced flexion with rotation is a meniscal root mechanism.
- Always seek the combination of cruciate and meniscal injury, because the mechanism produces both.
- Inability to continue is more reliable than the pop, because it is a behaviour.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the mechanism shapes what to look for and how urgently,
because the repairable injuries have windows.

A non-contact cruciate injury with a locked knee is a displaced meniscal tear
and is urgent, because a bucket handle tear in a young knee may be repairable
now and not later. 4.2.5 covers locking.

A non-contact injury with valgus collapse should prompt a deliberate search
for medial and rotational injury rather than a cruciate-only assessment.

The mechanism also predicts recurrence. A patient who injured the knee in a
movement central to their sport will meet that movement again, and the
rehabilitation has to address it. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting a non-contact label from the referral without
asking. Patients and clinicians both use contact loosely.

The second is treating a non-contact mechanism as a cruciate mechanism and
stopping there, missing the associated injuries that the same event produces.

The third is attributing a non-contact injury in an older patient to the same
pathology as in a young one. A degenerate structure fails under lower loads
and the history may sound trivial. 4.9.11 covers the degenerate version.

And a non-contact injury in a child needs the growth plate considered before
the ligament. 4.19.1 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Repairable injuries have windows, so the mechanism shapes urgency.
- A non-contact cruciate injury with a locked knee is urgent.
- Valgus collapse should prompt a deliberate search for medial and rotational injury.
- A degenerate structure fails under lower loads, so the history may sound trivial.
- In a child the growth plate comes before the ligament.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the non-contact mechanism matters most for what it says
about recurrence risk and about what the first operation did not address.

A graft that failed during the same movement that caused the original injury
raises rotational control and tunnel position, and the mechanism is the clue
that the rotational component was never addressed. 4.14.3 covers the pattern.

A graft that failed during a trivial movement raises biological failure,
fixation failure or an undiagnosed cause of abnormal loading such as
malalignment or a deficient meniscus. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

In a revision assessment the mechanism of the re-injury is usually recorded
poorly and is worth reconstructing carefully, because the two scenarios above
lead to different operations. 4.10.12 covers revision assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- The mechanism of a re-injury points at what the first operation did not address.
- Failure during the original movement raises rotational control and tunnel position.
- Failure during a trivial movement raises biology, fixation, alignment or meniscal deficiency.
- The two scenarios lead to different operations, so reconstruct the mechanism carefully.
