# History Versus Imaging

What the history tells you that no scan can, and the one question in the knee
where the history outperforms everything else available.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The history tests the mechanism and the course, and both are invisible on
imaging.

A scan taken today cannot say whether the knee swelled within an hour or over
two days, and that single fact separates a haemarthrosis from a reactive
effusion and changes the whole differential. 4.8.7 covers it.

It cannot say whether the knee gave way or whether the patient avoided loading
it, which are different complaints with different causes.

And it cannot say what the patient is unable to do, which is the only thing
that will eventually define success.

### What a Positive Means

Four historical features carry more weight than their reputation suggests.

Immediate swelling, within hours, points to bleeding into the joint and
therefore to a cruciate, an osteochondral fracture or a patellar dislocation.

A pop or a crack heard or felt at the moment of injury raises the likelihood
of a cruciate rupture substantially.

True locking, meaning the knee physically will not straighten and has to be
worked free, points to a mechanical block rather than to pain inhibition.
4.9.8 covers the distinction.

Giving way on a turn rather than on a step suggests rotational rather than
anterior instability. 4.14.3 covers the pattern.

### Key Learning Points

- The history carries the mechanism and the course, and imaging carries neither.
- Swelling within hours points to blood in the joint and therefore to a short differential.
- True locking means a mechanical block; pain that prevents movement is a different finding.
- Giving way on a turn rather than a step suggests rotational instability.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The history is the only part of the assessment that establishes a sequence,
and sequence is what distinguishes cause from coincidence.

A knee painful before an injury and more painful after it is a different
problem from one that was asymptomatic until a defined event.

A patient whose symptoms migrated from medial to anterior over months is
describing a compensation rather than a second diagnosis.

And a symptom that improved and then returned marks either a failure of
healing or a new event, and asking which is a history question with no imaging
equivalent.

### How Good It Is

For one question the history outperforms every other instrument available,
including imaging and examination: whether the knee is mechanically unstable
in use.

A patient's account of giving way during a specific activity is more
informative than any laxity measurement, because laxity and instability are
not the same thing and the patient is reporting the one that matters.

Many knees with substantial measured laxity are functionally stable, and some
with modest laxity are not. The examination measures the first and the history
reports the second.

For structural questions the ranking reverses. The history is poor at
localising a meniscal tear and imaging is good at it, which is the opposite of
the instability case.

### Key Learning Points

- Only the history establishes a sequence, and sequence separates cause from coincidence.
- For functional instability the history outperforms both examination and imaging.
- Laxity and instability are different: the examination measures one and the patient reports the other.
- For structural localisation the ranking reverses and imaging wins.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

The decision-relevant framing is that history, examination and imaging answer
three different questions and the mistake is asking one of them the wrong
question.

The history answers what the patient cannot do and how the problem behaves
over time. The examination answers what the joint does under defined load.
Imaging answers what the tissue looks like.

A reconstruction decision needs all three, and the one that most often tips it
is the first, because a knee that is not troubling the patient in use does not
need stabilising however it measures.

A degenerate knee is where this matters most. Imaging in that population finds
something in almost everybody, so the scan cannot select who should be treated
and the history has to. 4.9.11 covers the meniscal version of the problem.

### Where It Misleads

The history misleads in patterned ways and the patterns are worth recognising.

A rehearsed history misleads, and patients referred through several clinicians
arrive with one. The mechanism has been reshaped by each retelling and by each
explanation they were given.

A history taken after a scan is contaminated by it. A patient told they have a
torn meniscus describes meniscal symptoms, and the only reliable way to avoid
this is to take the history before reading the report.

Medicolegal and occupational context changes emphasis rather than
truthfulness, and pretending otherwise is naive rather than kind.

And a patient with low expectations under-reports. Someone who stopped running
three years ago will not mention that running is impossible unless asked
directly.

### Key Learning Points

- History, examination and imaging answer three different questions, and the error is asking one the wrong question.
- In degenerate knees imaging finds something in almost everybody, so the history has to select.
- A history taken after reading the scan report is contaminated by it.
- A patient who stopped an activity years ago will not report it unless asked directly.
- A rehearsed history has been reshaped by every previous explanation the patient was given.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

The hardest version of this problem is the patient whose history, examination
and imaging each support a different conclusion.

The temptation is to weight imaging, because it is the most concrete. The
pattern in referred practice is that this is wrong more often than it is
right.

A useful discipline is to decide, before reviewing the images, what the
history and examination together predict. If the images then disagree, that
disagreement is a finding rather than a correction.

Revision assessment is where the discipline pays most, because the previous
operation guarantees abnormal imaging and the only clean information comes
from what the patient now reports and what the knee now does. 4.10.12 covers
it.

The honest limit is that no part of this is measurable. The claim that history
outperforms imaging for instability is standard teaching and this page cites
nothing for it, which is the condition the whole site is in and is stated on
every page.

### Key Learning Points

- When all three disagree, weighting imaging because it is concrete is wrong more often than right.
- Predict from history and examination before reviewing images, so a disagreement is a finding rather than a correction.
- In revision assessment the previous operation guarantees abnormal imaging.
- None of this is measurable, and nothing on this page is cited.
