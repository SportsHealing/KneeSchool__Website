# Dynamic Alignment

The shape the limb takes under load, and the one finding in the alignment
assessment that cannot be obtained lying down.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Dynamic alignment is the limb's shape while it is being loaded, which differs
from its static shape because the collateral and corner structures slacken under
load. 3.13.7 covers the biomechanics.

A thrust is the visible version of that difference: the knee changes alignment
abruptly as weight is taken.

A thrust is the most consequential alignment finding in this section, because it
means a restraint is absent rather than stretched, and an absent restraint
changes the operation. 4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

### How It Is Performed

Watch the patient walk from the front, over a repeating distance, at knee level.
4.3.2 covers gait observation.

Look at the moment weight is taken on each leg and note whether the knee moves
sideways.

Note the direction. A knee moving laterally is a varus thrust; a knee moving
medially is a valgus thrust.

Compare the static standing alignment with what you see walking, because the
difference between the two is the finding. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Watch a single leg squat or a step down if the walking gait is equivocal, since
both increase the load on one limb. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing.

### What a Positive Means

A varus thrust indicates lateral ligament or corner deficiency, or medial
compartment collapse with secondary lateral slackening. 4.13.4 covers the
corner.

A valgus thrust indicates medial deficiency, or lateral compartment collapse
with secondary medial slackening. 4.12.2 covers the test it corresponds to.

A thrust with a normal static alignment means the restraint is absent and the
bone is intact, which points at a ligament problem. 4.13.1 covers varus stress
testing.

A thrust with a fixed deformity means both, and the bony problem usually leads
the treatment. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

A knee falling into valgus during a single leg squat is not a thrust but a
control problem, and the hip is where it is addressed. 4.4.5 covers that
pattern.

### Key Learning Points

- The limb's loaded shape differs from its static shape because the restraints slacken under load.
- A thrust means a restraint is absent rather than stretched.
- A varus thrust points at the lateral side; a valgus thrust at the medial.
- A thrust with a normal static alignment points at a ligament problem.
- Valgus collapse in a single leg squat is a control problem, not a thrust.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the thrust is the finding that converts an alignment discussion into a
surgical one, and looking for it without being prompted is the mark.

The expected points are the front view at knee level, the moment of weight
acceptance, the direction, the comparison with the static alignment, and a
loaded single leg task if needed.

Say that you would obtain long leg standing radiographs for any thrust, since
the static measurement is needed to interpret the dynamic finding. 4.5.1 covers
it.

Say that you would examine the corresponding ligaments, because a thrust names a
side and the ligament examination names the structure. 4.13.4 covers the
corner.

Say that you would distinguish a thrust from a control problem, since the two
look similar and are treated differently. 4.4.9 covers the task that separates
them.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a load applied to a knee with a deficient
collateral or corner structure produces an abrupt angular change, because
nothing resists it until a secondary restraint engages. 3.13.7 covers dynamic
alignment.

In a degenerative knee the same thing happens for a different reason: the
compartment collapses, the structures on that side slacken, and the knee
thrusts without any injury. 3.13.8 covers alignment and osteoarthritis.

That is why a thrust localises to a side rather than to a cause, and the static
alignment and the ligament examination together say which. 4.5.2 covers varus
alignment.

A thrust also loads any reconstruction on that side with every step, which is
the mechanism behind the stretched grafts this section repeatedly describes.
3.10.8 covers reconstruction biomechanics.

### How Good It Is

A thrust is the most specific finding in the alignment assessment and is visible
to anyone who looks from the front while the patient walks.

It is insensitive. A knee can have substantial laxity and no thrust, so its
absence excludes little. 4.13.5 covers the test that does not miss those.

It is better than any static measurement at predicting whether a reconstruction
will hold, which is its claim to importance.

It cannot be obtained lying down, which makes it the finding most often missing
from an otherwise complete examination. 4.3.5 covers the supine assessment.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- A load on a knee with a deficient restraint produces an abrupt angular change.
- A degenerative knee thrusts for a different reason: the compartment collapses and the side slackens.
- A thrust localises to a side rather than to a cause.
- It is specific and insensitive, so its absence excludes little.
- It cannot be obtained lying down, which is why it is so often missing.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a thrust decides whether the operation includes a correction,
and it does so more reliably than any static measurement.

A varus thrust in a knee being considered for a lateral or posterolateral
reconstruction moves the plan towards an osteotomy first or instead.
4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

A valgus thrust in a knee being considered for a medial reconstruction raises the
same question on the other side. 4.12.8 covers that assessment.

A thrust in a knee with a chronically deficient posterior cruciate indicates
corner involvement, and the corner determines whether anything done to the
cruciate survives. 4.11.7 covers that assessment.

Following a thrust after an operation is the most direct assessment of whether
the reconstruction is controlling the plane it was built for. 4.4.7 covers
post-operative gait.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not watching the patient walk.

The second is watching from the side, where a coronal thrust is invisible.

The third is reading a control problem as a thrust.

The fourth is treating a thrust as a ligament finding in a knee with a collapsed
compartment, where the bony problem leads. 4.4.6 covers the gait.

The fifth is recording a thrust without the static alignment, which leaves the
cause unresolved.

### Key Learning Points

- A thrust decides whether the operation includes a correction, more reliably than any static measurement.
- A varus thrust moves the plan towards an osteotomy first or instead.
- A thrust with a chronically deficient posterior cruciate indicates corner involvement.
- Following a thrust after an operation assesses whether the reconstruction controls its plane.
- Watching from the side makes a coronal thrust invisible.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the thrust is the finding that explains the failed
reconstruction, and it is usually the finding nobody looked for.

A patient with a stretched lateral or posterolateral reconstruction and a varus
thrust had a sequencing problem, and the revision is a correction rather than a
graft. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

A thrust after an osteotomy means an under correction or a ligament problem the
correction exposed, and the pre-operative films are needed to separate them.
4.5.1 covers the measurement.

An incompletely recovered common peroneal nerve palsy produces a gait
abnormality that resembles a thrust and is not ligamentous, and in a knee with
lateral surgery that distinction matters. 4.13.4 covers it.

In the chronically deficient knee the appearance of a thrust where there was
none is the clearest sign that the compartment has changed, and the gait sees it
before the imaging does. 4.11.6 covers that progression.

In medicolegal work a recorded thrust is strong objective evidence, and a
recorded normal gait with a claim of instability is a difficulty the examination
has created honestly. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The thrust explains the failed reconstruction and is usually the finding nobody looked for.
- A stretched lateral reconstruction with a varus thrust needs a correction, not a graft.
- A thrust after an osteotomy is under correction or a ligament problem the correction exposed.
- An unrecovered nerve palsy resembles a thrust and is not ligamentous.
- A new thrust in a deficient knee is the clearest sign the compartment has changed.
