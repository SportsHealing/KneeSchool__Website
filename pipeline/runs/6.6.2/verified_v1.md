# Hormonal Factors

The most studied explanation in the chapter and the least usable one, which is a
combination worth understanding on its own.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

Hormones are chemical messengers made by the body, and the ones in the monthly
cycle change the properties of soft tissues a little. 6.6.1 covers the
epidemiology.

Some research suggests the risk of this injury changes slightly across the
cycle. Nobody can currently use that to decide anything for one person. 6.6.3
covers neuromuscular factors.

### Causes and Risk Factors

The honest summary is that this is being investigated rather than known, and
that it is unwise to change what somebody does on the strength of it. 6.6.4
covers prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- Hormones are chemical messengers made by the body.
- The ones in the monthly cycle change soft tissue properties a little.
- Some research links injury risk to the cycle.
- Nobody can use it to decide anything for one person yet.
- Investigated is a different word from known.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

The hormonal explanation proposes that the changes in oestrogen and progesterone
across the menstrual cycle alter ligament properties and neuromuscular control
enough to change injury risk. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

It is plausible, it has been studied extensively, and it has not produced
anything a clinician can act on for an individual athlete. 6.6.3 covers
neuromuscular factors.

### Causes and Risk Factors

The research difficulty is practical. Knowing which phase of the cycle an
athlete was in at the moment of injury needs either hormone assays or accurate
diary data, and most studies have neither. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of
injury.

Hormonal contraception adds a second variable that is common, varied, and
frequently unrecorded. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### Management Overview

Nothing on this page should change training, team selection or treatment, and
saying that plainly is the page's main service. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Where it does belong is in the history, because a complete account of a young
athlete includes cycle regularity, contraception and the energy availability
questions that sit alongside them. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Those questions matter for bone health and for performance whether or not they
turn out to matter for the ACL. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- The proposal is that cycle hormones alter tissue and control.
- It is plausible, heavily studied and not yet usable.
- Phase at the moment of injury is hard to establish.
- Hormonal contraception is a common unrecorded variable.
- The questions belong in the history for other reasons anyway.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Should I avoid matches at a particular point in my cycle?
There is no basis for that advice at present. The research does not support
scheduling decisions for an individual, and this page does not make a
recommendation.

**Q.** Does the contraceptive pill protect the ligament?
It has been studied and the answer is not established. It is not a reason to
start or stop a contraceptive, and that decision belongs with the clinician who
prescribes it.

**Q.** Why does so much research exist if none of it is usable?
Because the question is easy to ask and hard to answer. Measuring cycle phase at
the moment of an injury that happens in a fraction of a second is the obstacle,
and it has not been solved.

**Q.** Is there anything in this area I should act on?
Yes, although it is not about the ligament. Irregular or absent periods in an
athlete are worth raising with a doctor, because of what they say about energy
availability and bone health.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

The hypothesis has two arms: a direct effect of sex hormones on collagen
structure and ligament mechanical properties, and an indirect effect on
neuromuscular control. 3.7.15 covers female ACL biomechanics.

Receptors for oestrogen have been identified in ligament tissue, which
establishes plausibility and not effect size. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Causes and Risk Factors

Three methodological problems account for most of the inconsistency in this
literature. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

The first is phase determination: self-reported cycle day, calendar counting and
hormone assay are not interchangeable, and studies using the weakest method
report the largest effects. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

The second is the cycle itself, which is irregular in exactly the athletic
population being studied, often because of training load and energy
availability. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The third is hormonal contraception, which suppresses the variation being
measured and is common and heterogeneous. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Management Overview

The clinically defensible position is that cycle phase does not currently
inform any decision about an individual athlete. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The adjacent questions do inform decisions: menstrual irregularity, low energy
availability and bone health belong in the assessment of any young athlete.
6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- The hypothesis has a direct tissue arm and an indirect control arm.
- Receptor presence establishes plausibility rather than effect size.
- Phase determination methods are not interchangeable.
- Cycle irregularity is common in the population being studied.
- Contraception suppresses the variation under measurement.

## MRCS Level

### Causes and Risk Factors

In a viva the expected answer states the hypothesis, names the methodological
problem, and declines to convert it into advice.

Say that oestrogen receptors in ligament establish plausibility, that effect
size is unresolved, and that phase determination is the limiting step. 3.7.15
covers female ACL biomechanics.

Say that the literature is inconsistent and that the inconsistency is explained
by method rather than by biology. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

### Management Overview

The answer that scores is that this changes nothing for the individual in front
of you, and that the neuromuscular factors do. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular
factors.

Offer the adjacent gain: the history questions this topic prompts are worth
asking for bone health and energy availability. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- State the hypothesis, the problem, and no advice.
- Receptors give plausibility and not effect size.
- Phase determination is the limiting step.
- The inconsistency is methodological rather than biological.
- The neuromuscular factors are where the usable content is.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is asked whether cycle phase should influence
the timing of reconstruction or of return to sport.

The answer is no, and the reasoning is what is being tested: the effect size is
unresolved, phase cannot be established reliably in practice, and a decision
built on it would be less defensible than the one it replaced. 6.6.1 covers the
epidemiology.

A candidate who says yes has to explain how the phase would be determined, which
is where the position fails. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Complications and Prognosis

The cost of overstating this is specific. An athlete told her cycle drives her
risk has been given a factor she cannot modify in place of one she can. 6.6.4
covers prevention.

That substitution reduces adherence to the training that has evidence behind it,
which makes the overstatement actively harmful rather than merely unsupported.
6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether a genuine phase effect exists at a size worth
measuring once phase is established by assay. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

The second is whether hormonal contraception alters risk, which is answerable
and under-answered. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The third is whether relative energy deficiency in sport, rather than cycle phase
itself, carries the association that has been attributed to hormones. 6.6.3
covers neuromuscular factors.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Cycle phase should not influence timing, and the reasoning is the answer.
- A candidate saying yes must explain how phase would be determined.
- Overstating this substitutes a fixed factor for a modifiable one.
- That substitution reduces adherence to training with evidence.
- Energy deficiency may carry the association attributed to hormones.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the practical issue is that athletes arrive having already
been given a hormonal explanation, usually by somebody with more contact time
than the surgeon has.

Correcting it costs consultation time and credibility with the coach, and
leaving it uncorrected costs adherence to the training that works. 6.6.4 covers
prevention.

The habit worth keeping is to ask what she has been told about why this
happened, before giving any explanation of your own. The answer shapes
everything afterwards and takes one question. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

The second habit is to record menstrual and energy availability history
properly, because it is clinically useful for reasons unrelated to this argument
and nobody else in the pathway is doing it. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

### Key Learning Points

- Athletes arrive with an explanation already supplied.
- Correcting it costs time and credibility with the coach.
- Leaving it costs adherence to training that works.
- Ask what she has been told before explaining anything.
- Record menstrual and energy availability history for its own sake.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that this is a subject where the honest answer
sounds evasive, and where a confident wrong answer is socially rewarded. 6.6.1
covers the epidemiology.

Saying that a widely discussed factor cannot currently guide her decisions reads
as a failure to engage, and it is the accurate position. The useful move is to
pair it with the factor that can guide them rather than to leave a gap. 6.6.4
covers prevention.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which here includes
menstrual and skeletal status. Their medical comorbidities, including low energy
availability, which belongs in this conversation on its own merits. The demands
they put on the knee, set by a season rather than by her. Their psychological
state, because an explanation that locates the cause in her body is heard
differently from one that locates it in her training. And their own ideas,
concerns and expectations, which commonly already contain a hormonal
explanation.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and replace the explanation
she arrived with rather than merely denying it. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular
factors.

### Key Learning Points

- The honest answer sounds evasive and a confident wrong answer is rewarded.
- Pair the declined factor with the one that can guide decisions.
- Low energy availability belongs in the conversation on its own merits.
- A cause located in her body is heard differently from one in her training.
- Replace the explanation she arrived with rather than denying it.
