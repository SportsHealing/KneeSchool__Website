# Root Attachments

The lateral meniscal roots: close together, crowded by the cruciates, and the
anterior one sitting where a cruciate tunnel wants to go.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The lateral meniscus has an anterior and a posterior root, and they attach
much closer together than the medial ones do. That is what makes the ring
nearly closed.

The anterior root attaches in the anterior intercondylar area, immediately
adjacent to and often overlapping the tibial footprint of the anterior
cruciate ligament.

The posterior root attaches in the posterior intercondylar area, in front of
the posterior cruciate's tibial attachment and lateral to the posterior root
of the medial meniscus.

Both insert into bone through a transitional zone, as described on 2.5.7.

### Relations

Anterior root: the anterior cruciate footprint behind and medially, with
overlap, and the transverse intermeniscal ligament continuing medially.

Posterior root: the medial meniscal posterior root medially, the posterior
cruciate attachment behind, and the origins of the meniscofemoral ligaments
immediately adjacent on the horn itself.

The tibial spine sits between the anterior and posterior intercondylar areas.

### Function

The roots anchor the circumferential collagen and transmit hoop tension into
bone, exactly as on the medial side.

Because the lateral roots are close together, the ring is shorter and the
geometry differs, but the mechanism is identical: lose a root and the hoop
cannot be tensioned.

The posterior horn also has the fascicles and the meniscofemoral ligaments
holding it, so a lateral posterior root tear is partly compensated in a way a
medial one is not.

### Clinical Relevance

The anterior root's overlap with the anterior cruciate footprint is the single
most practical fact here. Drilling a cruciate tunnel can detach part of it.

A lateral posterior root tear is strongly associated with anterior cruciate
injury, because the mechanism that tears one tears the other.

Pathology belongs to 6.34 and repair to 7.14.

### Key Learning Points

- The lateral roots attach close together, which is why the ring is nearly closed.
- The anterior root overlaps the anterior cruciate tibial footprint.
- The posterior root sits in front of the posterior cruciate and lateral to the medial root.
- The extra tethers on the lateral posterior horn partly compensate for a root tear, unlike the medial side.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Place the posterior root by its neighbours: anterior to the posterior cruciate
attachment, lateral to the medial meniscal posterior root, and immediately
medial to the lateral articular margin. The meniscofemoral ligaments arise
from the horn just lateral and superior to the root itself.

Place the anterior root by its neighbours: lateral and anterior to the
anterior cruciate footprint, with overlap of fibres between the two. In many
knees the root fibres and the cruciate fibres cannot be separated at their
bony insertion.

Both are entheses with the four zone structure described on 2.5.2, so both
avulse at bone rather than tearing in substance.

### Relations

The posterior intercondylar area holds, from medial to lateral: the medial
meniscal posterior root, the lateral meniscal posterior root, and the
posterior cruciate descending onto the posterior tibial surface behind both.
The posterior capsule and the popliteal artery lie behind all of it.

The anterior intercondylar area holds, from medial to lateral: the medial
meniscal anterior root, the anterior cruciate footprint, and the lateral
meniscal anterior root, with the transverse intermeniscal ligament crossing in
front.

Both roots have a bony blood supply in addition to the perimeniscal plexus.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

As on the medial side, the roots are among the better vascularised meniscal
tissue, supplied through the bony attachment and from the perimeniscal plexus.

The middle genicular artery supplies the posterior intercondylar region, so
the posterior root shares a supply with the cruciates and the meniscofemoral
ligaments.

Mechanoreceptors are concentrated at both roots.

### Function

Hoop tension transmission, as on 2.5.4. The lateral ring is shorter and the
roots are closer, so root position has a proportionally larger effect on the
direction the hoop tension acts in.

A lateral posterior root tear allows posterolateral extrusion of the posterior
horn, which increases anterior translation of the lateral tibia. That is why
these tears matter in the cruciate deficient or reconstructed knee.

The meniscofemoral ligaments and the fascicles hold the horn even when the
root has failed, which is why extrusion may be less obvious here than on the
medial side.

### Clinical Relevance

Inspect the lateral posterior root at every anterior cruciate reconstruction.
The association is strong enough that not looking is a choice to miss some of
them.

Protect the anterior root during tibial tunnel drilling. The footprints
overlap, so respecting the cruciate footprint does not automatically protect
the root.

A lateral posterior root tear in a cruciate deficient knee increases
rotational instability, and repairing it is part of restoring the knee rather
than an optional extra. 7.14 owns the technique.

Extrusion is a less reliable sign of root failure laterally, because the extra
tethers hold the horn. Direct inspection is more reliable than imaging here.

### Key Learning Points

- Posterior intercondylar area, medial to lateral: medial root, lateral root, then the posterior cruciate behind both.
- Anterior root fibres and anterior cruciate fibres often cannot be separated at the bone.
- The shorter lateral ring means root position has a proportionally larger effect on hoop direction.
- A lateral posterior root tear increases anterior translation of the lateral tibia.
- Extrusion is a less reliable sign laterally, because the extra tethers hold the horn.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The anterior intercondylar area is the operative conflict. The cruciate
footprint and the lateral anterior root share it, and a tunnel of any useful
diameter placed on the footprint will take some root fibres.

That is a known cost rather than an avoidable error, and the question is how
much and whether it is recognised.

### Function

A lateral posterior root tear changes rotational behaviour, not just load
distribution. In a knee undergoing cruciate reconstruction, leaving it
unrepaired leaves a rotational deficit the graft has to absorb.

That is the anatomical argument for routine inspection and for repair when
found, and it is stronger than the equivalent argument on the medial side.

### Clinical Relevance

Make lateral posterior root inspection part of every cruciate reconstruction,
before the graft is passed while access is still good.

Where the anterior root has been partly detached by a tunnel, recognise and
repair it at the time. It cannot be addressed later without revisiting the
tunnel.

In combined injury, plan the order so that root fixation is not compromised by
the tunnel and the tunnel is not compromised by the fixation.

Every published footprint description for these roots is quantitative, given
as distances from the cruciate footprints and the articular margins. None
appears here. Decision 006 records why, and this page and 2.5.7 are where the
absence costs most.

### Key Learning Points

- A tunnel of useful diameter on the cruciate footprint will take some lateral anterior root fibres.
- A lateral posterior root tear leaves a rotational deficit the graft has to absorb.
- Inspect the lateral posterior root before the graft is passed, while access is good.
- A tunnel damaged anterior root must be repaired at the time or not at all.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

Three decisions where the lateral roots are the deciding anatomy.

The cruciate reconstruction with an unrecognised lateral posterior root tear.
This is the commonest way a technically sound reconstruction ends up with
residual rotational laxity. The root is inspectable, the association with
cruciate injury is strong, and the only reason it is missed is that nobody
looked.

Tunnel versus root at the anterior intercondylar area. Where a large tibial
tunnel is planned, some anterior root detachment is accepted. Recognising it
and repairing it at the time is the difference between a managed cost and a
later problem. In revision, where the previous tunnel already occupies the
area, the root may be gone and the reconstruction has to proceed knowing that.

Lateral meniscal allograft after meniscectomy. Both horns have to be fixed in
a region shared with both cruciates, and in a knee that has also had a
cruciate reconstruction the space is already occupied. The sequencing of those
operations is anatomical.

In all three the roots have not moved. What changes is how much of the
intercondylar area is still available.

### Key Learning Points

- An unrecognised lateral posterior root tear is the commonest reason a sound reconstruction has residual rotational laxity.
- Anterior root detachment by a tunnel is a managed cost if recognised and a later problem if not.
- In revision the previous tunnel may already have taken the anterior root.
- Lateral allograft horn fixation competes for a space the cruciates already occupy.
