# Gait Observation

The thirty seconds of the examination that cost nothing and are left out of
almost every clinic letter.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Gait observation tests how the knee behaves under the load it actually carries,
repeated at a cadence no manual test reproduces.

It is a screen rather than a diagnosis. An abnormal gait says the limb is
compensating; which structure it is compensating for comes from the rest of the
examination. 4.4.1 covers normal gait in detail.

The chapter on gait examination covers the named patterns. This page covers the
observation itself: where to stand, what to look at, and in what order.
4.4.2 covers the first of those patterns.

### How It Is Performed

Expose the whole limb from the hip down, including both knees, because a gait
observed through trousers is a guess.

Give the patient a distance to walk in a straight line, towards you and away
from you, long enough for a pattern to repeat.

Watch from the front first, looking at the knee's alignment in stance and
whether it moves sideways as it takes weight. 4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

Watch from the side next, looking at whether the knee reaches full extension at
heel strike and whether it flexes through stance.

Watch from behind last, looking at the heel and the hindfoot, because a foot
that pronates changes the knee's loading. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

Ask the patient to turn at each end, and watch the turn rather than waiting for
the walk back.

### What a Positive Means

A shortened stance time on one side is antalgic and points at pain. 4.4.2
covers it.

A knee that moves laterally as it takes weight is a varus thrust, which points
at lateral ligament deficiency or at medial compartment collapse. 4.13.4 covers
the corner.

A knee that moves medially as it takes weight is a valgus thrust, which points
at the medial side or at a lateral compartment problem. 4.5.3 covers valgus
alignment.

A knee held in slight flexion throughout stance suggests a flexion deformity,
an effusion, or a quadriceps that cannot hold extension. 4.7.8 covers extension
lag.

A knee that extends fully and conspicuously early in stance, with no flexion
wave, is quadriceps avoidance, and it is the gait of a cruciate deficient knee.
4.4.3 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Expose the whole limb: a gait observed through trousers is a guess.
- Watch from the front, then the side, then behind, and watch the turn.
- A varus thrust points at lateral deficiency or medial compartment collapse.
- A knee held in flexion through stance suggests deformity, effusion or quadriceps weakness.
- Full early extension with no flexion wave is quadriceps avoidance.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the examiner is testing whether you have a system, and front, side,
behind, turn is the system.

The expected points are the exposure, the distance, the three viewpoints, the
turn, and the comparison between sides.

Say that you would look at the hip and the ankle as well, because a thrust can
originate above or below the knee and the gait cannot tell you which without
examining both. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Say that you would note the cadence and the step length, since both change
before an obvious limp appears.

Say that you would record what you saw rather than a diagnosis, because the
named patterns overlap and the raw observation survives better in a letter.
4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the knee's stance phase has a flexion wave which
absorbs load, and that most abnormal gaits are modifications of that wave.
3.14.2 covers the stance phase.

A knee that cannot tolerate the flexion wave, because of pain or quadriceps
weakness, extends through stance instead, which is the mechanism behind
quadriceps avoidance. 3.14.9 covers the cruciate deficient gait.

A thrust is a sudden change in alignment under load, which means a restraint is
absent rather than merely stretched, and that distinction is what makes a thrust
more significant than a measured laxity. 3.13.7 covers dynamic alignment.

An antalgic pattern shortens the time spent loading the painful side, which is
why it is the most common abnormality and the least specific. 3.14.1 covers
normal gait.

### How Good It Is

Gait observation is the most informative thirty seconds of the knee examination
and the least often recorded, and both halves of that are worth stating.

It is sensitive and not specific. Nearly every significant knee problem changes
the gait, and the change rarely names the problem.

It is more reproducible than most people expect for the gross patterns, a
thrust and an antalgic limp among them, and considerably less reproducible for
the subtle ones.

Instrumented gait analysis measures what this estimates, and it is a research
tool rather than a clinic one in most practice. 3.14.1 covers the underlying
analysis.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Most abnormal gaits are modifications of the stance phase flexion wave.
- A knee that cannot tolerate that wave extends through stance instead.
- A thrust means a restraint is absent rather than merely stretched.
- An antalgic pattern is the most common abnormality and the least specific.
- Gross patterns are reproducible; subtle ones are not.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level gait observation changes two decisions, and both of them are
about whether an operation will hold.

A varus thrust in a knee being considered for a lateral ligament reconstruction
means the limb is loading the reconstruction with every step, and the decision
moves towards correcting the alignment first. 4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

A valgus thrust in a knee being considered for a medial reconstruction raises the
same question on the other side. 4.12.8 covers that assessment.

Quadriceps avoidance in a cruciate deficient knee is the gait that predicts a
functional complaint, and in a patient with a grade three Lachman and no
complaint its absence is part of why there is no complaint. 4.10.3 covers the
chronic knee.

Post-operative gait is also where a technically successful operation shows
itself as unsuccessful, and following it is more informative than following the
laxity. 4.4.7 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not observing it.

The second is observing it through clothing.

The third is attributing a thrust to the knee without examining the hip and the
ankle.

The fourth is reading an antalgic limp as a diagnosis rather than as evidence of
pain, which it is and nothing more.

The fifth is recording a named pattern that the observation does not support,
since the named patterns are idealisations and most real gaits are mixtures.
4.4.6 covers one of those idealisations.

### Key Learning Points

- A varus thrust moves the decision towards correcting alignment before a lateral reconstruction.
- Quadriceps avoidance is the gait that predicts a functional complaint in a cruciate deficient knee.
- Post-operative gait shows a technically successful operation failing before the laxity does.
- An antalgic limp is evidence of pain and nothing more.
- The named patterns are idealisations and most real gaits are mixtures.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the gait is where the question behind the referral is
usually answered, and it is usually the part nobody has recorded.

A patient with a stretched posterior cruciate graft and a varus thrust has the
mechanical explanation for the stretch, and no laxity measurement supplies it.
4.11.2 covers the drawer.

A patient whose gait has deteriorated while the measurements have not is
developing compartment change rather than ligament failure, and the gait sees it
first. 4.11.6 covers that progression.

A patient with a thrust after an osteotomy has either an under corrected
alignment or a ligament problem the correction exposed, and separating them
needs the standing radiographs alongside the gait. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical
axis.

An incompletely recovered common peroneal nerve palsy produces a gait
abnormality that is permanent and is not ligamentous, and it is routinely
attributed to laxity in a knee with lateral surgery. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

In an athlete the clinic gait is normal and uninformative, so the observation
has to be moved onto a task that approaches the demand that fails. 4.4.9 covers
single leg landing.

### Key Learning Points

- A stretched graft with a varus thrust has its mechanical explanation, which no laxity measurement supplies.
- A gait that deteriorates while measurements do not is compartment change seen early.
- A thrust after an osteotomy is under correction or a ligament problem the correction exposed.
- An unrecovered nerve palsy gives a permanent gait abnormality that is not ligamentous.
- In an athlete the clinic gait is uninformative and the task has to be raised.
