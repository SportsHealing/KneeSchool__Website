# Bundle Function

The ligament is not one rope. Two functional bundles tighten at different
angles, which is how one structure covers the whole range.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A single rope between two points is tight at one length and slack at others. A
joint that moves through a large range cannot be controlled by one length of
rope.

Splitting the rope into parts that run slightly differently solves it. As the
joint moves, one part tightens while another slackens.

That is reciprocal tightening, and it is how a ligament stays effective across
a range.

### At the Knee

The anterior cruciate is conventionally described as two functional bundles,
named for their tibial attachments: anteromedial and posterolateral. 2.7.8 and
2.7.9 cover them.

They are not separate ligaments. They are regions of one structure whose
fibres run differently enough to behave differently.

Conventional teaching has the anteromedial bundle tightening in flexion and
the posterolateral bundle tightening near extension. 3.7.5 and 3.7.6 cover
each.

The femoral attachment is what makes this work. The two bundles' femoral
origins sit in different places, so flexing the knee changes their relative
lengths.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which is what the whole arrangement responds to.

Rotation, which stretches the bundles unequally.

Partial injury, which can involve one bundle more than the other.

Tunnel position in a reconstruction, which determines how a graft behaves
through range.

### Clinical Relevance

A partial tear can leave a knee tight at one flexion angle and lax at another,
which is why testing at more than one angle matters. Section 4 covers the
tests.

A graft placed in one position cannot reproduce both bundles exactly, which is
the mechanical basis of the single and double bundle discussion. 3.7.20 covers
the construct.

Tunnel position matters because it sets how the graft's length changes through
the range.

Describing the ligament as one rope makes reconstruction look simpler than it
is.

### Key Learning Points

- A single rope is tight at one length and slack at others.
- Two functional bundles tighten reciprocally through the range.
- They are regions of one structure, not separate ligaments.
- Conventional teaching: anteromedial tightens in flexion, posterolateral near extension.
- The separated femoral origins are what make the arrangement work.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The useful concept is isometry. A fibre is isometric if its length does not
change through the range, and no fibre in the anterior cruciate is fully
isometric.

Fibres therefore have a length change pattern, and the bundle names group
fibres with similar patterns. The grouping is a simplification of a continuum.

That is why conventional teaching describes the two bundle model as
functionally useful and anatomically approximate.

### At the Knee

The femoral footprint is the critical geometry. Fibres originating more
proximally and posteriorly in the notch behave differently from those more
distally and anteriorly. 2.7.6 covers the footprint.

Conventional teaching gives the anteromedial bundle maximal tension at around
60 to 90 degrees of flexion and the posterolateral bundle maximal tension near
full extension.

In rotation the bundles load unequally, with the posterolateral bundle
contributing more to rotational restraint near extension. 3.7.7 covers
rotation.

The reciprocal behaviour means the whole ligament is never uniformly tense, so
a single tension figure for the ligament is not meaningful.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, as the primary variable.

Rotation and coronal load, which distribute between bundles unequally.

Partial rupture, which commonly involves one bundle region predominantly.

Femoral tunnel position in a graft, which is the single largest determinant of
length change behaviour.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric fibre length measurement through the range, with markers or with
imaging, maps the length change patterns.

In situ force measurement per bundle after selective sectioning apportions the
load.

Magnetic resonance imaging at multiple flexion angles describes the geometry
without load.

The two bundle description predates the measurements and has been refined
rather than replaced by them.

### Clinical Relevance

Treat the two bundle model as functionally useful and anatomically
approximate.

Expect a partial tear to produce angle specific findings.

Place a femoral tunnel knowing it is the main determinant of length change
behaviour.

Avoid quoting a single tension figure for the whole ligament.

### Key Learning Points

- No fibre in the anterior cruciate is fully isometric.
- The bundle names group fibres with similar length change patterns.
- The two bundle model is functionally useful and anatomically approximate.
- Conventional teaching: anteromedial peaks at 60 to 90 degrees, posterolateral near extension.
- Femoral tunnel position is the largest determinant of a graft's length change behaviour.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the bundle model as the mechanical content of the tunnel position
debate. The question is which length change pattern a graft should reproduce,
and a single graft can reproduce one.

Conventional teaching holds that double bundle reconstruction restores
rotational control measurably better in cadaveric testing and has not shown a
consistent clinical advantage, which is a real tension between two levels of
evidence.

Anatomic single bundle reconstruction is the common resolution: place the
graft in the native footprint centre so its behaviour approximates the whole
ligament rather than either bundle. 3.7.20 covers the construct.

### What Changes It

Femoral tunnel position within the footprint, which sets the length change
pattern.

Graft diameter, which determines how much of the footprint is spanned.

Tensioning angle, which sets where the graft is tightest.

Notch morphology, which constrains where a tunnel can go.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares single and double bundle constructs against the
intact knee in all six degrees of freedom.

Randomised clinical trials compare them on laxity and outcome, and have not
shown a consistent difference.

Tunnel position is measured postoperatively on computed tomography against
footprint maps.

The gap between the cadaveric and the clinical result is the honest state of
this question.

### Clinical Relevance

Place the graft in the native footprint centre rather than aiming at a bundle.

State the gap between cadaveric and clinical evidence when the double bundle
question arises.

Choose graft diameter with footprint coverage in mind.

Verify tunnel position on imaging when a reconstruction underperforms.

### Key Learning Points

- The bundle model is the mechanical content of the tunnel position debate.
- Double bundle restores rotation better in cadavers without a consistent clinical advantage.
- Anatomic single bundle in the footprint centre is the common resolution.
- Graft diameter determines how much of the footprint is spanned.
- The cadaveric and clinical results disagree, which is the honest position.
