# Patellar Tracking

The patella is a sesamoid that slides in a groove. Where it sits depends on
the flexion angle, and its path is neither straight nor symmetrical.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A bone sitting inside a tendon changes the direction the tendon pulls, in the
way a pulley changes the direction of a rope.

A groove cut into the bone underneath keeps that pulley in line. The deeper
the groove, the less work the surrounding soft tissue has to do.

A pulley that is only held by soft tissue is secure in one direction and
vulnerable in the other, which is the whole problem of this joint.

### At the Knee

The patella sits in the extensor mechanism, between the quadriceps tendon
above and the patellar tendon below. 2.4.1 covers the anatomy.

Near full extension it rests above the groove rather than in it, held by soft
tissue alone. 2.12.1 covers the trochlea.

It enters the groove as the knee bends, at around 20 to 30 degrees, and
becomes progressively more constrained with further flexion.

Through the arc it moves distally and tilts, and its contact with the femur
shifts steadily up the back of the patella. 3.12.2 covers contact areas.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which decides how much of the groove is holding the patella.

Quadriceps pull, and the balance between its medial and lateral parts. 2.12.5
covers the dynamic stabilisers.

Trochlear shape, which is what the groove actually offers. 3.12.4 covers
trochlear function.

Patellar height, because a patella sitting high enters the groove later.
3.12.12 covers tubercle position.

### Clinical Relevance

Dislocations happen near extension, because that is where the groove is not
yet holding the patella.

The soft tissue restraint that matters near extension is the medial
patellofemoral ligament. 3.12.5 covers it.

Strengthening the quadriceps changes tracking only where the groove is not
already doing the work.

Tracking is assessed by watching the knee bend rather than by a static
measurement. 4.15.1 covers the examination.

### Key Learning Points

- The patella is a sesamoid acting as a pulley for the extensor mechanism.
- Near extension it sits above the groove and is held by soft tissue alone.
- It engages the groove at around 20 to 30 degrees of flexion.
- Constraint increases with flexion, which is why dislocation happens near extension.
- Trochlear shape, quadriceps balance and patellar height all change the path.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Constraint in this joint is handed over. Soft tissue holds the patella near
extension and bone holds it in flexion, and the handover happens over a narrow
arc.

A joint with a handover has a window of vulnerability, and the window is where
the clinical problems sit.

Describing the path is therefore less useful than describing when each
restraint is in charge.

### At the Knee

From full extension the patella translates distally and medially as it
engages, then follows the groove laterally and posteriorly through mid
flexion.

Tilt and rotation accompany the translation. The patella is not a flat plate
sliding on a flat surface. 2.4.1 covers its facets.

By 60 to 90 degrees the patella is deep in the groove and bone is the dominant
restraint. Soft tissue contributes little there.

In deep flexion contact moves onto the odd facet and the proximal patella, and
the quadriceps tendon itself begins to articulate. 3.12.2 covers that.

### What Changes It

The depth and the lateral facet height of the trochlea, which decide when bone
takes over. 3.12.4 covers dysplasia.

Patellar height, which shifts the whole handover later in the arc. 3.12.12
covers it.

Tubercle position in the transverse plane, which changes the direction of the
distal pull. 3.12.12 covers it.

Femoral and tibial rotation, which move the groove relative to the line of
pull. 3.12.11 covers malalignment.

Medial soft tissue integrity, which decides what happens inside the window.
3.12.5 covers the ligament.

### How It Is Measured

Axial radiographs at fixed flexion angles give congruence and tilt, and they
say nothing about the arc either side. 5.12.3 covers tilt.

Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging measure the same indices
with better bone and soft tissue detail. 5.12 covers the imaging.

Dynamic imaging through the engagement arc is the measurement that matches the
mechanics, and it is not routine.

Clinical tracking assessment is observational and poorly reproducible between
observers. 4.15.1 covers it.

### Clinical Relevance

Describe tracking by the arc in which it goes wrong rather than as a single
path.

Expect instability to present near extension and pain to present in flexion,
because the restraints differ there.

Assess the extensor mechanism through movement, since a static axial view
misses the engagement arc entirely.

Treat a poorly reproducible clinical sign as a prompt for imaging rather than
as a measurement. 4.15.1 covers the tests.

### Key Learning Points

- Constraint is handed over from soft tissue to bone over a narrow arc.
- The handover window is where the clinical problems sit.
- Tilt and rotation accompany translation; the patella is not a flat plate.
- By 60 to 90 degrees bone is the dominant restraint.
- A static axial view misses the engagement arc, which is where tracking fails.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The engagement arc is the mechanically interesting part of patellar tracking,
and almost every index in use measures outside it.

Before engagement the restraint to lateral translation is the medial
retinacular complex, of which the medial patellofemoral ligament is the
principal band. 2.12.2 covers it.

After engagement the restraining structure is the lateral trochlear facet, and
the force needed to displace the patella rises steeply with flexion.

The J sign is this handover failing visibly. The patella sits lateral in
extension and snaps medially as it drops into the groove. 4.15.1 covers the
sign.

A patella that engages late, from height or from a shallow groove, lengthens
the window in which only soft tissue is working. 3.12.12 covers height.

### What Changes It

Trochlear morphology, which is the single largest determinant of when the
handover happens. 3.12.4 covers it.

Patellar height, which delays engagement without changing the groove. 3.12.12
covers it.

Transverse tubercle position, which sets the lateral vector through the arc.

Femoral anteversion and tibial torsion, which rotate the groove under the
extensor mechanism. 3.12.11 covers them.

Quadriceps activation pattern and timing, which matter most in the unloaded
part of the arc. 3.12.7 covers dynamic valgus.

### How It Is Measured

Static indices measured at 30 degrees or more sit after engagement in most
knees and therefore miss the window.

Dynamic computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging through extension
to flexion capture the arc, at a dose or a time cost. 5.12.4 covers
subluxation.

Instrumented displacement testing gives a force to displacement curve in
cadavers and has no clinical equivalent. 3.16.1 covers cadaveric testing.

No index in routine use reports the flexion angle at which the patella
engages, which is the number the mechanics would ask for.

### Clinical Relevance

Identify which part of the arc fails before choosing between a soft tissue and
a bony procedure. 3.12.8 covers the mechanics.

Read a J sign as late or lateral engagement rather than as a quadriceps
problem.

Prefer a dynamic study where the static indices and the history disagree. 5.12
covers the imaging.

State that the flexion angle of engagement is not routinely measured, rather
than implying an index captures it.

Expect a soft tissue reconstruction to work inside the window and to do little
outside it. 7.51 covers the operation.

### Key Learning Points

- The engagement arc is where the mechanics matter and where most indices do not look.
- Before engagement the medial soft tissue restrains; after it, the lateral trochlear facet.
- The J sign is the handover failing visibly.
- Late engagement, from height or a shallow groove, lengthens the soft tissue window.
- No routine index reports the flexion angle at which the patella engages.
