# MQTFL

The part of the medial restraint that attaches to tendon rather than bone, and
the reason two names describe one sheet.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The medial quadriceps tendon femoral ligament runs from the same femoral area
as the medial patellofemoral ligament to the deep surface of the quadriceps
tendon, just above the patella.

It is the part of the medial restraint that attaches to tendon instead of to
the patella itself.

Conventional teaching places its attachment on the deep surface of the distal
quadriceps tendon over a span of about 20 mm, ending at the top of the
patella.

The two structures share a femoral origin and separate as they run forwards,
so they are often described together as the medial patellofemoral complex.

Whether it is a separate ligament or the proximal part of one sheet is argued,
in the same way and for the same reason as the anterolateral ligament in
2.11.3.

### Relations

Above and in front: the quadriceps tendon, into whose deep surface it inserts.

Below: the medial patellofemoral ligament proper, running to the patellar
border.

Behind: the femoral attachment they share, between the adductor tubercle and
the medial epicondyle.

Superficial: vastus medialis obliquus, which overlies the whole complex.

### Function

It restrains lateral movement of the patella, in the same direction and
through the same range as the medial patellofemoral ligament.

Because it attaches to tendon, its tension changes with quadriceps contraction
as well as with knee position.

Together the two structures form one restraint acting over a span from the
quadriceps tendon to the mid medial patellar border.

### Clinical Relevance

Its existence is why a reconstruction can be fixed to the quadriceps tendon
rather than to the patella, which avoids drilling a small and sometimes
fragile bone.

A tendon attachment means the restraint is partly dynamic, which no bone
attachment is.

7.51 covers reconstruction. This page covers what is there to reconstruct.

### Key Learning Points

- It runs from the shared femoral area to the deep surface of the quadriceps tendon.
- It attaches to tendon, not to the patella.
- Conventional teaching gives it a tendon attachment span of about 20 mm.
- Its tension changes with quadriceps contraction as well as with knee position.
- Its existence is why a graft can be fixed to tendon rather than to bone.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe the medial patellofemoral complex as one fan from a small femoral
origin to a broad anterior attachment, part on tendon and part on bone. The
two names divide the fan by where it lands.

The femoral origin is shared and small. Conventional teaching places it in the
same saddle 2.12.2 describes, which is why a single graft can reproduce both.

The tendon attachment is to the deep surface and is easily missed from
outside, because it is covered by the tendon it attaches to.

Identification rates differ between studies for the same reason they do at the
anterolateral knee: the dissection decides what is found.

### Relations

It lies in layer two with the medial patellofemoral ligament, deep to vastus
medialis obliquus and superficial to the capsule.

The adductor tubercle is the landmark for its femoral end, as for the ligament
below it.

The quadriceps tendon's deep surface is the landmark for its anterior end,
about 20 mm proximal to the patellar pole.

The suprapatellar pouch lies deep to it, so an attachment on the tendon's
undersurface sits directly over synovium.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Supply is shared with the medial patellofemoral ligament, from the descending
genicular and medial genicular arteries.

The quadriceps tendon end has the better supply of the two, which is relevant
where a graft is fixed there.

Nerve supply is from the medial articular branches.

### Function

Its contribution to lateral restraint is additive with the ligament below it,
and the two have not been reliably separated experimentally.

Its tendon attachment makes part of the restraint dependent on quadriceps
tone, so the complex is not purely passive.

Its line of pull is more proximal than the ligament's, so it acts on the
patella through the tendon rather than directly.

### Clinical Relevance

Consider a quadriceps tendon fixation where the patella is small, osteopenic
or previously drilled, and 7.51 carries the technique.

Expect a tendon fixation to behave slightly differently from a patellar one,
because the attachment is dynamic rather than fixed.

Avoid treating the two structures as alternatives. The anatomy is one fan and
a reconstruction reproduces part of it wherever it is fixed.

Identify the femoral point once. It serves both structures, which is the
practical consequence of the shared origin.

### Key Learning Points

- One fan from a small femoral origin to a broad anterior attachment, part tendon and part bone.
- The femoral origin is shared, so one graft can reproduce both.
- The tendon attachment is on the deep surface, about 20 mm above the patellar pole.
- Part of the restraint depends on quadriceps tone.
- Identification rates differ because the dissection decides what is found.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The shared femoral origin is the anatomy that matters most, because it means
the accuracy requirement is the same whichever anterior attachment a graft
uses.

The tendon's deep surface sits over the suprapatellar pouch, so a fixation
there passes close to synovium rather than to bone.

The complex has no clean posterior border with the retinaculum, which 2.12.4
covers.

### Function

Treat the complex as one restraint with two anterior attachments rather than
as two ligaments. The experimental literature has not separated them and the
operation does not need them separated.

The dynamic element from the tendon attachment is small and real. A construct
fixed only to bone reproduces a restraint that does not change with quadriceps
tone.

Length behaviour is set at the femur for both, so a correct femoral point
serves either fixation and an incorrect one ruins both.

### Clinical Relevance

Choose the anterior fixation by the patella's condition rather than by
preference for a named structure.

Confirm the femoral point radiographically whichever anterior attachment is
used, because that is where the error lives. 2.12.6 covers the landmarks.

Consider a tendon fixation in a patella that has already been drilled, since a
second tunnel in a small bone is a fracture risk.

Avoid describing a construct as anatomical for one structure when the femoral
point is shared. The accuracy claim belongs to the femur.

### Key Learning Points

- The shared femoral origin means accuracy requirements are identical for either fixation.
- A tendon fixation passes close to synovium rather than bone.
- The complex is one restraint with two anterior attachments.
- A correct femoral point serves either fixation; an incorrect one ruins both.
- Choose the anterior fixation by the patella's condition, not by preference.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

The named structure is less useful than the shared origin. Whether a construct
is called a patellofemoral or a quadriceps tendon femoral reconstruction, the
femoral point decides its length behaviour, and that point is the same for
both. Accuracy arguments about the anterior end are arguments about the less
important end.

Patellar fixation has a cost the tendon fixation does not. A small, previously
drilled or osteopenic patella takes a second tunnel badly, and patellar
fracture after reconstruction is a complication of bone rather than of
ligament. Where that risk is present the tendon attachment is anatomy rather
than improvisation.

The dynamic element is small and worth knowing about rather than relying on. A
tendon attachment changes tension with quadriceps contraction, which a graft
to bone does not, and in a knee with poor quadriceps control neither
reproduces much.

Over-tensioning is the shared failure mode. The complex is a check that is
unloaded in normal tracking, so a construct tensioned to hold the patella
rather than to stop it escaping loads the medial facet every step. 3.12.3
carries contact pressure.

Neither structure corrects bone. A patella that dislocates because of
trochlear geometry or tubercle position is not held by a 1 mm sheet, and the
anatomy says that before any trial does.

### Key Learning Points

- The femoral point decides length behaviour and is shared, so anterior naming arguments miss the point.
- Patellar fracture after reconstruction is a complication of bone, not of ligament.
- A tendon attachment adds a small dynamic element that a bone graft does not reproduce.
- Over-tensioning loads the medial facet every step.
- Neither structure corrects bony malalignment.
