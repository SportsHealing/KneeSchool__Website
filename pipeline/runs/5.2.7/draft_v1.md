# Frequency Selection

The one setting that decides whether an ultrasound study can see the structure
at all.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Frequency sets both the resolution and the depth an ultrasound beam reaches, and
the two move in opposite directions. 5.2.6 covers sound waves.

A higher frequency resolves finer detail and penetrates less far, so the choice
is made by how deep the target is. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

It matters because almost every failed knee ultrasound is a frequency and depth
mismatch rather than a missed finding. 5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

### How It Is Acquired

Pick the highest frequency that still reaches the target, which is the whole
rule. 5.2.6 covers sound waves.

A superficial tendon at the front of the knee takes the highest frequency probe
available. 5.4.6 covers the patellar tendon.

A deeper structure, or the same structure in a larger patient, needs a lower
frequency and accepts coarser detail. 5.4.4 covers the posterior knee.

The focal zone is set separately, and placing it at the target sharpens that
depth at the cost of others. 5.2.8 covers reflection.

A probe footprint has to fit the surface, so the shape of the knee limits the
choice as much as the physics does. 5.4.3 covers the lateral knee.

### Key Learning Points

- Frequency sets resolution and depth in opposite directions.
- Pick the highest frequency that still reaches the target.
- A superficial tendon takes the highest frequency available.
- A deeper target or a larger patient needs a lower frequency.
- The probe footprint has to fit the surface.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer states the trade in one sentence and then applies
it to a named structure.

The expected points are the resolution against depth trade, the highest usable
frequency, the deeper target, the focal zone, and the probe footprint.

Say that a study reported as normal at the wrong frequency has excluded nothing.
5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Say that the patient's size changes the frequency and so changes what the study
can show. 5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

Say that the choice is the operator's and is not recorded. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

### How Good It Is

The frequency choice is what determines whether an ultrasound study of a given
knee structure is diagnostic at all.

Its best contribution is at the front of the knee, where the target is shallow
and the highest frequencies apply. 5.4.5 covers the quadriceps tendon.

It is limited at the back of the knee and in a larger patient, where the
frequency that reaches the target may not resolve the lesion. 5.4.4 covers the
posterior knee.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
frequency values appear either, in megahertz: probe frequencies are equipment
specific and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The frequency choice decides whether the study is diagnostic.
- A study at the wrong frequency has excluded nothing.
- The front of the knee suits the highest frequencies.
- The back of the knee and a larger patient force a compromise.
- The choice is the operator's and is not recorded.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this page explains which ultrasound requests are reasonable
and which are set up to fail.

A patellar or quadriceps tendon question is shallow and is the strongest
indication for ultrasound in the knee. 5.4.5 covers the quadriceps tendon.

A posterior or deep question is a compromise, and a magnetic resonance study
answers it better. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

In a large patient the same request becomes a different study, which is worth
anticipating rather than discovering in the report. 5.4.1 covers the anterior
knee.

An interventional target chosen for depth rather than for pathology changes the
approach, not the diagnosis. 5.4.16 covers the knee injection.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting a normal report without knowing whether the
frequency could resolve the lesion. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

A deep structure is reported from a study optimised for a shallow one. 5.4.4
covers the posterior knee.

Reduced detail from a lower frequency is read as a tissue change. 5.2.8 covers
reflection.

The focal zone sits away from the target and that depth is reported as normal.
5.2.6 covers sound waves.

A larger patient's study is compared with a smaller patient's as though the
technique were constant. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal report means little without knowing the frequency used.
- A deep structure is reported from a shallow study.
- Reduced detail is read as a tissue change.
- A misplaced focal zone produces a falsely normal depth.
- Two patients' studies are compared as though the technique were constant.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level frequency is the clearest case of an acquisition parameter
that determines the result and never appears in the report.

Two studies of the same knee at different frequencies support different
conclusions, and the report gives no way to tell which was done. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

Published ultrasound accuracy for a knee structure is therefore tied to the
equipment of the study, which is a stronger limitation than it is usually
presented as. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

Probe technology has advanced faster than the literature, so older figures were
obtained at frequencies no longer in use. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence
in imaging.

A structured ultrasound report would name the probe and the frequency, and
almost none does, which leaves the single most determining variable undocumented.
5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The practical response is to ask the operator what they could see rather than
reading the conclusion, which is the same answer the previous page reached by a
different route. 5.4.1 covers the anterior knee.

### Key Learning Points

- Two frequencies support two conclusions and the report distinguishes neither.
- Published accuracy is tied to the equipment of the study.
- Older figures were obtained at frequencies no longer in use.
- Almost no report names the probe and frequency.
- Ask the operator what they could see.
