# Ganglion Cysts

Cysts of and around the cruciate ligament, which are usually incidental and
occasionally the reason the knee hurts.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A cruciate ganglion cyst is a fluid filled collection arising within or adjacent
to the ligament, with a thin wall and no solid component. 5.7.1 covers the
normal cruciate.

They are described as fusiform when they run along the ligament's fibres and
lobulated when they sit beside it. 2.7.1 covers the gross anatomy.

It matters because most are incidental and a minority cause mechanical symptoms,
so the decision rests on the pattern of symptoms rather than on the finding.
5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

### How It Is Acquired

Confirm the lesion follows fluid signal on every sequence, which is what makes
it a cyst. 5.2.14 covers T2 imaging.

Establish its relationship to the ligament, since a cyst within the fibres
differs from one displacing them. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Look for an intraosseous component at the attachments, which accompanies these
and is a separate observation. 5.7.6 covers mucoid degeneration.

Look for a neck connecting it to the joint, which indicates where it arose.
5.14.1 covers synovitis.

Assess the ligament itself, because mucoid change and a cyst frequently coexist.
5.7.6 covers mucoid degeneration.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A well defined collection following fluid signal on all sequences, thin walled
and without solid tissue.

A fusiform shape running along the ligament's fibres, or a lobulated collection
beside it.

An intraosseous cystic focus at the femoral or tibial attachment. 5.7.6 covers
mucoid degeneration.

A ligament that is otherwise continuous, with no secondary signs of injury.
5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

### Key Learning Points

- A ganglion cyst follows fluid signal on every sequence.
- Fusiform cysts run along the fibres and lobulated ones sit beside them.
- An intraosseous component at the attachments is common.
- Mucoid change and a cyst frequently coexist.
- Most are incidental and a minority are symptomatic.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer confirms the lesion is a cyst before discussing
what to do about it.

The expected points are the fluid signal on all sequences, the relationship to
the ligament, the intraosseous component, the neck, and the state of the
ligament.

Say that most of these are incidental findings. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Say that the symptomatic ones cause mechanical symptoms rather than instability.
3.7.8 covers anterior stability.

Say that a solid component takes the lesion out of this category entirely. 5.14.6
covers synovial chondromatosis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a lesion following fluid signal on every sequence
has no cellular content, which is what separates a cyst from a tumour and makes
the diagnosis an imaging one. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

A cyst within the ligament's fibres separates them without tearing them, which
is why the ligament's function is preserved. 2.7.3 covers the histology.

A cyst large enough to occupy the notch can limit extension mechanically, which
is the presentation that makes it symptomatic. 2.7.12 covers the surgical
anatomy.

The intraosseous component shares the process with mucoid degeneration, which is
why the two are found together and are probably one entity. 5.7.6 covers mucoid
degeneration.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging characterises these lesions well and says nothing
about whether they are causing symptoms.

Its best contribution is the confident exclusion of a solid lesion, which is the
question that matters when the appearance is unfamiliar. 5.14.6 covers synovial
chondromatosis.

It is limited by being unable to connect the finding to the complaint, which is
the only clinically useful question here. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and the
comparison is the claim: characterisation is reliable and attribution of symptoms
is not, which is a comparison rather than a number.

### Key Learning Points

- Fluid signal on every sequence means no cellular content.
- A cyst between fibres separates them without tearing them.
- A cyst filling the notch can limit extension mechanically.
- The intraosseous component shares the process with mucoid change.
- Imaging characterises the lesion and cannot attribute the symptoms.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the finding is almost always a reason to do nothing, and the
exceptions are defined clinically.

An incidental cyst in a knee with unrelated symptoms is reported and left,
because intervention offers nothing. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

A large notch cyst with mechanical loss of terminal extension is the case in
which aspiration or excision is discussed. 2.7.12 covers the surgical anatomy.

A cyst with coexisting mucoid change is the same process, and treating it means
treating a ligament that is still working. 5.7.6 covers mucoid degeneration.

A lesion that does not follow fluid signal on every sequence is a different
problem and needs characterising rather than treating. 5.13.7 covers bone
tumours.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is attributing a patient's knee pain to an incidental cyst.
5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

A cyst between the fibres is reported as a partial tear. 5.7.2 covers the partial
tears.

An intraosseous component is reported as a separate bone lesion. 5.13.7 covers
bone tumours.

The lesion is called a cyst without checking every sequence, so a solid lesion is
dismissed. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

The coexisting mucoid change is not mentioned, so two reports describe one
process. 5.7.6 covers mucoid degeneration.

### Key Learning Points

- Incidental cysts are blamed for unrelated pain.
- An intrasubstance cyst is reported as a partial tear.
- The intraosseous component is reported as a separate bone lesion.
- Calling it a cyst without checking every sequence can dismiss a solid lesion.
- Coexisting mucoid change goes unmentioned and one process becomes two reports.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the cruciate ganglion is a small problem that illustrates a
large one: imaging finds things whose relationship to symptoms it cannot test.

The prevalence in asymptomatic knees means the pre-test probability that any
given cyst explains a complaint is low, and the report rarely says so. 5.1.6
covers imaging pitfalls.

Whether these and mucoid degeneration are one entity is unsettled, and the
literature reports them separately while describing overlapping findings. 5.21.2
covers the cruciate imaging literature.

The published series on treatment are small, uncontrolled and selected on
imaging, which is the weakest basis for an intervention on an intact ligament.
3.7.8 covers anterior stability.

Size thresholds for significance are quoted and none is established, which is
why no measurement appears on this page. 5.21.2 covers the cruciate imaging
literature.

A report that states the finding is probably incidental does more for the patient
than one that describes it precisely, and the convention does not ask for that
sentence. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The pre-test probability that a given cyst explains a complaint is low.
- Whether this and mucoid change are one entity is unsettled.
- The treatment series are small, uncontrolled and selected on imaging.
- No size threshold for significance is established.
- Stating that a finding is probably incidental helps more than describing it well.
