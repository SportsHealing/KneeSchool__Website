# Secondary Restraints

What takes the load once the cruciate has gone. They are the reason a
deficient knee can examine as nearly normal, and the reason it gets worse with
time.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A structure that carries no load while the primary restraint is intact can
carry a great deal once it is not. That is a secondary restraint.

Secondary restraints are the reason a joint keeps working after an injury.
They are also why the injury can be missed.

Redundancy has a cost. The structures taking over were not designed for the
job, and they stretch or wear under it.

### At the Knee

The medial meniscus posterior horn is the most important secondary restraint
to anterior translation. The condyle sits against it like a chock. 3.6.5
covers meniscal load sharing.

The anterolateral structures contribute, particularly to the rotational part
of the motion. 3.11 covers them.

The collateral ligaments and the posterior capsule contribute at the ends of
range.

Bony geometry contributes too. A deeper tibial concavity resists translation
more than a flat or backward sloping one.

### What Changes It

Time, since secondary restraints stretch under repeated load.

Whether they were injured at the same time, which is common.

Activity, since each episode of giving way loads them heavily.

Bony shape, which does not change and sets a baseline.

### Clinical Relevance

A knee that examined as nearly normal early can examine as clearly abnormal
later, without a new injury. The secondary restraints have stretched.

The medial meniscus posterior horn is loaded as a secondary restraint, which
is why ramp lesions accompany cruciate injury. 6.35 covers them.

Looking for the secondary restraint injuries is part of assessing a cruciate
tear rather than a separate task. Section 4 covers the tests.

Relying on secondary restraints is what non operative management does, and it
works in selected patients. Section 6 covers management.

### Key Learning Points

- A secondary restraint carries load once the primary has gone.
- They are why a deficient knee can examine as nearly normal.
- Redundancy costs: the substitutes were not designed for the job.
- The medial meniscus posterior horn is the most important secondary restraint here.
- Findings can worsen with time without a new injury.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Secondary restraint is a load sharing statement with a time dimension. The
share taken depends on how far the joint has already translated, so secondary
restraints engage progressively rather than at once.

That progression is why laxity measured early underestimates what the knee
will show later, and why examination under anaesthetic shows more than the
clinic. 3.2.10 covers the handover.

It also means the apportionment figures change after injury. A sectioning
study in an intact knee does not describe a chronically deficient one.

### At the Knee

The medial meniscus posterior horn provides a bony like buttress, and
conventional teaching reports a measurable increase in anterior translation
after its loss in a cruciate deficient knee.

The anterolateral complex restrains the coupled internal rotation, which is
why a high grade pivot shift suggests involvement beyond the cruciate. 3.7.9
covers the pivot shift.

The iliotibial band acts dynamically as well as passively, and its tension is
what reduces a subluxated knee during the pivot shift manoeuvre.

Posterior tibial slope is a non modifiable contributor. A steeper slope
increases the anterior shear component of any compressive load. 3.13 covers
alignment.

### What Changes It

Chronicity, through progressive stretching of the secondary structures.

Concurrent meniscal and anterolateral injury, which is common and often
missed.

Slope and plateau shape, which are fixed and set the demand.

Muscle activity, which substitutes dynamically and is modifiable.

### How It Is Measured

Sequential sectioning after cruciate division measures each secondary
structure's share in the deficient state, which is the relevant experiment.

Instrumented laxity testing before and after a meniscal procedure measures the
change clinically.

Imaging identifies the secondary restraint injuries, with ramp lesions being
commonly missed. 5.6 covers meniscal imaging.

Examination under anaesthetic reveals what guarding was concealing.

### Clinical Relevance

Look for a ramp lesion in every cruciate injury, given the posterior horn's
secondary role.

Expect a chronically deficient knee to be more lax than an acutely injured
one.

Measure slope where laxity exceeds what the injury explains.

Treat examination under anaesthetic as the more complete structural
assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Secondary restraints engage progressively, not at once.
- Laxity measured early underestimates what the knee will later show.
- An intact knee sectioning study does not describe a chronically deficient knee.
- The medial posterior horn acts as a bony like buttress.
- The iliotibial band's tension is what reduces a subluxated knee in the pivot shift.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat secondary restraint injury as the commonest reason a reconstruction
underperforms without a technical error. The graft restores the primary and
the secondaries were never addressed.

Ramp lesions and lateral meniscal posterior horn tears are the two that matter
most and are the two most often missed, which is why they are sought rather
than noticed. 6.35 covers ramp lesions.

Slope is the non modifiable secondary contributor that has become modifiable.
Anterior closing wedge osteotomy addresses it, and it is the mechanical
response to a graft that failed without a new injury. 3.13 covers alignment.

### What Changes It

Whether secondary restraint injuries were identified and treated at the index
operation.

Slope, and whether it was measured.

Chronicity before reconstruction, since the secondaries stretch while the knee
waits.

Graft and tunnel position, which determine what the graft is carrying.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing of combined injury states apportions the restraint in the
configuration that actually presents.

Lateral radiography and magnetic resonance imaging measure slope, which is the
actionable variable.

Arthroscopic assessment of the posteromedial compartment identifies ramp
lesions that imaging missed.

Revision series report the secondary restraint findings at reoperation, which
is the evidence that they were missed the first time.

### Clinical Relevance

Inspect the posteromedial compartment arthroscopically in every cruciate
reconstruction.

Measure slope before revising a graft that failed without a new injury.

Repair the secondary restraints at the index operation rather than later.

Reconstruct without avoidable delay, since the secondaries stretch meanwhile.

### Key Learning Points

- Unaddressed secondary restraint injury is the commonest non technical reason for underperformance.
- Ramp lesions and lateral posterior horn tears are the two most often missed.
- They are sought rather than noticed.
- Slope has become a modifiable secondary contributor.
- Revision series are the evidence that secondaries were missed the first time.
