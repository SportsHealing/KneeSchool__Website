# Meniscal Deficiency

A knee that has lost its meniscus. What it feels like, what it looks like, and
why the examination is about load rather than about tears.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Meniscal deficiency is a knee that has lost a significant part of a meniscus,
usually to resection and occasionally to an untreated root tear.

Examining it tests the consequences of lost load distribution rather than the
presence of a tear, which is a different job from the rest of this chapter.
3.5.5 covers the effects of meniscal loss.

The findings are those of a compartment carrying more load than it was
designed to: activity-related pain, an effusion after use, and in time
alignment change.

### How It Is Performed

Take the surgical history first: what was resected, how much, and when. 4.2.30
covers previous surgery.

Count and site the arthroscopy portals, which indicate the compartment and
sometimes the number of procedures. 4.3.7 covers surgical scars.

Assess standing alignment, because a deficient compartment collapses over
years and the change is the main objective finding. 4.5.1 covers mechanical
axis assessment.

Assess for an effusion and ask whether it follows activity. 4.8.8 covers
chronic effusions.

Palpate the joint line, accepting that tenderness here is common and poorly
specific. 4.9.2 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

Activity-related pain in the resected compartment, with an effusion after use,
is the characteristic picture.

A developing varus in a medially deficient knee, or valgus in a laterally
deficient one, is the objective confirmation and takes years to appear.

Lateral deficiency deteriorates faster than medial in most accounts, which is
worth knowing because a laterally resected young knee is a more serious
problem than it looks.

Joint line tenderness adds little, because it is near universal after
resection.

A mechanical symptom in a deficient knee raises a new tear in the remaining
meniscus rather than the deficiency itself. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

### Key Learning Points

- Deficiency is lost load distribution rather than a tear, so the examination changes job.
- Activity-related pain with an effusion after use is the characteristic picture.
- Developing alignment change is the objective confirmation and takes years.
- Lateral deficiency deteriorates faster than medial in most accounts.
- A mechanical symptom raises a new tear in the remaining meniscus.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning is that removing meniscal tissue reduces contact area and
therefore raises contact pressure on the remaining cartilage. 3.5.9 covers
contact mechanics after meniscectomy.

So the symptom is load-related rather than mechanical, and the examination
findings follow the load rather than any fragment.

The relationship is continuous rather than threshold, so a larger resection
produces a worse knee without a safe amount existing. 3.17.3 covers why no
threshold is supported.

Alignment matters more in a deficient knee than in an intact one, because a
varus knee with a deficient medial meniscus concentrates load twice over.
3.13.3 covers varus alignment.

In a young patient the same findings carry a longer time horizon, which is why
age changes the plan more than the examination does.

### How Good It Is

The examination performs modestly for deficiency and well for its
consequences.

It performs poorly at detecting how much meniscus is missing, which is an
operative and imaging question.

It performs well at documenting alignment, effusion and function, which are
what actually change management.

Serial examination outperforms a single one, because the deficient knee's
story is a trajectory. 4.1.8 covers the record that makes that possible.

### Key Learning Points

- Removing meniscal tissue raises contact pressure on the remaining cartilage.
- The symptom is load-related rather than mechanical.
- The relationship is continuous: no safe resection amount is supported.
- A varus knee with a deficient medial meniscus concentrates load twice over.
- Serial examination outperforms a single one, because this is a trajectory.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the deficient knee raises three options and the examination
contributes to each.

Load management and rehabilitation, where the examination establishes the
functional baseline.

Realignment, where the alignment assessment is the whole indication and a
deficient compartment with a matching deformity is the classic case. 4.20.4
covers osteotomy assessment.

Meniscal transplantation, where the requirements are a young patient,
preserved cartilage, acceptable alignment and symptoms localised to the
deficient compartment, and the examination supplies the last two. 3.6.14
covers the biomechanics.

The honest framing is that transplantation is mechanically coherent and
clinically thin, which 3.17.3 sets out, so the conversation is about buying
time rather than restoring the knee.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating a deficient knee's pain as a new tear and
arthroscoping it, which removes more tissue and worsens the mechanics.

The second is ignoring alignment, which is the one modifiable factor in most
of these knees.

The third is offering transplantation on the strength of the mechanics without
checking the cartilage and the alignment, which is where the clinical results
come from.

And treating a young laterally deficient knee as a minor problem, when it is
among the faster deteriorating situations in this chapter.

### Key Learning Points

- Three options: load management, realignment, transplantation, and the examination serves all three.
- In realignment the alignment assessment is the whole indication.
- Transplantation is mechanically coherent and clinically thin, so the aim is buying time.
- Arthroscoping a deficient knee's load-related pain removes more tissue and worsens it.
- A young laterally deficient knee is among the faster deteriorating situations here.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

This page is where the chapter's argument lands, because meniscal deficiency
is the predictable end state of the resection that the rest of the chapter's
tests lead to.

In referred practice the commonest version is a patient in their thirties or
forties with a compartment resected in their twenties, now with
activity-related pain and early alignment change.

The assessment is alignment, cartilage, symptom localisation and function, and
the provocative tests contribute nothing.

The hardest judgement is timing: too early and the patient does not need an
intervention, too late and the cartilage has gone and only arthroplasty
remains. 4.20.7 covers partial replacement assessment.

The chapter's honest conclusion is that the best examination of a deficient
knee is the one that prevented it, which is 4.9.8's point about recognising a
block and 4.9.11's about resisting resection in the degenerate knee.

No figure here supports these claims, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Deficiency is the predictable end state of the resection the chapter's tests lead to.
- The assessment is alignment, cartilage, symptom localisation and function.
- The provocative tests contribute nothing to this knee.
- The hardest judgement is timing, between too early to need anything and too late for anything but arthroplasty.
- The best examination of a deficient knee is the one that prevented it.
