# Radial Fibres

The cross threads. They stop the meniscus splitting along its length, which is
a different job from carrying load and just as necessary.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A bundle of parallel fibres carries tension well and comes apart easily.
Anything pulling sideways separates the fibres from each other.

Weaving in cross threads stops that. They carry little of the main load and
hold the structure together.

Woven fabric works this way. The threads running one direction take the pull
and the ones crossing them stop it unravelling.

### At the Knee

Radial tie fibres run across the circumferential bundles, from the periphery
towards the free edge. 3.6.3 covers the circumferential bundles.

Their job is to resist longitudinal splitting. Without them the meniscus would
separate along its length under load.

They are fewer and thinner than the circumferential bundles, and conventional
teaching places them more densely in the posterior horn.

They explain why the common longitudinal tear propagates along the meniscus
rather than immediately splitting it apart: the tie fibres resist, up to a
point.

### What Changes It

A longitudinal tear, which the tie fibres are what resists.

Degeneration, which affects them as it does the main bundles.

Load direction, since they are loaded when the meniscus is pulled apart along
its length.

Location, with the posterior horn differing from the body.

### Clinical Relevance

A longitudinal tear left alone can extend, and the tie fibres are what is
resisting its extension.

A bucket handle tear is a longitudinal tear that has extended far enough to
displace, which is this mechanism failing. 6.30 covers the pattern.

The two fibre systems fail in different ways, which is part of why tear
patterns are classified at all.

Both systems have to be intact for the meniscus to work, which is a point
easily lost when hoop tension is taught alone.

### Key Learning Points

- Parallel fibres carry tension well and come apart easily under a sideways pull.
- Radial tie fibres cross the circumferential bundles and resist splitting.
- They carry little of the main load and hold the structure together.
- Conventional teaching places them more densely in the posterior horn.
- A longitudinal tear is this system being tested.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The two fibre systems make the meniscus a composite with two failure modes.
Circumferential failure loses hoop tension; radial failure loses cohesion.

Radial tensile strength is the lower of the two by a large margin, which is
consistent with the tie fibres being thinner and fewer. 3.6.3 covers the
anisotropy.

That asymmetry is why the clinically important tears are the ones that cross
the circumferential fibres, and why longitudinal tears are commoner: the
weaker direction fails first and costs less.

### At the Knee

Radial tie fibres branch from the peripheral capsular attachment and
interdigitate between the circumferential bundles.

Conventional teaching describes them as most prominent in the posterior third
of the medial meniscus, which is where longitudinal tears are commonest.

They contribute to the meniscus holding its shape under load, so their loss
allows deformation as well as splitting.

The surface layer's random network contributes to cohesion too, which is a
third structural element usually left out of the account.

### What Changes It

Tear propagation along the circumferential direction, which loads them
directly.

Degeneration, which reduces cohesion and allows horizontal cleavage tears.

Repeated loading, which accumulates damage in the weaker direction first.

Location within the meniscus, since their density varies.

### How It Is Measured

Radial strip tensile testing measures the strength in this direction, in
excised tissue.

Electron microscopy demonstrates the tie fibre architecture and its regional
variation.

Tear propagation testing in cadaveric menisci measures the resistance to
extension directly, which is the function rather than the material property.

Clinical tear patterns are indirect evidence of the architecture, and they are
the most abundant evidence available.

### Clinical Relevance

Expect a longitudinal tear to be commoner because it fails the weaker
direction.

Read a horizontal cleavage tear as a cohesion failure, typically in degenerate
tissue.

Treat loss of shape under load as a radial fibre problem as well as a hoop
problem.

Use the tear pattern as evidence of which fibre system failed.

### Key Learning Points

- Two fibre systems give two failure modes: lost hoop tension and lost cohesion.
- Radial tensile strength is much lower than circumferential.
- The weaker direction fails first, which is why longitudinal tears are commoner.
- Conventional teaching places the tie fibres most prominently in the posterior medial third.
- Horizontal cleavage tears are a cohesion failure in degenerate tissue.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the two systems as two separate repair problems. A longitudinal tear
needs cohesion restored across the split; a radial tear needs the
circumferential path reconstituted.

The constructs differ accordingly, and a configuration chosen for one pattern
is not automatically right for the other. 3.6.13 covers repair constructs.

Radial tear repair is the harder problem because the construct has to carry
the hoop tension itself until healing, and the tension is large. Conventional
teaching reflects this in the lower healing rates reported.

### What Changes It

Which fibre system failed, which the tear pattern names.

Construct configuration relative to the failed direction.

Tissue quality and vascularity, which decide healing capacity. 2.5.3 covers
the blood supply.

Load protection after repair, since the construct carries the tension until
healing.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric construct testing by tear pattern is the standard, and the patterns
are tested separately because they fail differently.

Cyclic testing measures gap formation, which matters more for a radial repair
carrying hoop tension.

Animal healing models assess whether the fibre path reconstitutes, which human
imaging cannot.

Clinical series report healing by pattern, and the differences between
patterns are consistent with the mechanics.

### Clinical Relevance

Match the construct to the fibre system that failed rather than to a default.

Expect a radial repair to be the harder problem, because the construct carries
the hoop tension.

Protect load after a radial repair, on the ground that the construct is the
tension path.

Read healing rates by tear pattern rather than in aggregate.

### Key Learning Points

- A longitudinal tear needs cohesion restored; a radial tear needs the hoop path restored.
- A construct chosen for one pattern is not automatically right for the other.
- Radial repair is harder because the construct carries the hoop tension until healing.
- Cyclic gap formation matters more for a radial repair.
- Healing rates should be read by tear pattern, not in aggregate.
