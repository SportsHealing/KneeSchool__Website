# ACL Reconstruction Biomechanics

The whole assembly. A graft, two fixations, two tunnels and a healing
interface, weakest in the months after surgery and never quite the original.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A repair is only as strong as its weakest part. Judging it means looking at
the whole assembly rather than the best component.

The assembly also changes over time. A biological construct is weaker at three
months than at implantation.

So a reconstruction has to be described at a stated time, in a stated
direction, as a whole.

### At the Knee

The construct is the graft, its fixation at each end, the tunnels it passes
through, and the healing between graft and bone. 3.7.16 covers grafts.

At implantation the fixation is usually the weakest part. After healing the
graft itself becomes the limit.

Where the tunnels are placed decides how the graft's length changes through
the range, which decides how the knee behaves. 3.7.4 covers the bundles.

Conventional teaching holds that a well placed reconstruction restores
anterior translation close to normal and rotational control less completely.
3.7.7 covers rotation.

### What Changes It

Tunnel position, which is the largest single factor in how the knee behaves
afterwards.

Graft choice and diameter.

Fixation method at each end.

Time, through graft remodelling.

### Clinical Relevance

Rehabilitation is staged because the construct is weakest in the months after
surgery, not because the knee is sore. Section 6 covers management.

A reconstruction that restores stability may not restore the feeling of
normality, which follows from what the graft does and does not replace. 3.7.1
covers the functions.

Tunnel position is the thing to check when a reconstruction underperforms.
Section 5 covers imaging.

Graft failure and a new injury are different events, and the history usually
separates them.

### Key Learning Points

- A construct is only as strong as its weakest part.
- The construct is graft, two fixations, two tunnels and a healing interface.
- Fixation is the early limit; the graft is the later one.
- Tunnel position decides how the graft's length changes through the range.
- Translation restores close to normal; rotational control less completely.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Three things are set at operation and cannot be changed afterwards: tunnel
position, graft and fixation. Everything else is rehabilitation.

Tunnel position dominates because it determines the length change pattern and
therefore the graft's behaviour at every angle. 3.7.4 covers the bundles.

Graft tension at fixation sets the angle at which the construct becomes the
limit. Too much tension over-constrains and too little leaves laxity.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching holds that a non anatomic, high anteromedial femoral
tunnel restores translation while leaving rotational laxity, which is the
commonest technical cause of a stable but symptomatic knee. 3.7.5 covers the
bundle.

Over-constraint is the opposite error. A graft fixed too tight or too far from
the native position restricts motion and raises contact pressure.

Graft tunnel motion causes tunnel widening, which matters for revision rather
than for the primary result. 3.7.17 covers it.

The remodelling curve means measured laxity can increase over the first year
without a new injury, which is creep and remodelling rather than failure.
3.7.16 covers graft properties.

### What Changes It

Femoral and tibial tunnel position within the footprints.

Graft tension and the flexion angle at fixation.

Fixation type and position relative to the joint line.

Rehabilitation loading during remodelling.

### How It Is Measured

Postoperative computed tomography locates the tunnels against footprint maps,
which is how a non anatomic position is identified.

Serial arthrometry measures the construct's behaviour over the first year.

Robotic cadaveric testing compares constructs for restored motion in all six
degrees of freedom.

Instrumented pivot shift measures the rotational result, without an agreed
threshold.

### Clinical Relevance

Place tunnels anatomically, since position dominates every other variable.

Tension deliberately, and record the flexion angle at fixation.

Expect measured laxity to change over the first year without a new injury.

Image the tunnels when a reconstruction underperforms.

### Key Learning Points

- Tunnel position, graft and fixation are set at operation and cannot be changed.
- Tunnel position dominates, because it sets the length change pattern.
- A high anteromedial tunnel leaves rotational laxity: the commonest technical cause.
- Over-constraint restricts motion and raises contact pressure.
- Laxity can increase over the first year through creep and remodelling.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the reconstruction as a construct in an environment rather than as an
operation. The environment is the secondary restraints, the menisci, the
alignment and the slope, and a construct in a hostile environment fails. 3.7.3
covers secondary restraints.

That framing organises revision. A failed graft is a question about which of
the four was wrong: position, graft, environment, or a genuinely new injury.

Conventional teaching identifies non anatomic tunnel position as the commonest
technical cause of failure and increased posterior tibial slope as the
commonest unaddressed environmental one. 3.13 covers alignment.

### What Changes It

Tunnel position and the achievable position in revision.

Slope and alignment, and whether they were corrected.

Meniscal and secondary restraint status.

Graft diameter and fixation in compromised bone.

### How It Is Measured

Computed tomography measures tunnel position and width and drives the revision
plan.

Lateral radiography and magnetic resonance imaging measure slope.

Arthroscopic assessment finds the secondary restraint injuries imaging missed.

Registry revision data reports outcomes, which are worse than primary across
every series.

### Clinical Relevance

Analyse a failure by position, graft, environment and new injury before
planning revision.

Measure slope in every revision, since it is the commonest unaddressed factor.

Correct the environment as part of the revision rather than afterwards.

Counsel that revision outcomes are worse than primary.

### Key Learning Points

- A reconstruction is a construct in an environment.
- A failed graft is a question about position, graft, environment or a new injury.
- Non anatomic tunnel position is the commonest technical cause of failure.
- Increased posterior tibial slope is the commonest unaddressed environmental one.
- Revision outcomes are worse than primary across every series.

## Fellowship Level

### At the Knee

The fellowship problem is the compromised construct: wide tunnels, confluent
tunnels, poor bone, a used donor site, an uncorrected slope, and a patient who
wants to pivot again.

Staging is the main mechanical tool. Bone grafting a wide tunnel and returning
later accepts a delay to obtain fixation that will hold, which is a mechanical
decision rather than a cautious one.

Combining a revision with a slope correcting osteotomy and a lateral
augmentation is the maximal environmental intervention, and conventional
teaching reserves it for the knee that has already failed a conventional
revision. 3.11 covers augmentation.

### What Changes It

Tunnel width, position and confluence from previous surgery.

Bone quality and whether grafting is needed first.

Slope, and whether correction is being added.

Available graft and the patient's demand.

### How It Is Measured

Computed tomography is the planning measurement for tunnels and drives single
or two stage.

Lateral radiography measures slope and plans the correction.

Revision registry and multi procedure series report outcomes in small numbers.

No measurement predicts whether a given compromised construct will hold.

### Clinical Relevance

Plan single or two stage revision from tunnel imaging rather than from
preference.

Correct slope in a knee that has failed a conventional revision.

Combine environmental corrections where the knee has failed once already.

Share the uncertainty, since no measurement predicts a compromised construct's
result.

### Key Learning Points

- The fellowship problem is the compromised construct.
- Staging is a mechanical decision, not a cautious one.
- Maximal environmental intervention is reserved for a knee that failed a conventional revision.
- Tunnel imaging drives single or two stage.
- No measurement predicts whether a compromised construct will hold.
