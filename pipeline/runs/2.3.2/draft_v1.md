# Attachments

What attaches to the proximal fibula: the lateral collateral ligament, the
biceps femoris and the posterolateral structures, and how they are arranged.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

Four structures of consequence attach to the proximal fibula.

The lateral collateral ligament, descending from the lateral femoral
epicondyle to the lateral aspect of the head.

The biceps femoris tendon, descending from behind to the head, with its own
expansions.

The popliteofibular ligament, running from the popliteus tendon to the styloid
process.

The peroneus longus, arising from the head and the upper lateral shaft.

### Relations

The lateral collateral ligament and the biceps femoris converge on the head
from different directions and their fibres blend there. The common peroneal
nerve emerges from behind the biceps tendon immediately before crossing the
neck, so the tendon is the landmark used to find the nerve.

The popliteofibular ligament lies deep to both, running up and medially to
join the popliteus tendon inside the capsule.

The peroneus longus origin arches over the nerve, and the nerve passes between
the muscle and the bone.

### Function

Together these structures make the fibula the distal anchor of the lateral
side of the knee.

The lateral collateral ligament resists varus. The popliteofibular ligament
and the popliteus resist external rotation of the tibia. The biceps femoris
flexes the knee and contributes to external rotation.

Because they converge on one small bone, an injury or an operation at the head
affects varus and rotational restraint at the same time.

### Clinical Relevance

An avulsion of the fibular styloid, sometimes called the arcuate sign,
indicates injury to the posterolateral structures. The pathology belongs to
Section 6; the attachment that avulses is here.

Finding the common peroneal nerve by following the biceps tendon is the
practical consequence of the arrangement above.

### Key Learning Points

- The lateral collateral ligament, biceps femoris, popliteofibular ligament and peroneus longus all attach here.
- The common peroneal nerve emerges from behind the biceps tendon before crossing the neck.
- The popliteofibular ligament runs to the styloid process and resists external rotation.
- An injury at the head affects varus and rotational restraint together.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Place each attachment precisely. The lateral collateral ligament inserts on
the lateral aspect of the head, in a depression, with its insertion deep to
the biceps expansion. The biceps femoris tendon inserts on the head
posterolaterally, splitting around the collateral so that part of it passes
superficial to the ligament and part attaches behind it. The popliteofibular
ligament attaches to the styloid process, which is why an avulsion of the
styloid implies that ligament.

The arcuate ligament, where present, arches over the popliteus from the
styloid to the posterior capsule. Its presence is variable, which is one
reason posterolateral corner descriptions differ between texts.

The anterior and posterior tibiofibular ligaments of the superior joint attach
around the facet rather than to these structures.

### Relations

Layered from superficial to deep on the lateral side: the iliotibial band most
superficially, though it goes to the tibia rather than the fibula; then the
biceps femoris with its expansions; then the lateral collateral ligament; then
the popliteus and the popliteofibular ligament inside the capsule.

The common peroneal nerve lies behind the biceps tendon above the head and on
the neck below it, so the tendon and the nerve are in a fixed relationship
that holds in a scarred field as well as a clean one.

The inferior lateral genicular artery runs forward just above the head, deep
to the lateral collateral ligament.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The attachments are supplied from the inferior lateral genicular artery and
from branches of the peroneal and anterior tibial recurrent arteries. Being
extracapsular, they retain a soft tissue blood supply after injury, which is
why collateral injuries heal and cruciate injuries do not.

The popliteofibular ligament and the popliteus are intracapsular and share the
popliteal and inferior genicular supply.

Innervation is from the common peroneal nerve and its recurrent branch, and
from the nerve to popliteus from the tibial nerve.

### Function

The lateral collateral ligament is the primary restraint to varus in the first
part of flexion and becomes less effective as the knee bends.

The popliteofibular ligament, the popliteus tendon and the arcuate complex
together restrain external rotation of the tibia, and they do most of that
work in extension and early flexion.

Biceps femoris adds a dynamic contribution to both, which is why the
posterolateral corner is described as having static and dynamic components.

### Clinical Relevance

Posterolateral corner reconstruction reproduces two or three of these
structures rather than all of them, and the fibular attachments are where the
distal fixation goes.

The fixed relationship between the biceps tendon and the nerve is the reliable
way to find the nerve in a revision or a swollen knee.

A styloid avulsion is an indirect sign of posterolateral injury and should
prompt assessment of rotational stability rather than treatment of the
fragment alone.

Harvesting or dividing the biceps tendon for reconstruction changes the
dynamic restraint as well as providing graft, and that trade is anatomical.

### Key Learning Points

- The biceps femoris splits around the lateral collateral insertion on the head.
- The popliteofibular ligament attaches to the styloid, so a styloid avulsion implies it.
- The arcuate ligament is variable, which is why posterolateral descriptions differ between texts.
- The biceps tendon to nerve relationship is fixed and holds in a scarred field.
- These attachments are extracapsular and retain a blood supply, unlike the cruciates.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The posterolateral corner is a small region with many named structures and a
contested nomenclature. The fibular attachments are the part everyone agrees
on, which is why reconstructions are described from them.

The arcuate complex's variability means an operative finding that differs from
the textbook is more likely to be normal variation than injury, and
distinguishing the two is a judgement made at the time.

### Function

Restoring varus restraint and restoring rotational restraint are separate
goals with separate anatomy, and a single fibular based graft rarely does both
well.

That is the argument for two tailed or anatomical reconstructions over single
limb ones, and it follows from the fact that the collateral and the
popliteofibular ligament run in different directions from nearly the same
point.

### Clinical Relevance

Fibular fixation in posterolateral reconstruction passes through or around a
bone with the common peroneal nerve on its neck. The nerve is identified first
in every described technique.

In combined cruciate and posterolateral injury the sequence of reconstruction
matters, because tensioning one changes the position in which the other is
tensioned.

Where the posterolateral structures cannot be reconstructed anatomically, the
alternatives trade restraint for durability. Section 7 owns the comparison.

The published anatomy of this corner is quantitative and the figures differ
between authors. None appears on this page, because a contested measurement
written from memory is the worst of both.

### Key Learning Points

- The fibular attachments are the uncontested part of a contested region.
- Varus restraint and rotational restraint are separate goals with separate anatomy.
- The collateral and popliteofibular ligaments leave nearly the same point in different directions.
- A finding that differs from the textbook here is more often variation than injury.
