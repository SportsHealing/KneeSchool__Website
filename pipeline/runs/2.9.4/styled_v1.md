# Posteromedial Corner

Five structures in three layers behind the collateral ligament, and the part
of the knee most often missed because no single test examines it.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The posteromedial corner is the region behind the superficial medial
collateral ligament and in front of the posterior cruciate ligament, on the
inner side of the knee.

Five structures make it up: the posterior oblique ligament, the
semimembranosus tendon with its arms, the oblique popliteal ligament, the
posteromedial capsule, and the posterior horn of the medial meniscus with its
attachments.

The three layer scheme that 2.9.1 introduced is what organises them. Layers
two and three merge here, and the merged sheet is the posterior oblique
ligament.

The corner has no clear boundary at either end. In front it becomes the
collateral ligament, behind it becomes the posterior capsule, and the
structures named above are regions of a continuous sheet rather than separate
objects.

### Relations

In front: the superficial medial collateral ligament and the interval between
it and the posterior oblique ligament.

Behind: the posterior capsule and the medial head of gastrocnemius.

Deep: the posterior horn of the medial meniscus and the joint.

Crossing it: the oblique popliteal ligament, running up and laterally from the
semimembranosus tendon to the opposite side of the capsule.

### Function

The corner restrains valgus near extension, when the collateral ligament is at
its slackest.

It is the primary restraint to internal rotation of the tibia in the extended
knee.

It resists hyperextension, mainly through the oblique popliteal ligament.

Semimembranosus tensions the whole corner when it contracts, so the corner is
both static and dynamic.

### Clinical Relevance

A corner injury is missed because no single test finds it. Valgus at 20
degrees tests the collateral ligament; valgus at 0 degrees and rotation in
extension test this.

A corner injury rarely occurs alone. It usually accompanies a collateral
ligament injury, an anterior cruciate injury, or both.

The combination of an anterior cruciate injury and a corner injury produces a
particular pattern of instability on rotation, which 3.9.7 covers.

Because the corner is capsular, an injury here bleeds into the joint.

### Key Learning Points

- Five structures: posterior oblique ligament, semimembranosus, oblique popliteal ligament, capsule, posterior horn.
- Layers two and three merge here, and the merged sheet is the posterior oblique ligament.
- It restrains valgus near extension and internal rotation in the extended knee.
- No single test examines it; valgus at 0 degrees and rotation in extension do.
- It is rarely injured alone.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe it by layers rather than by a list. Layer one is the sartorius
fascia, layer two the collateral ligament fading into the posterior oblique
ligament, layer three the capsule. Posteriorly two and three are one sheet.

The posterior oblique ligament is the static centre of the corner and
semimembranosus is its dynamic centre. Everything else attaches to one or the
other.

The oblique popliteal ligament is the largest structure on the back of the
knee. Conventional teaching gives it a width of the order of 15 mm where it
crosses the midline.

The posterior horn of the medial meniscus is part of the corner rather than a
neighbour, because the central arm of the posterior oblique ligament attaches
to it.

### Relations

The interval between the collateral ligament and the posterior oblique
ligament is the anterior boundary and it is only a real plane proximally.

The medial head of gastrocnemius arises just behind the corner's femoral
attachments and covers them from behind.

The semimembranosus bursa separates the tendon from gastrocnemius and
communicates with the joint in a substantial minority of knees.

The posterior cruciate ligament lies medial to nothing here: it is
intra-articular, and the corner is the capsule outside it. 2.8.1 covers the
relationship.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Supply is from the medial superior and medial inferior genicular arteries and
from branches of the popliteal artery through the capsule.

The corner is capsular tissue with a better blood supply than an
intra-articular ligament, which is the anatomical basis for repairing acute
injuries here.

Nerve supply is from the posterior articular branches of the tibial nerve and
from the medial articular branches, which is why posteromedial pain localises
poorly.

### Function

Through the last 30 degrees of extension the corner takes over from the
collateral ligament as the main valgus restraint.

It is the primary restraint to internal rotation near extension and a
secondary restraint to anterior translation, which is how it interacts with
the anterior cruciate.

Anteromedial rotatory instability is the combination of corner and anterior
cruciate loss. The tibia rotates and translates anteromedially as a unit.
3.9.7 carries the mechanics.

Semimembranosus tensions the corner, so its competence depends on a working
muscle as well as intact tissue.

### Clinical Relevance

Test valgus at 0 and at 20 degrees, and test rotation in extension. Three
findings separate corner from collateral, and 4.10 covers the examination.

Suspect a corner injury whenever valgus laxity is present in full extension,
whatever the finding at 20 degrees.

Examine the anterior cruciate in any knee with a corner injury, because the
combination is commoner than either alone in this pattern.

Expect an effusion. The corner is capsular, so bleeding reaches the joint.

### Key Learning Points

- The posterior oblique ligament is the static centre, semimembranosus the dynamic centre.
- Conventional teaching gives the oblique popliteal ligament a width of about 15 mm at the midline.
- The corner takes over from the collateral ligament through the last 30 degrees of extension.
- Anteromedial rotatory instability is corner loss plus anterior cruciate loss.
- Valgus laxity in full extension means a corner injury whatever the 20 degree finding.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The corner's anterior boundary is a plane that exists at the femur and closes
distally, so an approach that separates the collateral ligament from the
corner can do so proximally and not below.

Its femoral attachments cluster around the gastrocnemius tubercle and the
adductor tubercle. Three structures take their origin from a few square
centimetres of bone, which constrains every fixation point. 2.9.7 carries the
landmarks.

The posterior horn's attachment through the central arm means corner surgery
and meniscal position are the same question asked twice.

### Function

Treat the corner as the extension restraint and the collateral ligament as the
flexion restraint. The pattern matches the cruciate bundles in 2.8.2 and
2.8.3, and it is the reason a single structure repair restores part of a
range.

Its contribution to anterior restraint explains why an anterior cruciate graft
in a knee with an unrecognised corner injury is loaded beyond what it was
tensioned for.

Dynamic tensioning by semimembranosus means a reconstruction that restores
static tissue restores less than the corner's whole function.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess the corner before any anterior cruciate reconstruction in a knee with
medial findings. An unrecognised corner injury loads the graft.

Document valgus at 0 degrees, valgus at 20 degrees and rotation in extension
separately, because the three together stage the injury and no one of them
does.

Consider acute repair where the tissue is capsular and recently injured, since
the blood supply supports it. 7.38 carries the operative content.

Avoid treating capsular tissue that has already stretched as repairable.
Capsule that has failed once is not tissue to rely on a second time.

### Key Learning Points

- The plane separating corner from collateral ligament exists proximally and closes distally.
- Three structures originate from a few square centimetres around the two tubercles.
- The corner restrains extension, the collateral ligament restrains flexion.
- An unrecognised corner injury loads an anterior cruciate graft beyond its tension.
- Corner surgery and medial meniscal position are the same question asked twice.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

Staging is the whole problem. The corner is five structures and the
examination gives three findings, so a stage is an inference and the operative
plan has to survive being wrong. Plan the approach to expose more than the
plan needs.

Repair and reconstruction answer different anatomies. Acute capsular tissue
with its blood supply intact is repairable; the same tissue three months later
has remodelled at the length it healed to, and advancing it moves a structure
that has already failed.

In a multiligament knee the corner is usually injured and usually not the
first thing addressed. That order is defensible, but the anatomy says the
corner determines the rotational position in which everything else is fixed.

Reconstruction points compete for bone. The posterior oblique ligament, the
collateral ligament and the medial head of gastrocnemius originate within a
few square centimetres, so a construct that reproduces two structures has to
place two points in one small field. 2.9.7 carries the measurements.

The dynamic component cannot be reconstructed. Semimembranosus tensions the
corner and no graft does, so a static reconstruction in a limb with a poor
medial hamstring restores a limit without restoring the tension that keeps the
limit from being reached.

Consider the meniscus in every corner plan. The central arm carries the
posterior horn, so a corner that is advanced, repaired or reconstructed moves
it, and a corner left lax leaves it moving.

### Key Learning Points

- Five structures and three findings, so a stage is an inference the plan must survive.
- Acute capsule repairs; remodelled capsule has healed at the wrong length.
- The corner sets the rotational position in which the rest of a multiligament knee is fixed.
- Two reconstruction points compete for one small field of femoral bone.
- No graft reproduces the dynamic tension semimembranosus supplies.
