# Generation of X-rays

Where the beam comes from, which decides what a radiograph of a knee can and
cannot show.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

An X-ray tube produces a beam by accelerating electrons onto a metal target,
where most of their energy becomes heat and a small fraction becomes X-rays.
5.2.2 covers the image formation.

The beam leaves the tube as a spread of energies rather than a single one, which
is why a radiograph has the contrast it has. 5.2.4 covers scatter.

It matters because the two settings a radiographer chooses, the beam's energy and
the number of photons, decide whether a knee radiograph answers the question it
was requested for. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

### How It Is Acquired

The tube voltage sets how penetrating the beam is, so raising it pushes more
photons through bone and flattens the difference between bone and soft tissue.
5.2.2 covers the image formation.

The tube current and the exposure time together set how many photons are
produced, which is what controls image noise. 5.2.3 covers the radiation dose.

Filtration removes the lowest energy photons, which would be absorbed by the
patient without reaching the detector. 5.2.3 covers the radiation dose.

The focal spot is the area of the target the beam comes from, and a smaller one
gives a sharper edge. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Every one of these is a trade, which is the reason a knee specialist needs this
page at all. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

### Key Learning Points

- A tube produces X-rays by accelerating electrons onto a metal target.
- Most of the energy becomes heat rather than X-rays.
- The beam carries a spread of energies, not one.
- Tube voltage sets penetration, and current and time set photon number.
- A smaller focal spot gives a sharper edge.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer connects each setting to what it does to the
image rather than reciting the physics.

The expected points are the tube voltage and penetration, the current and time
and noise, the filtration, the focal spot, and the trade between them.

Say that a higher voltage reduces the dose and reduces the contrast between
bone and soft tissue. 5.2.3 covers the radiation dose.

Say that reducing the photon number raises the noise. 5.2.2 covers the image
formation.

Say that the exposure chosen for a knee differs from one chosen for a chest for
this reason. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

### How Good It Is

Radiography is the best available first test of bone architecture in the knee
and tells you very little about soft tissue.

Its best contribution is bone, cortical outline and joint space, delivered
quickly, cheaply and under load. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

It is limited by soft tissue contrast, because tendon, meniscus and cartilage
differ too little in their absorption to be separated. 5.5.1 covers the
standard protocol.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
exposure values appear either: tube voltage and current settings are local
protocol quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- A higher tube voltage lowers the dose and lowers the bone to soft tissue contrast.
- Fewer photons means more noise.
- The knee exposure differs from the chest exposure for that reason.
- Radiography is the best first test of bone architecture in the knee.
- Soft tissues absorb too similarly to be separated.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the physics explains why some questions should never be put to
a radiograph.

Alignment, joint space and bone architecture under load are radiographic
questions and are answered better here than anywhere. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

Meniscal, ligamentous and chondral questions are not radiographic, and the
commonest waste in a knee pathway is a radiograph requested to answer one.
5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

A technically poor exposure is a repeat dose rather than a lesser answer, which
makes the request detail worth getting right. 5.2.3 covers the radiation dose.

In a child the same exposure carries a different consequence, which changes the
threshold rather than the technique. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric
anatomy.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading a normal radiograph as a normal knee. 5.1.6
covers imaging pitfalls.

An over-penetrated film hides the trabecular detail that the question depended
on. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

An under-exposed film is noisy, and noise is mistaken for texture. 5.2.2 covers
the image formation.

Magnification from the focal spot and the geometry is read as a size. 5.2.5
covers magnification.

Scatter reduces contrast and is read as a soft tissue change. 5.2.4 covers
scatter.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal radiograph is not a normal knee.
- An over-penetrated film loses the trabecular detail.
- Noise on an under-exposed film is mistaken for texture.
- Geometric magnification is read as a size.
- Scatter is read as a soft tissue change.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the acquisition settings are invisible on the image and
determine what the image can support.

Two radiographs of the same knee at different exposures support different
conclusions about bone density, and neither records the exposure where the
reader looks. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

Digital post-processing rescales the displayed contrast, so an under or over
exposed acquisition can look correctly exposed while carrying less information
than it appears to. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

Serial comparison of bone texture across studies from different departments
compares different acquisitions, which is why texture is a weak longitudinal
measure. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

Automated analysis of radiographs inherits this, since a model trained on one
department's exposures meets another's. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in
imaging.

The practical consequence is that the request should name the question, because
the question is what sets the exposure. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Two exposures of one knee support different density conclusions.
- Post-processing can make a poor acquisition look correctly exposed.
- Bone texture is a weak longitudinal measure across departments.
- Automated analysis inherits the acquisition variation.
- The request should name the question, because the question sets the exposure.
