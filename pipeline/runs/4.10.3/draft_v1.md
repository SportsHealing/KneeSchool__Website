# Chronic ACL Deficiency

A knee that has adapted. What the examination finds, what it cannot find, and
why the patient's account matters more here than anywhere.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A chronically deficient knee has had months or years to adapt, and the
examination tests both the laxity and the consequences of living with it.

The laxity is usually easier to demonstrate than acutely, because the patient
is no longer guarding and the knee is not swollen.

The consequences are what matter more: muscle wasting, secondary meniscal and
cartilage injury, and in time alignment change. 4.3.9 covers muscle wasting.

### How It Is Performed

Observe the gait, because a chronically deficient knee may show a quadriceps
avoidance pattern or a varus thrust. 4.4.3 covers quadriceps avoidance gait.

Measure thigh circumference or compare it visually, since quadriceps wasting
is common and is the main modifiable finding.

Perform the Lachman test, which should be easier and more convincing than in
an acute knee. 4.10.4 covers it.

Perform the pivot shift, which is now obtainable and is the test that
correlates with the patient's symptom. 4.10.6 covers it.

Examine the menisci and the joint lines deliberately, because secondary injury
is common and changes the plan. 4.9.1 covers the meniscal presentation.

### What a Positive Means

A clear Lachman with a soft endpoint confirms the deficiency and says nothing
about whether it troubles the patient.

A positive pivot shift in a chronically deficient knee corresponds more
closely to the patient's complaint than the Lachman does, which is why it is
worth obtaining.

Quadriceps wasting indicates both a disuse consequence and a treatable
contributor to instability.

Joint line tenderness or mechanical symptoms raise a secondary meniscal tear,
which is common in chronic deficiency and often the reason for presentation.

A varus thrust during gait raises a lateral side component or established
degenerative change. 4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

### Key Learning Points

- A chronically deficient knee is easier to examine because it is no longer guarding.
- The consequences matter more than the laxity: wasting, secondary injury, alignment change.
- A clear Lachman confirms deficiency and says nothing about whether it troubles the patient.
- A positive pivot shift corresponds more closely to the patient's complaint.
- Secondary meniscal injury is common and is often the reason for presentation.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a deficient knee is not a static finding but a
trajectory.

The ligament does not heal, so the laxity persists, and the question is what
the laxity has done to the rest of the joint.

Secondary meniscal tears accumulate, because the abnormal translation loads
the posterior horn of the medial meniscus repeatedly. 3.5.8 covers contact
mechanics after injury.

Cartilage damage follows the meniscal loss, and the sequence is why a
long-deficient knee looks different from a recently injured one.

Gait adaptation is also a finding rather than a compensation to be corrected:
a quadriceps avoidance pattern reduces the load the ligament would resist, and
it appears in a minority of deficient knees rather than most. 3.14.9 covers
that correction.

### How Good It Is

The examination performs better in a chronic knee than in an acute one for
laxity, and that difference is large.

The Lachman test is reliable here and the pivot shift becomes obtainable,
which makes the chronic examination the most informative awake assessment
available short of anaesthesia.

It performs poorly at the question that matters, which is whether this patient
needs reconstruction, because that depends on their symptoms and demands
rather than on their laxity grade. 4.10.1 covers the history.

It performs well at detecting the secondary injuries, and that is where the
examination earns its place in a chronic knee.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament does not heal, so the question is what the laxity has done to the joint.
- Secondary meniscal tears accumulate because abnormal translation loads the posterior horn.
- Quadriceps avoidance appears in a minority of deficient knees rather than most.
- The chronic examination is the most informative awake assessment short of anaesthesia.
- It detects secondary injury well and answers the reconstruction question badly.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the chronic examination contributes the secondary findings
and the alignment, and the history contributes the indication.

A chronically deficient knee with a repairable meniscal tear is a stronger
case for surgery than one with laxity alone, because there is something to
repair.

A chronically deficient knee with established degenerative change and
malalignment is a different operation, and may be a realignment rather than a
reconstruction. 4.20.4 covers osteotomy assessment.

Quadriceps deficit should be addressed before reconstruction rather than
after, because a knee with better quadriceps function before surgery does
better after it. 4.2.34 covers rehabilitation.

A patient who has adapted successfully, has no secondary injury and does not
want to return to pivoting is reasonably managed without surgery, and 3.17.1
sets out why that balance is genuine.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reconstructing on the strength of the laxity in a
patient whose function is acceptable.

The second is missing the secondary meniscal tear, which is frequently the
actual reason they presented. 4.9.1 covers it.

The third is missing the alignment, because a varus deficient knee that is
reconstructed without realignment loads the graft abnormally. 4.5.2 covers
varus alignment.

And treating quadriceps wasting as a consequence to be managed afterwards
rather than a problem to be fixed first.

### Key Learning Points

- The examination contributes secondary findings and alignment; the history contributes the indication.
- A repairable meniscal tear strengthens the case, because there is something to repair.
- Established degeneration with malalignment may be a realignment rather than a reconstruction.
- Address quadriceps deficit before reconstruction rather than after.
- A varus deficient knee reconstructed without realignment loads the graft abnormally.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the long-deficient knee is frequently presented as a
reconstruction candidate when the assessment that matters is whether
reconstruction is still appropriate.

The three findings that argue against it are established degenerative change,
a deficient meniscus, and malalignment, and all three are assessable in
clinic. 4.9.10 covers deficiency.

The patient who has coped for twenty years and now presents with pain rather
than instability usually has degenerative disease, and reconstructing their
ligament treats the wrong problem. 4.18.2 covers established disease.

The patient who has coped and now presents with instability during a changed
occupation or activity has a genuine new indication.

Separating those two is a history task and the examination's role is to
establish what the joint can still tolerate. 4.2.1 covers the pain history.

### Key Learning Points

- The question in a long-deficient knee is whether reconstruction is still appropriate.
- Degeneration, meniscal deficiency and malalignment all argue against it and are assessable in clinic.
- Pain after twenty years of coping is usually degenerative disease.
- Instability after a change of occupation is a genuine new indication.
- The examination establishes what the joint can still tolerate.
