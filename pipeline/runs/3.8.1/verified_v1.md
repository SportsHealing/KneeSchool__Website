# Functions of the PCL

The strongest ligament in the knee, resisting the tibia sliding backwards. It
also holds the position the rest of the joint is measured from.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A ligament resists the movement that stretches it. One running from the front
of the femur to the back of the tibia resists the tibia sliding backwards.

A ligament at the centre of a joint also sets where the bones sit relative to
each other at rest, which is a position rather than a limit.

Position matters because every other measurement is taken from it. Move the
starting point and every reading changes.

### At the Knee

The posterior cruciate runs from the lateral wall of the medial femoral
condyle to the back of the tibia, below the joint surface. 2.8.1 covers the
anatomy.

Its primary function is resisting posterior translation of the tibia. 3.8.2
covers it.

It is the strongest ligament in the knee. Conventional teaching gives it
roughly twice the ultimate strength of the anterior cruciate.

It is often called the central pivot of the knee, because the joint rotates
about it and it holds the tibia in its resting position.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, since its tension rises with flexion. 3.8.3 covers the bundles.

Quadriceps activity, which pulls the tibia forwards and unloads it.

The posterolateral corner, which shares the restraint of posterior and
rotational motion. 2.10.4 covers the corner.

Gravity and limb position, which is why a sag appears when the knee is held
flexed and unsupported.

### Clinical Relevance

A deficient posterior cruciate lets the tibia sag backwards, which changes the
resting position before it changes any excursion. 3.8.6 covers the drawer.

That is why the step off between the femoral condyle and the tibial plateau is
examined before any drawer is performed. Section 4 covers the tests.

Patients describe difficulty going downhill and down stairs rather than
rotational giving way, which follows from the direction it resists.

It heals in continuity more often than the anterior cruciate, which is one
reason its management differs. Section 6 covers management.

### Key Learning Points

- A ligament resists the movement that stretches it.
- The posterior cruciate resists posterior tibial translation.
- Conventional teaching gives it roughly twice the strength of the anterior cruciate.
- It is the central pivot: it sets the tibia's resting position.
- Deficiency changes the resting position before it changes any excursion.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Separate three functions: a limit, a position and a guide. The ligament limits
posterior translation, sets the resting position, and guides the rollback of
the femoral condyles in flexion. 3.3.2 covers roll and glide.

The position function is what makes posterior instability different from
anterior. An anterior cruciate deficient knee sits normally and translates
further; a posterior cruciate deficient knee sits wrongly.

That difference changes how it is measured, because a drawer measured from a
sagged starting position underestimates the excursion.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the posterior cruciate roughly 95 per cent of the
restraint to posterior translation at 90 degrees of flexion, which is a larger
share than the anterior cruciate holds for its direction. 3.7.2 covers that
comparison.

Its tension rises with flexion, so the restraint is greatest where posterior
loads are largest. 3.8.3 covers the bundles.

It is a secondary restraint to external rotation, which is why an isolated
injury and a combined corner injury behave differently. 2.10.4 covers the
corner.

The meniscofemoral ligaments provide some posterior restraint independently,
which is why a deficient knee is not completely unrestrained. 2.6.3 covers
them.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, as the dominant variable.

Quadriceps activity, which reduces posterior translation actively.

Posterolateral corner integrity, which decides whether the picture is
posterior or posterolateral.

Time, since partial healing changes the measured laxity over months.

### How It Is Measured

Sectioning studies with a posterior load apportion the restraint, with the
usual sectioning order limitation. 3.2.9 covers that.

Stress radiography measures the posterior translation against bony landmarks
and is the most reproducible clinical measurement here.

The quadriceps active test demonstrates the sag mechanically by reducing it.

Instrumented arthrometry is less reliable posteriorly than anteriorly, because
the starting position is uncertain.

### Clinical Relevance

Establish the resting position before measuring any posterior excursion.

Use stress radiography where the grade will decide the treatment.

Examine the posterolateral corner in every posterior cruciate injury.

Expect measured laxity to change over months through partial healing.

### Key Learning Points

- Three functions: a limit, a position and a guide.
- A posterior cruciate deficient knee sits wrongly rather than translating further.
- Conventional teaching gives it roughly 95 per cent of posterior restraint at 90 degrees.
- Its tension rises with flexion, where posterior loads are largest.
- A drawer measured from a sagged start underestimates the excursion.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the position function as the one that explains the natural history. A
chronically sagged knee loads the medial and patellofemoral compartments
differently, which is the mechanical route to degeneration after posterior
cruciate injury. 3.5.8 covers contact mechanics after cruciate injury.

Partial healing in continuity at a greater length is the finding that
separates this ligament from the anterior cruciate. The imaging can look
normal in a knee that is measurably lax. 5.8 covers imaging.

That healing is why non operative management succeeds more often here, and why
reconstruction results are less reliable: the operation competes with a
ligament that is partly working. Section 6 covers management.

### What Changes It

Corner integrity, which decides the pattern and the operation.

Alignment, since a varus limb with posterolateral laxity is a different
problem. 3.13 covers alignment.

Time and the degree of healing achieved.

Quadriceps capacity, which substitutes for the restraint dynamically.

### How It Is Measured

Stress radiography quantifies the grade and is underused relative to its
value.

Examination under anaesthetic removes the quadriceps contribution, which
matters more here than anteriorly.

Sectioning studies describe an acutely divided ligament; most patients present
with one that healed long.

Serial measurement over months captures the healing, which a single
measurement cannot.

### Clinical Relevance

Measure with stress radiography where the grade decides the treatment.

Reassess over months rather than treating an early measurement as final.

Assess alignment in any chronic posterolateral injury before planning
reconstruction.

Expect reconstruction to compete with a partly working ligament.

### Key Learning Points

- The position function explains the natural history.
- A chronically sagged knee loads the medial and patellofemoral compartments differently.
- Partial healing in continuity at a greater length separates this ligament from the anterior.
- Imaging can look normal in a measurably lax knee.
- Reconstruction competes with a ligament that is partly working.
