# MCL

The medial collateral ligament, where ultrasound can grade by layer and watch the
ligament work.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The medial collateral ligament has a superficial band from femur to tibia and a
deep layer attaching to the meniscus. 2.9.1 covers the superficial collateral
anatomy.

Ultrasound shows both layers and the plane between them. 5.4.2 covers the medial
knee.

It matters because which layer failed determines the grade, and the grade
determines whether the injury is managed non-operatively. 5.9.3 covers deep
medial collateral injuries.

### How It Is Acquired

Scan in the coronal plane along the superficial band from femur to tibia. 5.4.2
covers the medial knee.

Identify the deep layer separately, with the meniscus between its two parts.
2.9.2 covers the deep collateral anatomy.

Keep the beam perpendicular, since the band is parallel fibred. 5.2.10 covers
anisotropy.

Apply gentle valgus while scanning, which shows whether the band restrains. 5.4.15
covers dynamic instability.

Scan the femoral and tibial attachments separately, because the injury site
affects healing. 2.9.7 covers the medial surgical anatomy.

### What Normal Looks Like

A thin bright band of even thickness running femur to tibia. 2.9.1 covers the
superficial collateral anatomy.

A deep layer attached to the meniscus without fluid between the layers. 2.9.2
covers the deep collateral anatomy.

No gapping on gentle valgus. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

Intact attachments at both ends. 2.9.7 covers the medial surgical anatomy.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Thickening and loss of the fibrillar pattern, which is a low grade injury. 5.9.2
covers medial collateral sprains.

Fluid between the superficial and deep layers, indicating deep involvement. 5.9.3
covers deep medial collateral injuries.

Discontinuity of the band, indicating a complete tear. 5.9.2 covers medial
collateral sprains.

Gapping on valgus with the band not restraining, which is the functional finding.
5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament has a superficial band and a deep layer.
- Ultrasound shows both and the plane between them.
- Which layer failed determines the grade.
- Valgus applied during scanning shows whether the band restrains.
- The injury site affects healing.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the ligament to name when asked where ultrasound can both grade
and test a structure.

The expected points are the coronal plane, the deep layer, the perpendicular
beam, the dynamic valgus, and the attachment sites.

Say that the deep layer's involvement changes the grade. 5.9.3 covers deep medial
collateral injuries.

Say that the dynamic examination is functional rather than structural. 4.12.1
covers valgus stress testing.

Say that a medial injury rarely occurs alone. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate
injury.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the superficial band is the primary valgus
restraint and the deep layer anchors the meniscus, so the two failures produce
different problems. 3.9.1 covers valgus stability.

Deep layer failure separates the meniscus from the capsule, which is why a deep
injury is a meniscal problem as much as a ligamentous one. 5.6.10 covers ramp
lesions.

A femoral sided injury heals more predictably than a tibial sided one, which is
why the site is worth reporting. 2.9.7 covers the medial surgical anatomy.

Watching the band under valgus tests restraint directly, which no static study
does, and this is the chapter's recurring advantage. 5.4.15 covers dynamic
instability.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound grades this ligament by layer and watches it work, and sees nothing
else in the knee.

Its best contribution is the layer grading combined with the dynamic valgus
examination. 5.9.3 covers deep medial collateral injuries.

It is limited because the associated cruciate and meniscal injuries are out of
reach and usually decide the plan. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no gapping
or thickness measurements appear either: those are published quantities and this
environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The superficial band is the primary valgus restraint.
- The deep layer anchors the meniscus.
- A deep injury is a meniscal problem as much as a ligamentous one.
- A femoral sided injury heals more predictably.
- Watching the band under valgus tests restraint directly.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a layer level grade plus a dynamic examination is useful, and
only in an isolated injury.

An isolated injury with a normal cruciate examination is where ultrasound is
sufficient on its own. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Deep layer involvement or a posteromedial corner injury changes whether the
injury is managed non-operatively. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

A tibial sided complete tear is the pattern with the least predictable healing,
which makes the site a planning finding. 2.9.7 covers the medial surgical
anatomy.

Any suspicion of a combined injury makes magnetic resonance imaging the study.
5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting an isolated medial injury without a cruciate
assessment. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Anisotropy is read as a sprain. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

Only the superficial band is followed, so deep involvement is missed. 5.9.3
covers deep medial collateral injuries.

The injury site is not reported. 2.9.7 covers the medial surgical anatomy.

A dynamic finding is recorded without the technique that produced it. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- An isolated medial injury is reported without a cruciate assessment.
- Anisotropy is read as a sprain.
- Following only the superficial band misses deep involvement.
- The injury site goes unreported.
- A dynamic finding is recorded without its technique.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this ligament is where ultrasound's dynamic advantage is most
real and least standardised.

Layer level grading on ultrasound is defensible and the published grading systems
were built on examination and on magnetic resonance imaging, so the grade from
one modality and the threshold from another are mixed routinely. 5.21.1 covers
the meniscal imaging literature.

Dynamic valgus assessment has no protocol, no applied force measurement and no
agreed gapping threshold, so it is an impression recorded as a finding. 5.4.15
covers dynamic instability.

Stress ultrasound would quantify it and is not in routine use, which leaves the
quantitative laxity question with the examination under anaesthesia. 4.12.1
covers valgus stress testing.

The injury almost never occurs alone, so the modality that grades the medial side
best is answering the part of the question that least often decides the
operation. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

The reasonable use is a focused question in a knee whose cruciate and meniscus
are already accounted for. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

### Key Learning Points

- Grades from one modality are mixed with thresholds from another.
- Dynamic valgus assessment has no protocol and no force measurement.
- Stress ultrasound would quantify it and is not in routine use.
- The best graded structure is the least decisive part of the question.
- Use it for a focused question in an accounted for knee.
