# Acute ACL Injury

Examining a swollen, painful, guarding knee. What is answerable now and what
has to wait.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The acute examination tests three things that are answerable in a guarding
knee, and it should not attempt more.

Whether there is a haemarthrosis, whether the extensor mechanism works, and
whether there is a mechanical block to extension.

Laxity testing in an acutely injured knee under-reports, because pain and
muscle spasm resist the translation the test is trying to produce. 4.1.5
covers the timing problem.

### How It Is Performed

Inspect for swelling and for the position the knee is held in.

Confirm an effusion by the sweep test or the patellar tap, which are gentler
than most manoeuvres. 4.8.5 covers the sweep test.

Ask the patient to straighten the knee against gravity, which screens the
extensor mechanism. 4.7.8 covers extension lag.

Attempt gentle passive extension and characterise the endpoint, looking for a
block. 4.9.8 covers it.

Then attempt a Lachman test gently, accepting that a negative result in this
knee means very little. 4.10.4 covers the test.

Do not attempt a pivot shift in an acute knee. It will not be tolerated and
the result will be uninterpretable. 4.10.6 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

A haemarthrosis after a non-contact pivoting injury means a cruciate rupture,
a patellar dislocation, an osteochondral fracture or a peripheral meniscal
tear. 4.8.7 covers it.

A positive Lachman in this setting is strong, because a guarding knee
under-reports and a positive has overcome that.

A negative Lachman is weak and should not be recorded as excluding the injury.

A block to extension is a displaced meniscal fragment and changes the urgency.

An inability to straighten against gravity is an extensor mechanism injury
until proven otherwise.

### Key Learning Points

- Three things are answerable acutely: haemarthrosis, extensor mechanism, mechanical block.
- Laxity testing under-reports in a guarding knee.
- A positive Lachman acutely is strong; a negative one is weak.
- Do not attempt a pivot shift in an acute knee.
- A block to extension changes the urgency.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected sequence for an acute knee is compressed and the
candidate is marked on knowing why.

Say that you would not perform the full examination, and say which three
questions you are answering.

Say that you would re-examine at two to six weeks, because the examination at
that point is substantially more informative and is what should drive a
reconstruction decision.

Say that you would aspirate only if the knee were tense and the diagnosis
uncertain, and that aspiration is not routine.

Finish with the neurovascular assessment and with a statement about the energy
of the injury, because a high energy mechanism changes the priorities. 4.23.6
covers multiligament assessment.

### How Good It Is

Every laxity test in this chapter performs worse acutely, and the reason is
uniform: they all depend on the patient permitting translation.

The Lachman test is the least affected and remains the best available test
acutely, which is one of the reasons it outperforms the anterior drawer.

The anterior drawer is heavily affected, because the ninety degree position
shortens the hamstrings and a guarding patient resists easily. 4.10.5 covers
it.

The pivot shift is almost unobtainable acutely and is the test most
transformed by anaesthesia. 4.10.10 covers examination under anaesthesia.

So the ranking of these tests is not fixed: it depends on when the examination
is performed, and no figures are quoted for any of it, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Compress the acute examination deliberately and know which three questions you are answering.
- Re-examine at two to six weeks, which is what should drive the reconstruction decision.
- The Lachman is the least affected by guarding and the best test available acutely.
- The anterior drawer is heavily affected by the hamstrings in its test position.
- The ranking of these tests depends on when the examination is performed.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the acute examination's job is triage rather than diagnosis.

The findings that change the next few days are a mechanical block, an extensor
mechanism injury, a high energy mechanism with multidirectional laxity, and a
vascular or neurological deficit.

Everything else waits, and saying so is better practice than persisting with
an examination the knee will not permit.

The reconstruction decision should rest on an examination at two to six weeks,
when the effusion has settled and the patient is not guarding, and a decision
taken on the first examination is taken on the least informative one
available.

A repairable meniscal tear is the one thing that argues for earlier
intervention, and the history establishes that rather than the examination.
4.2.9 covers onset.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording a negative laxity examination in an acute
knee and treating it as reassuring.

The second is attempting the full battery of tests, which hurts the patient
and produces findings nobody should rely on.

The third is missing the combined injury, because the obvious cruciate
diagnosis closes the assessment. 4.12.7 covers one combination.

And aspirating a tense haemarthrosis without considering that the blood itself
is the diagnostic finding, which is a reason to record it before removing it.

### Key Learning Points

- The acute examination's job is triage rather than diagnosis.
- Block, extensor mechanism, high energy laxity, and neurovascular deficit are what change the next few days.
- The reconstruction decision should rest on an examination at two to six weeks.
- Recording a negative acute laxity examination as reassuring is the commonest error.
- The obvious cruciate diagnosis frequently closes the assessment too early.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

The acute examination matters most at this level for what it failed to record,
because the referred patient's pre-operative rotational state frequently was
never assessed.

A guarding acute knee cannot be assessed rotationally, which is a legitimate
reason and not a reason to leave the record blank: recording that rotation
could not be assessed is different from recording that it was normal. 4.1.8
covers the record.

In a multiligament knee the acute examination is both the hardest and the most
important, because the pattern of laxity recorded then is frequently the only
pre-operative account available. 4.23.6 covers the assessment.

An examination under anaesthetic at the time of any urgent procedure is a free
and under-used opportunity to record what an awake knee could not show.
4.10.10 covers it.

No figure here supports these claims, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Recording that rotation could not be assessed is different from recording that it was normal.
- In a multiligament knee the acute laxity pattern is often the only pre-operative account.
- An examination under anaesthetic during an urgent procedure is a free and under-used opportunity.
- The referred patient's pre-operative rotational state was frequently never assessed.
