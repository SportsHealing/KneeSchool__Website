# Skiing

The only sport here where equipment lengthens the lever acting on the knee,
and the one where a slow fall can tear a ligament.

## For Patients

### The Principle

A long lever turns a small force into a large turning effect.

If something is strapped to a long lever, the forces reaching it are
multiplied.

That is how a slow, undramatic fall can injure a knee badly.

### At the Knee

A ski is roughly a metre and a half long and is fixed firmly to the boot,
which is fixed firmly to the lower leg.

Anything that catches the tip or the tail of the ski twists the whole leg, and
the knee is the first joint that can give.

Bindings release at high forces, and they are not designed to protect against
every slow twisting fall.

The classic mechanism is sitting back while the ski turns away, which loads
the ligament inside the knee without any speed at all.

### Clinical Relevance

A knee injury from a slow fall in skiing can be as serious as one from a fast
crash.

Swelling within a few hours of a skiing fall is a reason to be assessed before
flying home.

Bindings should be set for the skier rather than left at a default. A shop can
do this.

No injury rates are given on this page, because no source was available to
check them against.

### Key Learning Points

- A long lever turns a small force into a large turning effect.
- The ski, boot and lower leg form one long rigid lever.
- The knee is the first joint that can give when the ski is twisted.
- Bindings do not protect against every slow twisting fall.
- A slow fall in skiing can injure a knee as badly as a fast one.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why are knee injuries so common in skiing when crashes look survivable?
Because the ski multiplies the twisting force reaching the knee. The visible
drama of a fall is a poor guide to what the knee experienced.

**Q.** Do bindings prevent knee injuries?
They are designed mainly to protect the lower leg from twisting fractures,
which they do well. They are less effective against the slower knee twisting
mechanisms.

**Q.** Is a brace worth wearing?
After a knee injury, many skiers find one helpful and some evidence supports
braces in previously injured knees. They are not a substitute for correctly
set bindings.

**Q.** Can I ski home on it?
Not if the knee gave way, swelled quickly, or will not take your weight.
Skiing on an unstable knee is how one injured structure becomes several.

See a clinician before continuing to ski if your knee swells within a few
hours of a fall, gives way, locks, or will not take your weight.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Equipment that extends a limb changes the mechanics of every injury mechanism
acting through it.

A ski lengthens the moment arm in the transverse and coronal planes and
stiffens the sagittal connection through the boot.

So skiing injuries are not simply high speed versions of other sports'
injuries. The mechanism itself differs. 3.4.4 covers lever arms.

### At the Knee

The phantom foot mechanism occurs with the skier sitting back, the uphill ski
turning away, producing internal rotation of a flexed knee with valgus. It
injures the anterior cruciate. 6.1 covers the ligament.

The boot induced mechanism occurs when a backward landing drives the boot's
rear cuff into the calf, pushing the tibia forward. It also injures the
anterior cruciate.

Catching an inside edge produces valgus with external rotation and loads the
medial collateral complex. 3.9.1 covers valgus stability.

The stiff boot prevents ankle absorption, so energy passes to the knee rather
than being shared. 3.4.8 covers energy absorption.

Speed is not required for any of these. Many occur at low velocity.

### What Changes It

Binding settings, which govern release and are set from weight, height, age
and ability.

Boot stiffness and height, which determine how much is transmitted to the
knee.

Technique and fatigue, particularly sitting back late in a day.

Snow conditions, which alter edge grip and therefore torque.

Previous injury and the use of a functional brace.

### Clinical Relevance

Ask about the mechanism in detail, since skiing has named mechanisms that
predict the structure.

Do not take low speed as reassurance, because the common mechanisms are slow.

Ask whether the bindings released, which is relevant to the energy involved.

Avoid quoting injury rates, which this page does not, because no source was
available.

### Key Learning Points

- Equipment that extends a limb changes the mechanism, not just the magnitude.
- The phantom foot mechanism is internal rotation of a flexed knee, sitting back.
- The boot induced mechanism drives the tibia forward from the rear cuff.
- A stiff boot prevents ankle absorption, so energy passes to the knee.
- Speed is not required; many of these mechanisms are slow.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Skiing is the one sport in this chapter where a technological change
measurably moved the injury pattern, and it moved it to the knee.

Modern boots and bindings nearly eliminated the tibial fractures that
characterised the sport decades ago.

The energy did not disappear. It moved up to the next available structure,
which is why the knee is now the characteristic skiing injury.

### At the Knee

The phantom foot position combines flexion beyond 90 degrees, internal tibial
rotation and valgus, which is an efficient anterior cruciate loading
configuration. 3.7.14 covers the mechanisms.

The boot induced anterior drawer mechanism loads the cruciate directly through
the rear cuff without any rotation.

Medial collateral injury from edge catching is commoner than cruciate injury
and is often managed non operatively. 3.9 covers the medial side.

Multiligament and tibial plateau injuries occur in high energy collisions,
which are a different population from the recreational twisting fall.

The anterolateral structures are loaded by the rotational component and are
relevant to the residual instability some skiers report. 3.11.4 covers the
pivot shift.

### What Changes It

Binding release settings, which are the main engineered protection and are
frequently set too high.

Boot cuff height and stiffness, which determine the boot induced mechanism's
availability.

Skier ability and fatigue, since the phantom foot position is a late in the
day position.

Terrain and snow conditions.

Equipment hire practice, where settings are often standardised rather than
individualised.

### How It Is Measured

Video and witness reconstruction of real falls, which is how the named
mechanisms were described.

Cadaveric and robotic simulation of the phantom foot and boot induced
positions. 3.16.2 covers robotic testing.

Binding release testing to standards, which is mechanical and excludes the
skier.

Resort injury surveillance, which gives rates this page does not quote,
because no source was retrieved.

The shift from tibial fracture to knee injury is a historical observation in
the surveillance literature rather than something measured prospectively.

### Clinical Relevance

Take mechanism in detail, since skiing's named mechanisms map onto structures
better than most sports' histories do.

Ask about binding release and settings, which bears on energy and on
prevention advice.

Treat a low energy mechanism as capable of producing a complete cruciate
rupture.

State that the historical shift from tibia to knee is an observation rather
than a prospective finding.

Weigh the skier's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, since this is often a
holiday injury in a patient whose sport is occasional.

### Key Learning Points

- A technological change moved this sport's injury pattern to the knee.
- Modern boots nearly eliminated tibial fractures; the energy moved up.
- The phantom foot position is an efficient cruciate loading configuration.
- Boot induced anterior drawer loads the cruciate without rotation.
- Medial collateral injury from edge catching is commoner than cruciate injury.
