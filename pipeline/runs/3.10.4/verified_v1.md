# Popliteus Function

The structure that is a muscle, a ligament and a meniscal guide at once.
Nothing else in the knee does three jobs that are this different.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A muscle with a tendon that crosses a joint inside the capsule can act as a
ligament when it is not contracting and as a motor when it is.

A tendon with attachments to a third structure on its way past also guides
that structure.

So one unit can be an active rotator, a passive restraint and a guide,
depending on which question is being asked.

### At the Knee

The popliteus runs from the popliteal sulcus of the lateral femoral condyle,
inside the capsule, to the posteromedial tibia. 2.10.2 covers the anatomy.

As a muscle it internally rotates the tibia and unlocks the knee from full
extension. 3.3.6 covers the reverse screw home mechanism.

As a passive restraint it resists external rotation and contributes to varus
restraint, through the popliteofibular ligament. 3.10.5 covers that ligament.

Through the popliteomeniscal fascicles it retracts the lateral meniscus during
flexion, keeping the posterior horn out of the closing joint. 2.6.2 covers the
fascicles.

### What Changes It

Whether the question is about active or passive function.

Flexion angle, which changes both.

Whether the tendon, the fascicles or the popliteofibular ligament is injured.

Lateral meniscal integrity, since the fascicles attach to it.

### Clinical Relevance

A popliteus injury is almost always part of a posterolateral corner injury
rather than isolated. 3.10.6 covers the corner.

Its three roles mean an injury affects unlocking, rotational stability and
meniscal behaviour together.

A hypermobile lateral meniscus without a tear can be a fascicular problem.
3.6.10 covers extrusion.

No clinical test isolates popliteus function, so it is assessed through the
corner's findings. Section 4 covers the tests.

### Key Learning Points

- A muscle crossing a joint can act as a ligament when it is not contracting.
- The popliteus is an active internal rotator and unlocks the knee from extension.
- As a passive restraint it resists external rotation and contributes to varus restraint.
- Through the popliteomeniscal fascicles it retracts the lateral meniscus in flexion.
- No clinical test isolates popliteus function.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Treating it as a complex rather than a muscle is the useful move. The
popliteus complex is the muscle, its tendon, the popliteofibular ligament and
the popliteomeniscal fascicles.

Each component has a different mechanical role, and sectioning studies that
divide the tendon are measuring something different from those that divide the
popliteofibular ligament.

That is one reason corner apportionment figures vary between studies: the
complex is not divided the same way twice. 3.2.9 covers the sectioning order
limitation.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the popliteus complex a major role in restraining
external rotation, with the popliteofibular ligament carrying much of the
passive share. 3.10.5 covers it.

Its intra-articular, extrasynovial course means it is visible at arthroscopy,
which is how a corner injury can be inferred intraoperatively.

The tendon's femoral origin sits in the popliteal sulcus, anterior and distal
to the lateral collateral's origin, and the two are distinguishable in
reconstruction. 2.10.7 covers the surgical anatomy.

As an active internal rotator it opposes the external rotation that a corner
injury allows, so its loss removes an active and a passive restraint in the
same direction.

### What Changes It

Which component of the complex is injured.

Flexion angle, which changes the passive tension and the active lever arm.

Lateral meniscal status, through the fascicles.

Fibular styloid integrity, which the popliteofibular ligament depends on.

### How It Is Measured

Selective sectioning of the complex's components apportions the restraint,
with the components divided differently between studies.

Electromyography describes the muscle's timing and is technically difficult
because the muscle is deep.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates the tendon and the fascicles. 5.10
covers imaging.

Arthroscopic assessment of the popliteal hiatus identifies tendon and
fascicular injury.

### Clinical Relevance

Treat it as a complex and name the component when discussing injury or
reconstruction.

Inspect the popliteal hiatus arthroscopically where a corner injury is
suspected.

Expect its loss to remove an active and a passive restraint in the same
direction.

Read apportionment variation as reflecting different sectioning of the
complex.

### Key Learning Points

- The complex is the muscle, the tendon, the popliteofibular ligament and the fascicles.
- Studies dividing the tendon measure something different from those dividing the ligament.
- That is one reason corner apportionment figures vary.
- Its intra-articular course means it is visible at arthroscopy.
- Its loss removes an active and a passive restraint in the same direction.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the complex as the structure that makes anatomic corner reconstruction a
three component operation: the lateral collateral, the popliteus tendon and
the popliteofibular ligament. 3.10.8 covers the construct.

Conventional teaching holds that reconstructing all three restores rotational
and coronal restraint better than reconstructing a subset, which is the
mechanical argument behind anatomic over non anatomic techniques.

The active component cannot be reconstructed. A graft replaces the passive
restraint and the muscle's rotational and meniscal roles are not restored,
which is worth stating rather than implying. 3.3.6 covers the unlocking
function.

### What Changes It

Which components are reconstructed and where they are attached.

Fibular head and styloid bone quality for fixation.

Alignment, which loads the construct. 3.13 covers alignment.

Graft availability for three limbs.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares anatomic and non anatomic corner reconstructions
against the intact knee.

Varus stress radiography and the dial test measure the clinical result, the
second without an instrument.

Postoperative imaging confirms tunnel positions against footprint maps.

No measurement confirms the active functions have been restored, because they
have not.

### Clinical Relevance

Reconstruct all three components where the injury and the graft allow.

State that the muscle's active roles are not restored by a graft.

Confirm tunnel positions on imaging, since the femoral origins are close
together.

Correct alignment, since the construct cannot carry a varus limb's load.

### Key Learning Points

- Anatomic corner reconstruction is a three component operation.
- Reconstructing all three restores restraint better than a subset.
- The active component cannot be reconstructed.
- A graft replaces the passive restraint only.
- The femoral origins are close together, so position needs confirming.
