# Deformity

The shape the knee has settled into, and the distinction between a shape that
can be corrected by hand and one that cannot.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Deformity is an abnormal resting shape of the knee, and the examination's first
job is to establish whether it corrects.

A deformity that corrects passively is a position the knee is being held in. One
that does not is a structural change, and the two have different causes and
different treatments. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

The alignment chapter covers measurement of the named deformities. This page
covers recognising a deformity and deciding whether it is fixed. 4.5.1 covers
the measurement.

### How It Is Performed

Look at the standing alignment from the front and the side, with the feet
together. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Look at the knee supine with both legs straight and nothing under them, and
compare the two resting positions. 4.3.5 covers the supine assessment.

Attempt to correct the deformity gently by hand, and note whether it corrects
fully, partly, or not at all.

Note whether correction is limited by a firm bony stop, a soft tissue stretch, or
pain, since those are three different findings. 4.7.5 covers end feel.

Measure the deformity you cannot correct, in degrees, and record the limb it is
in, because a tibial deformity and a femoral deformity are corrected in
different places. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

### What a Positive Means

A varus or valgus deformity that corrects passively indicates ligament laxity
rather than bony change, and the ligament examination is what localises it.
4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

A varus or valgus deformity that does not correct indicates bony or cartilage
change, and the radiograph rather than the examination grades it. 4.5.2 covers
varus alignment.

A fixed flexion deformity indicates a contracture, an effusion holding the knee
flexed, or a mechanical block, and the end feel distinguishes them. 4.5.4
covers it.

Hyperextension, particularly asymmetrical, indicates posterolateral corner
deficiency or generalised hypermobility. 4.5.5 covers it.

A rotational deformity points at the tibia, the femur or the hip rather than at
the knee joint itself. 4.5.6 covers rotational alignment.

### Key Learning Points

- The first job is establishing whether the deformity corrects.
- A correctable deformity is a position; a fixed one is a structural change.
- Correction limited by a bony stop, a soft tissue stretch or pain are three different findings.
- A fixed flexion deformity is a contracture, an effusion, or a mechanical block.
- A rotational deformity points away from the knee joint itself.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the question behind the deformity is always whether it is fixed, and
answering it without being asked is the mark.

The expected points are the standing and supine inspection, the attempt at
passive correction, the end feel at the limit, the measurement of the
uncorrectable part, and where in the limb it sits.

Say that you would examine standing and lying, because a deformity that appears
only standing is a dynamic one and belongs to the alignment assessment.
4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

Say that you would assess the hip and the ankle, since a knee deformity can be
compensating for one above or below it. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Say that you would obtain long leg standing radiographs for any deformity you
intend to quantify. 4.1.6 covers the relationship with imaging.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the knee's resting shape reflects the balance
between the bony geometry, the ligaments and the muscles crossing it, so a
deformity localises to one of those three.

A correctable deformity means the bony geometry is intact and the restraint is
not, which is the mechanical definition of laxity. 3.13.3 covers varus
alignment.

A fixed deformity means the geometry has changed, by cartilage loss, by bone
loss or by a malunion, and no soft tissue procedure corrects it. 3.13.8 covers
alignment and osteoarthritis.

A flexion deformity has a separate mechanism again, because the posterior
capsule shortens when a knee is held flexed, and the shortening becomes the
limit. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

### How Good It Is

The clinical assessment of deformity is a reliable screen for a fixed deformity
and a poor measurement of any deformity.

The fixed or correctable distinction is the part it does well, and it is also the
part that changes the plan, which is a convenient arrangement.

It is unreliable for small deformities and in obese patients, where the contours
are hidden and the radiograph carries the assessment. 4.23.4 covers those
patients.

It cannot distinguish a femoral from a tibial deformity reliably, which matters
because the correction is performed where the deformity is. 4.5.1 covers the
mechanical axis.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- A deformity localises to the bony geometry, the ligaments, or the muscles.
- A correctable deformity means the geometry is intact and the restraint is not.
- A fixed deformity means the geometry has changed, and no soft tissue procedure corrects it.
- A flexion deformity has its own mechanism in the shortening posterior capsule.
- The clinical assessment cannot reliably separate a femoral from a tibial deformity.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the fixed or correctable distinction decides whether the
operation is bony or soft tissue, which is the largest single fork in this
section.

A fixed varus deformity with lateral ligament laxity is an osteotomy problem
first, and a lateral reconstruction into it stretches. 4.13.8 covers combined
injuries.

A correctable valgus deformity with medial laxity is a ligament problem, and the
reconstruction has a limb that will protect it. 4.12.8 covers that assessment.

A fixed flexion deformity changes feasibility. A knee that cannot reach
extension cannot be reconstructed to a normal length, and correcting the
deformity precedes the reconstruction. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

Where the deformity is in the bone, the examination's job is to establish that
and hand the measurement to the radiograph, rather than to quantify it.
4.5.1 covers the measurement.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not attempting correction, which leaves the central
question unanswered.

The second is assessing deformity lying only, where a loaded deformity
disappears.

The third is attributing a deformity to the knee when it is in the tibia, the
femur, the hip or the foot.

The fourth is reading a flexion deformity in an acutely effused knee as a
contracture, when it resolves with the effusion. 4.8.2 covers the mechanism.

The fifth is recording deformity present without saying whether it corrected,
which is the only part of the finding that changes anything.

### Key Learning Points

- The fixed or correctable distinction decides whether the operation is bony or soft tissue.
- A fixed varus deformity with lateral laxity is an osteotomy problem first.
- A fixed flexion deformity changes feasibility rather than indication.
- A flexion deformity in an acutely effused knee is not a contracture.
- Recording deformity without saying whether it corrected records nothing useful.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the deformity is often the finding that explains a failed
operation, and it is often absent from the first assessment.

A patient with a stretched lateral reconstruction and a fixed varus deformity
has a sequencing error, and the revision is an osteotomy rather than another
graft. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

A patient with a fixed flexion deformity after a reconstruction has either a
cyclops lesion, an arthrofibrotic knee, or a graft placed with the knee in
flexion, and separating them needs the operation note and the end feel together.
4.7.5 covers end feel.

A deformity that has developed since the injury is a different finding from one
that preceded it, and in a chronically deficient knee the deformity is often a
consequence of the laxity rather than an independent problem. 4.11.6 covers that
progression.

In a knee with a previous osteotomy the deformity has been deliberately altered,
so the question is whether the correction achieved what was planned, and the
pre-operative films are required. 4.5.1 covers the measurement.

In medicolegal work the pre-injury alignment is almost never recorded, which
makes attributing a deformity to an injury difficult in either direction, and
saying so is the honest position. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A stretched lateral reconstruction with a fixed varus deformity needs an osteotomy, not another graft.
- A fixed flexion deformity after reconstruction has three causes separated by the end feel and the notes.
- In a chronically deficient knee the deformity is often a consequence of the laxity.
- After an osteotomy the question is whether the correction achieved what was planned.
- Pre-injury alignment is almost never recorded, which complicates attribution.
