# Football

The sport that generates most knee injuries seen in clinic. What position,
level and surface change about the history.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A football history tests what the knee was being asked to do, because the
sport asks for repeated direction changes on a planted foot. 3.15.1 covers the
mechanics.

Asking about the sport specifically establishes the likely mechanism, the
likely associated injuries, and what the patient needs to get back to.

The last of those is the one that determines whether treatment succeeded, and
it differs between a goalkeeper, a defender and a winger.

### How It Is Performed

Ask what level they play at, how many times a week, and whether they are paid.

Ask what position, since the demands differ.

Ask what surface: grass, artificial turf, or indoor, because the shoe and
surface interaction affects the forces the knee sees.

Ask what boots and studs they use, which is a modifiable factor patients
rarely mention.

Ask about the injury itself in the terms of the sport: turning, shooting,
being tackled, or landing. 4.2.16 covers non-contact mechanisms.

### What a Positive Means

A non-contact turn or deceleration with a pop and immediate swelling is an
anterior cruciate rupture, and football is the commonest setting for it in
clinic.

A tackle from the outside forcing the knee inwards is a medial structure
injury. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

A twisting injury with slower swelling and mechanical symptoms fits a meniscal
tear.

A goalkeeper diving onto a flexed knee raises a direct blow and a patellar or
osteochondral injury. 4.2.20 covers it.

Repeated anterior pain with no injury in an adolescent player is usually a
loading problem rather than a structural one. 4.16.3 covers one version.

### Key Learning Points

- Football asks for repeated direction changes on a planted foot.
- Level, position, surface and footwear all change the history.
- A non-contact turn with a pop and immediate swelling is a cruciate rupture.
- A tackle from the outside is a medial structure injury.
- What the patient needs to return to determines whether treatment succeeded.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning connects the sport's demands to the structures at risk.

Cutting and pivoting load the anterior cruciate and the anterolateral
structures, which is why isolated cruciate injury is less common than teaching
implies. 3.7.13 covers loading during cutting.

Contact tackles load the collaterals, and the medial side is involved far more
often than the lateral.

Repeated submaximal loading in a growing player produces apophyseal and tendon
problems rather than ligament injuries. 4.16.4 covers one.

A footballer with a cruciate injury should be assessed deliberately for
meniscal and chondral injury, because the mechanism produces them together and
the meniscal injury is the one with a repair window. 4.9.9 covers root tears.

### How Good It Is

A sporting history performs well for anticipating the injury and for setting
the rehabilitation target, and that second use is the one most often
neglected.

It performs well for recurrence risk: a player returning to the same movement
at the same level will see the same forces again.

It performs poorly as a diagnostic instrument on its own, since every injury
in this chapter occurs in football.

The most useful single question is what level they intend to return to,
because it changes the threshold for reconstruction more than any examination
finding. 4.21.8 covers return to play decisions.

### Key Learning Points

- Cutting and pivoting load the cruciate and the anterolateral structures together.
- Contact tackles load the collaterals, and the medial side far more often.
- In a growing player, repeated loading produces apophyseal and tendon problems.
- A footballer with a cruciate injury needs a deliberate meniscal and chondral assessment.
- What level they intend to return to changes the threshold more than any examination finding.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the football history determines the reconstruction
conversation more than the laxity grade does.

A player who intends to return to pivoting sport is a stronger candidate for
reconstruction, because the activity they cannot avoid is the one that
provokes instability.

A recreational player who has already stopped is a different conversation, and
the honest version of it includes the option of not reconstructing.

Age and intended level together predict both reinjury risk and graft choice
discussion, and both come from the history. 3.17.1 covers the balance the
literature supports.

The timing of the season is a real factor in the decision for a professional
player and is not a clinical one, which is worth stating rather than
pretending otherwise.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is assuming a footballer wants to return to football. Many
do not, and asking is quicker than assuming.

The second is accepting the player's or the club's timetable as a clinical
constraint without saying what the cost is.

The third is missing the associated injury because the cruciate is the obvious
one, which this chapter repeats because it is the commonest avoidable error in
this group.

And a young player returning early to the same sport has a reinjury risk that
should be discussed explicitly, since the contralateral knee is also at risk.
4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

### Key Learning Points

- Intended level of return changes the reconstruction conversation more than the laxity grade.
- The honest conversation with a player who has stopped includes not reconstructing.
- Do not assume a footballer wants to return to football.
- A club timetable is a real factor and not a clinical one, and should be named as such.
- Early return carries a reinjury risk to both knees and should be discussed explicitly.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the footballer with a failed reconstruction is the common
presentation, and the history separates the causes.

A failure during the same cutting movement raises rotational control and
tunnel position. 4.10.12 covers revision assessment.

A failure during a trivial movement raises graft choice, fixation, alignment
or a deficient meniscus. 4.9.10 covers deficiency.

A player who never returned, with a stable knee, usually has a confidence or a
rehabilitation problem rather than a structural one, and this is a larger
group than the structural failures. 4.22.6 covers the relevant instrument.

Separating those three is a history task and determines whether the next
intervention is an operation, a rehabilitation programme or a conversation.

No figure here supports their relative frequency, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Failure during the original cutting movement raises rotational control and tunnel position.
- Failure during a trivial movement raises graft, fixation, alignment or meniscal deficiency.
- A player who never returned with a stable knee usually has a confidence problem.
- That group is larger than the structural failures.
- The history decides whether the next step is an operation, rehabilitation or a conversation.
