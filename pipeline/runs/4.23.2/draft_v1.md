# Elite Athletes

Examining a knee whose owner is stronger, better compensated and under more
pressure than the patient the examination's references were built on.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The examination is the same, and the interpretation changes because an elite
athlete compensates better, presents earlier and is measured against a higher
baseline. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

The examination is looking for findings that a strong, well controlled limb can
hide, which means the functional tests carry more of the weight. 4.21.2 covers
the single leg hop.

It matters because a deficit that would be disabling in a sedentary patient can
be invisible in an athlete, and still ends their season. 4.7.9 covers the
functional range.

### How It Is Performed

Establish the sport, the position, the level and the point in the season, since
all four change what the findings mean. 4.2.23 covers football.

Establish the athlete's own baseline, which is usually documented from screening
rather than inferred. 4.1.8 covers examination documentation.

Perform the standard examination, since the tests do not change. 4.1.4 covers
the structured approach.

Perform the functional tests, because that is where a compensated deficit shows.
4.21.4 covers the crossover hop.

Test under fatigue where it is safe to, since a well compensated limb fails
later rather than not at all. 4.21.3 covers the triple hop.

Record the comparison with the other limb, accepting that an athlete's limbs may
be asymmetrical by sport. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Record the pressure the athlete is under as context rather than as a finding.
4.21.8 covers return to play decision making.

### What a Positive Means

A finding in an athlete is read against their own baseline rather than against a
population reference.

A small asymmetry that would be ignored in another patient can be the finding,
because the demand is higher. 4.21.2 covers the single leg hop.

A normal static examination with a functional deficit is a common pattern here
and the functional result is the one that matters. 4.21.7 covers return to sport
testing.

A sport specific asymmetry is normal for that athlete rather than a deficit, and
the baseline is what distinguishes them. 4.1.8 covers examination
documentation.

A deficit appearing only under fatigue is still a deficit. 4.21.3 covers the
triple hop.

### Key Learning Points

- The tests do not change and the interpretation does.
- The athlete's own baseline is the reference.
- A small asymmetry can be the finding, because the demand is higher.
- A normal static examination with a functional deficit is common here.
- A deficit appearing only under fatigue is still a deficit.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the athlete's knee and the expected answer names the baseline
and the compensation.

The expected points are the sport and context, the documented baseline, the
standard examination, the functional battery, fatigue testing, the limb
comparison and the external pressure as context.

Say that the athlete's own baseline is the reference, and give the reason.
4.1.8 covers examination documentation.

Say that the functional tests carry more weight here. 4.21.7 covers return to
sport testing.

Say that the pressure on the decision is context and not part of the
assessment. 4.21.8 covers return to play decision making.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that dynamic stability comes from passive restraint
and muscle control together, and an athlete's control can compensate for a
deficient restraint. 3.7.3 covers the secondary restraints.

Compensation is load dependent, so it fails at high demand and under fatigue
rather than in a clinic. 3.14.6 covers cutting manoeuvres.

A higher baseline means a given absolute deficit costs more function, which is
why small findings matter. 4.7.9 covers the functional range.

Early presentation means the examination is performed at a stage where the
findings are smaller than usual. 4.2.9 covers the onset.

### How Good It Is

The standard examination performs as it does in anyone, and its yield is lower
because compensation hides findings.

Its best contribution in this group is the functional battery against a
documented baseline, which no population reference supplies. 4.21.7 covers
return to sport testing.

It is insensitive to the small deficits that matter most here.

Imaging detects structural change the examination misses in a compensated
athlete, which raises its relative value in this group. 4.1.6 covers that
relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Control can compensate for a deficient passive restraint.
- Compensation is load dependent and fails at high demand.
- A higher baseline makes a given deficit cost more function.
- Early presentation means smaller findings.
- The examination is insensitive to the deficits that matter most here.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the athlete's examination feeds a decision with more parties
and more pressure than any other in the section.

A documented baseline converts a borderline finding into a measured change,
which is the single most useful thing available in this group. 4.1.8 covers
examination documentation.

A functional deficit with a normal static examination supports a specific
rehabilitation rather than a wait. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

The point in the season changes the cost of every option without changing the
findings, and keeping those separate is the clinician's discipline. 4.21.8
covers return to play decision making.

A deficit that only appears under fatigue is the one most likely to be
discounted under pressure. 4.21.3 covers the triple hop.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading a compensated knee as a normal one. 4.21.2 covers
the single leg hop.

A population reference is used where a personal baseline exists. 4.1.8 covers
examination documentation.

A sport specific asymmetry is called a deficit. 4.1.4 covers the structured
approach.

Pressure from the athlete or the club enters the assessment rather than staying
in the context. 4.21.8 covers return to play decision making.

A single clinic assessment is treated as sufficient in a group where fatigue is
the relevant condition. 4.21.3 covers the triple hop.

### Key Learning Points

- Reading a compensated knee as normal is the commonest error.
- A personal baseline beats a population reference.
- A sport specific asymmetry is not a deficit.
- External pressure belongs in the context, not the assessment.
- A single clinic assessment is insufficient where fatigue is the condition.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this group exposes the limits of every reference the
examination uses.

The contralateral comparison is weakest here, because sport produces real
asymmetry and the dominant limb is not a control. 4.1.4 covers the structured
approach.

Compensation is the athlete's skill, so the better the athlete the less the
examination detects, which inverts the usual relationship between severity and
findings. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

Functional thresholds come from populations that are not elite, so applying them
to this group is an extrapolation in the direction that matters. 4.21.8 covers
return to play decision making.

The decision has interested parties beyond the patient, which is a structural
feature of the setting rather than a failure of individuals. 4.22.8 covers the
use of these scores.

A career length consequence is weighed against a season, and the examination
contributes nothing to that weighing. 4.2.10 covers severity.

### Key Learning Points

- Sport produces real asymmetry, so the dominant limb is not a control.
- The better the athlete, the less the examination detects.
- Functional thresholds come from non elite populations.
- The decision has interested parties beyond the patient.
- The examination contributes nothing to weighing a career against a season.
