# Aggravating Factors

What makes it worse is the most mechanically informative question in the knee
history, because each activity loads a known structure.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Asking what makes the pain worse tests which loading pattern provokes it, and
loading patterns map onto structures.

This is the question where biomechanics meets the history. Section 3
established what each activity loads, and this question asks the patient which
of those hurts. 3.3.12 covers stair climbing.

It is more informative than the site of pain, because an activity is specific
and a region is shared.

### How It Is Performed

Ask for activities rather than movements. Stairs, slopes, squatting, kneeling,
sitting for long periods, getting out of a car, running, and turning.

Ask about stairs up and stairs down separately, because they load differently
and the answer differs.

Ask how soon the pain comes on: immediately, after a distance, or the
following day.

Ask whether the activity has to be stopped or merely hurts, since the two are
different severities.

### What a Positive Means

Pain on stairs, squatting, kneeling and rising from sitting points to the
patellofemoral joint, which is loaded in flexion. 4.15.9 covers it.

Pain on turning and pivoting points to rotational structures and to the
menisci.

Pain on walking distance with relief on stopping points to degenerative
disease.

Pain descending stairs or slopes specifically points to the extensor mechanism
or the posterior cruciate. 4.11.6 covers the posterior version.

Pain the following day rather than during the activity suggests a reactive
synovitis rather than a mechanical catch. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

### Key Learning Points

- Loading patterns map onto structures, which is what makes this question informative.
- Ask about activities rather than movements, and ask about stairs up and down separately.
- Flexion loading points to the patellofemoral joint; turning points to rotational structures.
- Pain the following day suggests a reactive synovitis rather than a mechanical catch.
- Whether the activity has to be stopped measures severity as well as mechanism.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

At this level the reasoning runs from the activity to the compartment via the
mechanics rather than by recall.

Deep flexion raises patellofemoral joint reaction force substantially, which
is why squatting and stair descent provoke patellofemoral pain and level
walking does not. 3.12.3 covers contact pressures.

A pivot loads the anterior cruciate and the anterolateral structures, so pain
or apprehension on turning implicates them.

Prolonged sitting with the knee flexed loads the patellofemoral joint
statically, which is why the complaint of pain after a long drive is
patellofemoral until proven otherwise.

Weight bearing through a varus knee concentrates medial load, which is why
walking distance is the limiting activity in medial degenerative disease.
3.13.3 covers varus alignment.

### How Good It Is

This is among the strongest questions in the knee history for localisation,
and it outperforms the patient's report of where the pain is.

The reason is that an activity is an unambiguous fact and a region is an
interpretation.

It performs best for the patellofemoral joint, where the provoking activities
are characteristic and consistent.

It performs worst in a knee with several problems, where every activity hurts
and the pattern is lost. That answer is itself informative and points towards
a reactive or diffusely degenerate joint rather than a single lesion.

### Key Learning Points

- Reason from the activity to the compartment via the mechanics rather than by recall.
- Deep flexion loads the patellofemoral joint, which is why squatting provokes and walking does not.
- Pain after a long drive is patellofemoral until proven otherwise.
- This question outperforms the reported site of pain for localisation.
- Everything hurting is itself informative and points away from a single lesion.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

The decision this question informs is whether the loading that provokes the
symptom is loading the operation would change.

An osteotomy changes medial load, so it addresses a symptom provoked by weight
bearing distance in a varus knee. It does not address a symptom provoked by
deep flexion. 4.18.3 covers varus disease.

A patellofemoral procedure addresses flexion loading and does not address
walking distance.

A meniscal procedure addresses a mechanical event and does not address diffuse
load-related pain.

Matching the provoking activity to the operation's mechanism is one of the
most reliable ways to predict whether a patient will be satisfied, and it is
available from the history alone.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting that everything hurts without pursuing what
hurts most and first. Even in a diffusely painful knee there is usually a
hierarchy.

The second is missing avoidance. A patient who no longer uses stairs will
report that stairs do not hurt, and the question has to be whether they use
them.

The third is attributing an activity-provoked symptom to a structure that
activity does not load, which is where the Section 3 mechanics are a
protection rather than an ornament.

And in an obese patient every weight bearing activity is provocative and the
pattern compresses, which makes the non-weight bearing questions more
informative. 4.23.4 covers the assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Ask whether the provoking loading is loading the operation would change.
- An osteotomy addresses walking distance in a varus knee, not deep flexion pain.
- Matching provoking activity to operative mechanism predicts satisfaction from the history alone.
- A patient who no longer uses stairs will say stairs do not hurt.
- In an obese patient the weight bearing pattern compresses, so ask the non-weight bearing questions.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

After surgery the provoking activities frequently change, and a change in the
pattern is more informative than the pattern itself.

A reconstruction that has relieved instability but produced new
flexion-provoked anterior pain has usually produced a patellofemoral or
harvest site problem rather than a graft problem. 4.20.1 covers the
assessment.

An arthroplasty painful on stair descent with a stable knee raises the
patellofemoral articulation, the extensor mechanism or component position
rather than loosening.

An osteotomy that relieved walking pain and introduced lateral pain has
transferred load, which is the intended mechanism behaving excessively rather
than a complication of a different kind. 4.20.4 covers osteotomy assessment.

The discipline is to establish the pre-operative provoking pattern before
interpreting the post-operative one, and that is frequently not recorded.

No figure here supports these patterns; they are standard teaching, under
decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- After surgery a change in the provoking pattern is more informative than the pattern.
- New flexion-provoked anterior pain after reconstruction is usually patellofemoral or harvest site.
- New lateral pain after an osteotomy is transferred load behaving excessively.
- Establish the pre-operative pattern first, which is frequently not recorded.
