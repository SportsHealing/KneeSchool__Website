# Single Leg Squat

The simplest functional test, which exposes control the couch examination cannot
see.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The single leg squat tests the knee under load on one limb, so it measures
quadriceps strength, hip control and the knee's tolerance of flexion at the same
time. 4.4.10 covers functional movement screening.

The examination is looking at the quality of the movement rather than at whether
the patient can do it. 4.3.9 covers muscle wasting.

It matters because a knee that examines normally lying down can lose control as
soon as it carries body weight. 4.7.9 covers the functional range.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the patient to stand on one leg, with the other foot clear of the ground and
the arms free. 4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

Ask them to squat as far as they comfortably can and return, watching from the
front and then from the side.

Watch the knee in the coronal plane, specifically for it moving inwards relative
to the foot. 3.12.7 covers dynamic valgus.

Watch the pelvis, since a pelvis dropping on the unsupported side indicates hip
abductor weakness rather than a knee problem. 4.4.5 covers the Trendelenburg
gait.

Watch the trunk, because leaning over the stance leg compensates for both.

Note the depth reached and whether pain or apprehension stops it. 4.7.6 covers
the painful arc.

Compare both sides, since the test is interpreted by comparison. 4.1.4 covers
the structured approach.

### What a Positive Means

A controlled squat with the knee tracking over the foot, a level pelvis and an
upright trunk, to a similar depth on both sides, is normal.

The knee moving inwards during the descent indicates poor dynamic control, which
is a modifiable finding rather than a structural one. 3.12.7 covers dynamic
valgus.

A pelvis that drops indicates hip abductor weakness, and the knee is the
downstream site rather than the source. 4.4.5 covers the Trendelenburg gait.

A reduced depth with pain at a particular angle localises the symptom to the
arc rather than to the squat. 4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

A knee that gives way during the test raises instability rather than weakness.
4.2.6 covers giving way.

### Key Learning Points

- The test measures movement quality rather than ability.
- Knee, pelvis and trunk are watched separately.
- Inward knee movement is a modifiable control finding.
- A dropping pelvis makes the hip the source and the knee the site.
- The test is interpreted by comparing the two sides.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the functional assessment candidates skip, and including it
distinguishes a complete answer.

The expected points are the setup, the view from the front and the side, the
coronal knee movement, the pelvis, the trunk, the depth, the pain and the
comparison.

Say that you would watch the pelvis and the trunk as well as the knee, and give
the reason. 4.4.5 covers the Trendelenburg gait.

Say that you would record the depth and what limited it. 4.7.9 covers the
functional range.

Say that this test informs rehabilitation rather than diagnosis. 4.2.32 covers
previous physiotherapy.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that single limb loading requires the hip, the knee and
the trunk to work together, so a failure anywhere shows at the knee. 3.14.7
covers jumping.

Dynamic valgus during loading increases the load on the anterior cruciate and
the patellofemoral joint, which links this test to both. 3.7.12 covers loading
during landing.

Quadriceps weakness reduces the depth achievable and alters the strategy, which
is why the strategy is observed alongside the depth. 4.3.9 covers muscle
wasting.

Hip abductor weakness permits the femur to adduct and rotate, which produces the
knee's inward movement without any knee pathology. 4.4.5 covers the
Trendelenburg gait.

### How Good It Is

The test is reproducible for gross abnormalities of control and subjective for
subtle ones.

Its best contribution is exposing a control problem that no static test shows,
which is the reason it is included. 4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

It is not diagnostic for any structure, since the same abnormal pattern follows
from several causes.

No imaging corresponds to this test, so it sits alongside the structural
assessment rather than competing with it. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Single limb loading needs hip, knee and trunk together.
- Dynamic valgus loads the cruciate and the patellofemoral joint.
- Quadriceps weakness changes the strategy as well as the depth.
- Hip abductor weakness produces inward knee movement with a normal knee.
- No static test shows what this one shows.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the test contributes to rehabilitation planning and to return
to sport judgements rather than to diagnosis.

Poor control with a structurally intact knee is a rehabilitation target, and the
test identifies it. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

Poor control after a reconstruction indicates the rehabilitation is incomplete
whatever the laxity examination shows. 4.20.1 covers the reconstruction
assessment.

Dynamic valgus in an uninjured athlete is a risk pattern rather than a
diagnosis, and the distinction matters when advising. 3.7.14 covers injury
mechanisms.

Pain reproduced at a specific depth localises a patellofemoral or a compartment
problem, and the test is then diagnostic by provocation. 4.15.9 covers
patellofemoral pain.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is watching only the knee, so a hip or a trunk cause is
attributed to it. 4.4.5 covers the Trendelenburg gait.

A fit patient compensates well enough to look normal, so the test is insensitive
in the strong. 4.23.2 covers the elite athlete.

The test is performed once, and fatigue is what exposes the control problem in
many patients. 4.21.2 covers the single leg hop.

Pain stops the test and is recorded as poor control.

The comparison side is assumed normal, which it often is not after a bilateral
problem. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

### Key Learning Points

- Watching only the knee attributes hip and trunk causes to it.
- A strong patient compensates and the test is insensitive in them.
- One repetition does not expose a control problem fatigue would reveal.
- Pain stopping the test is recorded as poor control.
- The comparison side is not necessarily normal.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the single leg squat is a screening observation whose
interpretation is less standardised than its popularity suggests.

Scoring is by observation, so the same squat is graded differently by different
examiners and across time. 4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

The movement pattern is modifiable within a single session by coaching, which
means the finding measures current strategy rather than capacity. 4.2.32 covers
previous physiotherapy.

Dynamic valgus is associated with injury at a population level and does not
predict it in an individual, which is the limit of what the test licenses.
3.7.14 covers injury mechanisms.

Pain and control are confounded, since a painful knee alters its strategy and
the altered strategy is then recorded as poor control. 4.7.6 covers the painful
arc.

A single plane observation misses the rotational component, which is part of the
pattern that matters. 4.19.7 covers torsional assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Observational scoring varies between examiners and over time.
- The pattern is coachable within a session, so it measures strategy not capacity.
- Dynamic valgus predicts at a population level and not in an individual.
- Pain and control are confounded in the recorded result.
- Single plane observation misses the rotational component.
