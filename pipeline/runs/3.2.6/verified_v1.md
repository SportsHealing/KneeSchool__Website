# Valgus Motion

Opening the inner side of the joint. The mirror of varus, with a different
primary restraint and a very different natural history.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Valgus motion is coronal angulation that opens the medial side of the joint
and closes the lateral side.

As with varus, a distinction holds throughout: valgus alignment is where the
limb sits, valgus motion is how far the joint opens under a force.

Most knees have a few degrees of valgus alignment normally, which is why the
word describes a normal state as well as an abnormal one.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives physiological valgus alignment as about 5 to 7
degrees, with women on average slightly higher than men.

The superficial medial collateral ligament is the primary restraint to valgus
motion, with the deep collateral, the posteromedial corner and the cruciates
secondary. 2.9.1 covers it.

Testing at 20 to 30 degrees of flexion isolates the collateral ligament,
because the secondary restraints contribute less there.

Opening in full extension means the posteromedial corner has gone as well,
which 2.9.4 covers.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, deciding which restraints are loaded.

Alignment, with a valgus limb loading the lateral compartment and the medial
soft tissues.

Injury, with the medial collateral the most commonly injured ligament at the
knee.

Healing. Unlike the cruciates, this ligament usually heals, so medial laxity
often resolves without surgery. 2.9.1 covers why.

### Clinical Relevance

Valgus laxity at 20 to 30 degrees alone suggests the medial collateral
ligament. Laxity in full extension as well suggests the posteromedial corner
and a cruciate. 4.12 covers the examination.

Because the ligament heals, the question in a medial injury is usually the
position it heals in rather than whether it heals.

Valgus alignment increases patellofemoral load as well as lateral compartment
load, which is why it appears in two different clinical conversations.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus motion opens the medial side; valgus alignment is where the limb sits.
- Conventional teaching gives physiological valgus alignment as about 5 to 7 degrees.
- The superficial medial collateral ligament is the primary restraint.
- Testing at 20 to 30 degrees isolates it; opening in extension means the corner has gone too.
- The ligament usually heals, so the question is the position it heals in.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The medial and lateral sides are not mirror images, despite the symmetry of
the motions.

The medial side heals and the lateral side does less well, because of the
vascularity difference 2.10.1 describes. That asymmetry decides treatment more
than the mechanics do.

The medial side also has a dynamic component through semimembranosus and the
pes, which 2.9.6 covers, and the lateral has one through biceps femoris.

### At the Knee

The superficial collateral carries the large majority of valgus restraint at
25 degrees of flexion, with the posteromedial corner taking an increasing
share towards extension. 3.9.1 carries the apportionment.

Conventional teaching grades medial injury by the opening at 30 degrees: a few
millimetres for grade one and two, and a complete loss of endpoint for grade
three.

The anterior cruciate is a secondary restraint to valgus, which is why a
complete medial injury and a cruciate injury often occur together.

The distal and proximal divisions of the collateral restrain rotation as well
as valgus, so a medial injury can present rotationally. 2.9.1 covers it.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle and injury pattern, as throughout.

Healing, which changes the finding over weeks in a way no other knee ligament
does.

Bracing, which changes the position the ligament heals in and is therefore a
mechanical intervention rather than a comfort measure.

### How It Is Measured

Valgus stress testing at 0 and 30 degrees, graded by opening and endpoint.

Stress radiography gives a number and is used where the grade matters, for
example before a cruciate reconstruction.

Serial examination matters here more than elsewhere, because the finding
changes as the ligament heals.

### Clinical Relevance

Test at both angles and record both, since the pair separates the collateral
from the corner.

Re-examine a medial injury after a few weeks, because the ligament heals and
the early finding is not the final one.

Examine the anterior cruciate in any complete medial injury.

Treat bracing as a mechanical intervention aimed at healing position, and
explain it that way.

### Key Learning Points

- The two sides are not mirror images: the medial heals and the lateral does less well.
- The superficial collateral carries the large majority of valgus restraint at 25 degrees.
- A complete medial injury and a cruciate injury often occur together.
- The finding changes over weeks as the ligament heals, so serial examination matters.
- Bracing is a mechanical intervention aimed at healing position.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat medial laxity as a question about length rather than continuity. The
tissue is usually present and usually healed; what matters is whether it
healed short enough to restrain.

The posteromedial corner carries the extension restraint and the collateral
the flexion restraint, a reciprocal pair like the cruciate bundles. 2.9.4
covers it.

Valgus alignment loads the lateral compartment and the patellofemoral joint
together, so a valgus knee presents in two ways.

### What Changes It

Time, uniquely among the ligament injuries at this joint. A medial injury
reassessed at six weeks is a different problem from the same injury on the
day.

Alignment, which decides whether a reconstruction is loaded or protected.

Associated cruciate injury, which changes both the mechanics and the
rehabilitation that the medial side needs to heal in a good position.

### How It Is Measured

Stress radiography is the quantitative method and the one that settles a grade
three from a grade two when the decision matters.

Examination under anaesthetic removes guarding and is the measurement that
most closely reflects the structural state.

Sectioning study figures describe the acute cut ligament and not the
chronically lengthened one, which is the state most patients present in.

### Clinical Relevance

Delay the decision in an isolated medial injury where the anatomy says healing
is likely, and re-examine rather than operating on the first finding.

Assess alignment before reconstructing chronic medial laxity, since a valgus
limb protects a medial reconstruction and a varus one does not.

Consider shortening rather than only reattaching in a chronic injury, because
the problem is length.

Avoid reading continuity on imaging as competence.

### Key Learning Points

- Medial laxity is a question about length, not continuity.
- The corner restrains in extension and the collateral in flexion, a reciprocal pair.
- Time changes this injury in a way it changes no other knee ligament.
- Sectioning figures describe an acutely cut ligament, not a chronically lengthened one.
- Continuity on imaging is not competence.
