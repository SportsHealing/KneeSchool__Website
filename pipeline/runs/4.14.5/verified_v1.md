# Combined Instability Patterns

Four corners, two cruciates, and the handful of combinations that actually turn
up in a clinic.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A combined instability pattern is a description of which movements a knee
cannot resist, rather than a list of which structures are torn.

The distinction matters because the operation is planned from the movements. An
imaging report names structures; the examination says which laxity the patient
has. 4.1.6 covers that division.

The patterns worth knowing are few. Straight anterior, straight posterior,
anterolateral rotatory, anteromedial rotatory, posterolateral rotatory, and the
multiligament knee where several coexist. 4.11.7 covers the last.

### How It Is Performed

Examine in a fixed order, so the pattern emerges rather than being assembled
from remembered findings. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Translation first: the Lachman test and the posterior drawer with the tibia
reduced. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

Then the collaterals: valgus and varus at thirty degrees and in full extension.
4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Then rotation: the dial test at thirty and ninety degrees, the pivot shift, and
the anterior drawer in external rotation. 4.13.5 covers the dial test.

Then the whole limb: alignment standing, gait, and the nerve and circulation in
any knee with multiple findings. 4.11.5 covers dynamic assessment.

Record the pattern as a sentence rather than a list of positive tests, because
that is what the next clinician needs. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

Increased anterior translation with a large pivot shift and no collateral or
rotational abnormality elsewhere is anterolateral rotatory instability.
4.14.3 covers it.

Increased anterior translation with valgus opening in extension and an increased
anteromedial drawer is anteromedial rotatory instability. 4.14.4 covers it.

A posterior sag with an increased dial difference at thirty and ninety degrees
is posterolateral rotatory instability with posterior cruciate deficiency.
4.13.8 covers it.

Abnormal findings in three or more planes, with a high energy mechanism, is a
multiligament knee until proven otherwise, and the vascular and nerve
examination comes first. 4.11.7 covers the sequence.

### Key Learning Points

- A pattern describes movements the knee cannot resist, not structures that are torn.
- Imaging names structures and the examination says which laxity the patient has.
- Examine in a fixed order so the pattern emerges rather than being assembled from memory.
- Abnormal findings in three or more planes after a high energy injury is a multiligament knee.
- Record the pattern as a sentence rather than as a list of positive tests.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the synthesis station, and the examiner is testing whether the
findings become a diagnosis or stay a list.

The expected sequence is translation, collaterals at two angles, rotation at two
angles, then the limb, with the nerve and circulation first in any knee that
could have been dislocated.

Say the pattern aloud at the end, because that is the answer, and a candidate
who recites positive tests has not answered.

Say that you would examine the other knee completely, since every one of these
findings is a comparison. 4.1.2 covers the principle.

Say that examination under anaesthesia is where the pattern is established in an
acute high energy knee. 4.14.6 covers it.

Say that stress radiographs convert the grades into numbers where a plan turns
on one.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that each structure has a plane and a flexion angle in
which it dominates, so a combination of deficiencies produces a reproducible
pattern rather than an arbitrary set of findings. 3.9.9 covers the medial
angles.

Secondary restraints matter as much as primary ones. Each structure is a
secondary restraint for several movements, so a combined injury produces more
laxity than the sum of the single injuries. 3.10.8 covers that argument.

That is the mechanical reason a cruciate reconstruction into an uncontrolled
corner stretches, and the reason the pattern rather than the structure list
determines the operation. 3.10.9 covers the combination.

A pattern can also be produced by a single structure plus a bony factor, such
as a posterior tibial slope or a varus alignment, which is why the limb is part
of the examination. 4.11.5 covers it.

### How Good It Is

The examination establishes patterns well and grades components poorly, and
every page in this section has reached the same conclusion.

Patterns are what the operation needs, so that is the right way round, and it is
the argument for teaching the examination as a sequence rather than as a set of
named tests.

The acute high energy knee is where the assessment fails, and it fails towards
false reassurance because guarding reduces every finding. 4.13.4 covers the
corner assessment that suffers most.

Imaging is the better description of the structures and the worse guide to the
operation, so the two are read together. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Each structure dominates in a plane and an angle, so combinations produce reproducible patterns.
- Each structure is a secondary restraint for several movements, so combined laxity exceeds the sum.
- That is why a cruciate reconstruction into an uncontrolled corner stretches.
- A pattern can be produced by one structure plus a bony factor, which is why the limb is examined.
- The assessment fails in the acute high energy knee, and it fails towards false reassurance.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the pattern is the operation. Which structures are
reconstructed, in what order, whether the knee is staged, and whether a bony
correction comes first all follow from it.

The patterns that change an operation most are the two that involve a corner,
because a corner left untreated stretches whatever else is done. 4.13.8 covers
the lateral combinations.

The pattern that most often goes unrecognised is anterolateral rotatory
instability over a competent graft, because the Lachman test is normal and the
examination looks reassuring. 4.14.3 covers it.

The pattern most often over treated is a borderline corner finding in a knee
with a cruciate injury, where an unnecessary lateral reconstruction produces
stiffness. 4.2.4 covers stiffness.

Alignment and slope are part of the pattern rather than a separate
consideration, and in a revision they are often the main finding. 4.10.12
covers revision assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording tests rather than a pattern, which leaves the
next clinician without the reasoning.

The second is examining in a different order each time, so findings are missed
rather than contradicted.

The third is accepting a pattern established on a guarding patient.

The fourth is building the pattern from imaging, which names structures and
cannot grade laxity.

The fifth is omitting the limb, where alignment and slope explain a pattern the
soft tissues do not.

### Key Learning Points

- The pattern is the operation: what is reconstructed, in what order, and whether it is staged.
- The patterns involving a corner change the operation most.
- Anterolateral rotatory instability over a competent graft is the pattern most often missed.
- A borderline corner finding is the pattern most often over treated.
- Alignment and slope are part of the pattern, not a separate consideration.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the pattern is usually being reconstructed retrospectively
from a failed operation, and the examination is better evidence than the
operation note.

A stretched posterior cruciate graft with a dial difference at both angles is a
posterolateral pattern that was present and unrecorded, and the revision
addresses both. 4.13.5 covers the test.

A knee with two sound reconstructions and a persisting complaint usually has a
bony factor: a posterior slope favouring posterior translation, or a varus limb
loading a lateral reconstruction. Neither is a soft tissue problem and neither
is fixed by revising soft tissue.

A pattern that does not fit any of the recognised combinations should raise the
question of generalised hypermobility or a connective tissue disorder, where
the knee is one joint of several and the operation is a different conversation.
4.23.3 covers those patients.

In a multiply operated knee the pattern is constrained by previous surgery, so
the examination under reads some planes and over reads others, and the
operation notes are needed to interpret it. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

The commonest reason a pattern cannot be established at all is an incompletely
recovered common peroneal nerve palsy, which changes the gait and the dynamic
examination permanently and is attributed to laxity. 4.23.6 covers complex
injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- The examination is better evidence of the original pattern than the operation note.
- Two sound reconstructions with a persisting complaint usually means a bony factor.
- A pattern that fits no recognised combination raises generalised hypermobility.
- Previous surgery constrains the pattern, so the operation notes are needed to interpret it.
- An unrecovered nerve palsy changes the dynamic examination permanently and gets blamed on laxity.
