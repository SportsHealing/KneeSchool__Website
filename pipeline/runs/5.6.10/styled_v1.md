# Ramp Lesions

A tear at the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction, which sits outside the
usual search area and accompanies cruciate injury.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A ramp lesion is a tear or separation at the junction between the posterior horn
of the medial meniscus and the capsule. 2.5.1 covers its gross anatomy.

It is strongly associated with anterior cruciate injury, and it is frequently
present when the cruciate tear is the reported finding. 5.7.4 covers the acute
cruciate injury.

It matters because it is at the edge of the field of view, at the edge of the
meniscus, and at the edge of what a standard report covers. 5.20.2 covers
reporting meniscal tears.

### How It Is Acquired

Look at the posteromedial corner specifically, on the coronal and sagittal
images through the posterior horn. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Look for fluid between the meniscus and the capsule, which is the separation
rather than a tear through the meniscal substance. 5.2.16 covers fat
suppression.

Look for irregularity of the posterior meniscocapsular outline rather than for a
line within the meniscus. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Look for the associated findings: posteromedial tibial marrow signal and
disruption of the meniscotibial attachment. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

Assess the cruciate, because the association runs both ways and a ramp lesion
should prompt a careful cruciate review. 5.7.3 covers complete cruciate tears.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Fluid signal in the plane between the posterior horn and the capsule, separating
two structures that should be continuous.

An irregular or indistinct posteromedial meniscocapsular outline, rather than a
discrete line within the meniscus. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Marrow signal in the posteromedial tibia, which is a useful secondary sign in a
cruciate injured knee. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

A meniscus that is otherwise normal, because this lesion does not need a
degenerate meniscus. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

### Key Learning Points

- A ramp lesion is at the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction.
- It is strongly associated with anterior cruciate injury.
- It is a separation rather than a tear through the meniscal substance.
- Posteromedial tibial marrow signal is a useful secondary sign.
- The meniscus is often otherwise normal.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the lesion to name alongside a cruciate tear, and the expected
answer says where it is and why it is missed.

The expected points are the location, the fluid in the meniscocapsular plane,
the posteromedial marrow signal, the cruciate, and why the lesion is missed.

Say that you would look at the posteromedial corner specifically in any cruciate
injured knee. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Say that the lesion is a separation at a junction rather than a tear within the
meniscus. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Say that a negative report does not exclude it, because detection is poor.
5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the posteromedial meniscocapsular attachment
restrains anterior tibial translation, so it is loaded by the same event that
tears the cruciate. 3.6.9 covers root biomechanics.

Separating it adds to the rotational laxity, which is why an unaddressed ramp
lesion is implicated in residual instability after reconstruction. 5.7.11 covers
graft failure.

Its position at the capsular margin means it is in vascular tissue and can heal
or be repaired. 2.5.3 covers the vascular supply.

The posteromedial marrow signal reflects the impaction that accompanied the
same event. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

### How Good It Is

This is the meniscal lesion magnetic resonance imaging detects least reliably,
and the gap between imaging and arthroscopic detection is the largest in the
chapter.

Its best contribution is the secondary signs, because the posteromedial marrow
signal and the fluid plane raise it when the lesion itself is not visible.
5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

It is limited by position, by slice thickness, and by the lesion being a
separation rather than a signal line. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028,
and the comparison is the claim being made. The gap between imaging and
arthroscopic detection is the claim, and it is a comparison rather than a
number.

### Key Learning Points

- The attachment restrains anterior translation and is loaded with the cruciate.
- Separation adds rotational laxity and is implicated in residual instability.
- Its capsular position means it can heal or be repaired.
- Posteromedial marrow signal reflects the same impaction event.
- This is the meniscal lesion imaging detects least reliably.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a ramp lesion changes what happens at a cruciate
reconstruction, which makes a pre-operative suspicion worth having.

A cruciate injured knee with posteromedial signs is inspected posteromedially at
arthroscopy, and that inspection is a decision made before the operation. 5.7.4
covers the acute cruciate injury.

An unaddressed ramp lesion is one of the explanations offered for residual
rotational laxity after reconstruction, which makes it a graft failure question
as well as a meniscal one. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

A negative scan does not remove the need to look, which is the practical
consequence of poor detection. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

The lesion is repairable, so finding it has an answer rather than only a
prognosis. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting a normal medial meniscus report in a cruciate
injured knee as excluding a ramp lesion. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

The posteromedial corner is outside the area the eye sweeps when assessing the
meniscus. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

The lesion is looked for as a signal line within the meniscus rather than as a
separation at its margin. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

Posteromedial marrow signal is reported as a bone bruise without the
meniscocapsular question being asked. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

Thick slices average the junction away. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

### Key Learning Points

- A normal meniscus report does not exclude a ramp lesion.
- The posteromedial corner is outside the usual visual sweep.
- Looking for a signal line rather than a separation misses it.
- Marrow signal reported as a bruise leaves the question unasked.
- Thick slices average the junction away.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the ramp lesion is the clearest case in this section of
imaging being known to under-report a lesion that matters.

The imaging and arthroscopic detection rates differ enough that a tertiary
practice inspects the corner regardless of the report, which is a rational
response to a known limitation. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

The lesion's definition is not uniform, since authors include different
combinations of meniscocapsular and meniscotibial separation, so comparing
reported detection across studies compares different lesions. 5.21.1 covers the
meniscal imaging literature.

Whether an asymptomatic ramp lesion needs repair is open, and the imaging cannot
distinguish one that will matter from one that will not. 5.6.13 covers meniscal
repair imaging.

Secondary signs raise suspicion without confirming it, so the pre-operative
position is usually a probability rather than a diagnosis. 5.11.7 covers bone
marrow lesions.

Improving detection here is a protocol and reporting problem together, which
makes it the clearest argument in the section for stating the clinical question
on the request. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Imaging and arthroscopic detection differ enough to justify inspecting regardless.
- The lesion's definition is not uniform across the literature.
- Whether an asymptomatic ramp lesion needs repair is open.
- Secondary signs give a probability rather than a diagnosis.
- Improving detection is a protocol and a reporting problem together.
