# Rotational Stability

The function patients care about and instruments measure worst. The cruciate
shares it, which is why restoring it is harder than restoring translation.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Stopping a joint rotating is a different job from stopping it sliding. A
structure can be good at one and moderate at the other.

Rotation is resisted by structures placed away from the axis of rotation,
because they have a longer lever arm against the turn.

A central structure resists rotation less well than a peripheral one, for the
same strength.

### At the Knee

The anterior cruciate sits centrally, so it is better at resisting translation
than rotation. It contributes to rotational control as a secondary restraint.
3.7.2 covers primary restraint.

The peripheral structures resist rotation better. The anterolateral complex
and the collaterals have the lever arm the cruciate lacks. 3.11 covers the
anterolateral complex.

Rotation and anterior translation are coupled, so loading one loads the other.
That is why the cruciate is involved at all. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

Conventional teaching places the cruciate's rotational contribution highest
near extension, where the posterolateral bundle is tight. 3.7.6 covers it.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, since the structures tighten at different angles.

Whether the peripheral structures are intact, as they carry most of it.

Rotation direction, since internal and external rotation load different
structures.

Muscle activity, which resists rotation dynamically.

### Clinical Relevance

Patients describe rotational failure, which is why a rotational test reflects
their complaint better than a translation test. Section 4 covers the tests.

Restoring rotation is harder than restoring translation, because the cruciate
was never the main rotational restraint.

A high grade rotational finding suggests injury beyond the cruciate alone.
3.7.9 covers the pivot shift.

This is the mechanical reason some reconstructions add a lateral procedure.
3.11 covers them.

### Key Learning Points

- Resisting rotation and resisting translation are different jobs.
- A central structure resists rotation less well than a peripheral one.
- The anterior cruciate is a secondary restraint to rotation.
- Rotation and anterior translation are coupled, which is why the cruciate is involved.
- Restoring rotation is harder than restoring translation.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Rotatory laxity is a combined motion and not a single degree of freedom, which
is why it is measured badly. The pivot shift is a coupled event involving
translation and rotation together. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

Anterolateral rotatory instability is the conventional name for the pattern,
and it describes a behaviour rather than a structure.

A single structure's contribution to a combined motion is harder to apportion
than its contribution to a pure one, which is why these figures are less
settled.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the anterior cruciate the primary role in
restraining the coupled anterior translation of the lateral compartment, with
the anterolateral structures as important secondary restraints.

The lateral meniscus posterior horn contributes, which is why a lateral
meniscal tear increases the pivot shift. 3.6.5 covers meniscal load sharing.

Bony morphology contributes. Conventional teaching identifies increased
posterior tibial slope and a shallower or more convex lateral plateau as
increasing rotatory laxity.

The restraint is angle dependent and greatest near extension, which is why
rotational testing is performed there.

### What Changes It

Anterolateral complex integrity, which carries much of the share.

Lateral meniscus posterior horn status.

Slope and lateral plateau geometry, which are fixed and set the demand.

Generalised laxity, which raises rotatory laxity throughout.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing with a simulated pivot shift load measures the coupled motion
and apportions it between structures.

Graded clinical pivot shift is the clinical standard and is an ordinal scale
with modest reliability.

Instrumented pivot shift measurement converts it to a number without an agreed
threshold.

No measurement of rotatory laxity has the reproducibility that arthrometry has
for translation, which is the central practical problem here.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess rotation near extension and record the grade, accepting its modest
reliability.

Look for a lateral meniscal tear when the pivot shift is higher than expected.

Measure slope and plateau morphology where rotatory laxity is prominent.

Treat rotatory laxity as a combined motion rather than a single measurement.

### Key Learning Points

- Rotatory laxity is a combined motion, which is why it is measured badly.
- Anterolateral rotatory instability names a behaviour, not a structure.
- A lateral meniscal tear increases the pivot shift.
- Slope and lateral plateau shape increase rotatory laxity.
- No rotatory measurement matches arthrometry's reproducibility for translation.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat rotational control as the outcome that decides whether a pivoting
athlete is satisfied, and as the one the evidence base measures least well.

That mismatch is why the lateral augmentation literature has moved on
cadaveric and registry evidence rather than on a reliable clinical
measurement. 3.11 covers the constructs.

Conventional teaching now supports lateral extra-articular augmentation in
high grade rotatory laxity, in revision, in young pivoting athletes and in
generalised laxity, which is a set of indications defined by rotational demand
rather than by translation.

### What Changes It

Whether a lateral procedure was added, and which one.

Femoral tunnel position, which determines the graft's rotational contribution.
3.7.6 covers it.

Slope and plateau morphology, now partly modifiable by osteotomy. 3.13 covers
alignment.

Lateral meniscal status, including whether a posterior horn tear was repaired.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares constructs for restored rotational control under a
simulated pivot shift.

Randomised trials report reinjury rates, which is the outcome the mechanism
predicts.

Instrumented pivot shift measures the construct's effect in cohorts, without a
threshold.

Patient reported return to pivoting sport is the clinically meaningful
endpoint.

### Clinical Relevance

Consider lateral augmentation by rotational demand and laxity grade rather
than by translation.

Repair the lateral posterior horn where it is torn, on rotational grounds.

Measure slope in a pivoting athlete, particularly before revision.

Judge the construct by reinjury and return to sport rather than by
arthrometry.

### Key Learning Points

- Rotational control decides satisfaction in a pivoting athlete and is measured least well.
- The augmentation literature moved on cadaveric and registry evidence, not a clinical measure.
- The indications are defined by rotational demand rather than translation.
- Lateral posterior horn repair has a rotational rationale.
- Judge the construct by reinjury and return to sport.
