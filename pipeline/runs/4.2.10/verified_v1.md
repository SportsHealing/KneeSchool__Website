# Severity

The question that measures the patient rather than the knee, and is useful for
exactly that reason.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Asking how bad the pain is tests how much the symptom is costing the patient.
It does not test how much damage there is.

Those two diverge in both directions and routinely. A severely degenerate knee
can be mildly symptomatic and a structurally minor problem can be disabling.

Severity is therefore a measure of impact rather than of pathology, and it is
the measure that determines whether anything needs doing.

### How It Is Performed

Ask what the pain stops them doing, rather than asking for a number out of
ten.

A number is easy to record and hard to interpret, because patients calibrate
differently and the same number means different things in different people.

Ask about specific activities: walking distance, stairs, sleep, work, and
whatever they used to do for enjoyment.

Ask what painkillers they take, how often, and whether they help, since that
is a behavioural measure of severity rather than a reported one.

Ask whether it is getting worse, better or unchanged, because the trajectory
matters more than the level.

### What a Positive Means

Pain that wakes the patient at night is a severity marker and shifts the
differential. 4.2.13 covers it.

Pain that has stopped work or stopped walking is severe regardless of what
number the patient offers.

Pain requiring regular opiates in a knee with modest findings should raise
sensitisation or a cause outside the knee rather than more imaging.

Pain the patient describes as severe but which has not changed any activity is
a different presentation and is worth exploring rather than discounting.

### Key Learning Points

- Severity measures impact on the patient, not damage in the knee.
- Ask what the pain stops them doing rather than for a number out of ten.
- Analgesic use is a behavioural measure of severity and more useful than a score.
- Night pain is a severity marker that also shifts the differential.
- The trajectory matters more than the level.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected framing is that severity determines urgency and threshold, not
diagnosis.

A patient whose function is preserved has time, whatever the imaging shows.

A patient whose function has collapsed needs something done, and what is done
depends on the diagnosis rather than on the severity.

Severity disproportionate to the findings is itself a clinical finding, and
the correct response is to broaden the assessment rather than to repeat the
imaging. 4.2.1 covers the same point for pain generally.

Scored instruments exist to make this comparable between visits and between
patients, and they are the subject of chapter 4.22. 4.22.8 covers their use in
practice.

### How Good It Is

Severity performs badly as a guide to pathology and well as a guide to what
the patient will accept.

A patient with mild symptoms will decline a long rehabilitation and a patient
with severe symptoms will accept one, and that is the decision severity
actually informs.

Function based questions outperform numerical scales, because an activity is a
fact and a number is a judgement.

Comparing severity between patients is unreliable. Comparing it in the same
patient over time is reliable, which is why the trajectory is the useful
measure. 4.1.8 covers the record that makes that comparison possible.

### Key Learning Points

- Severity determines urgency and threshold, not diagnosis.
- Severity disproportionate to the findings calls for a broader assessment, not more imaging.
- Function based questions outperform numerical scales, because an activity is a fact.
- Comparison between patients is unreliable; comparison in the same patient over time is not.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level severity is the main determinant of threshold, and
under-weighting it is the commoner error.

An operation offered to a patient whose symptoms are mild risks a result that
is mechanically successful and subjectively worse, because there was little to
improve and a recovery to endure.

An operation withheld from a patient whose function has collapsed on the
grounds that the imaging is unimpressive ignores the measure that matters.

The most useful single severity question before elective surgery is what the
patient expects to be able to do afterwards, because expectation predicts
satisfaction more reliably than any structural measure. 4.20.8 covers the
arthroplasty version.

### Where It Misleads

Severity misleads when it is taken as a proxy for pathology, which is the
error running through this page.

It also misleads when under-reported. Stoical patients, older patients and
patients who have adapted their lives systematically understate it, and the
remedy is to ask about activities they have abandoned rather than activities
that hurt.

It misleads when over-reported in a context with consequences, which is a
reason to use function and behaviour rather than numbers. 4.23.7 covers
medicolegal assessment.

And a severity that fluctuates widely from day to day with no mechanical
pattern points away from a structural cause.

### Key Learning Points

- Severity is the main determinant of threshold, and under-weighting it is the commoner error.
- Operating on mild symptoms risks a mechanically successful and subjectively worse result.
- Expectation predicts satisfaction more reliably than any structural measure.
- Stoical and adapted patients understate severity; ask what they have abandoned.
- Wide day to day fluctuation with no mechanical pattern points away from structure.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred and revision practice severity is frequently the reason for
referral and the least useful part of the letter, because it has no comparator
attached.

The useful version is severity relative to before the last intervention, and
that comparison usually has to be reconstructed in the consultation because it
was not recorded. 4.1.8 covers the record.

A patient worse after an operation than before it is a different problem from
one who is better but not better enough, and the plans diverge completely.

Severe pain with no mechanical pattern after multiple operations is the
presentation most likely to receive a further operation and least likely to
benefit, and this chapter says so on several pages because it is the commonest
avoidable harm in the subject.

No figure here supports that claim. It is standard teaching and the numbers
were refused under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Severity without a comparator is the least useful part of a referral letter.
- The useful version is severity relative to before the last intervention.
- Worse than before an operation and better but not enough are different problems.
- Severe pain with no mechanical pattern after multiple operations is the commonest avoidable harm here.
