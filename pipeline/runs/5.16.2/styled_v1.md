# Tibial Plateau Fractures

The injury computed tomography was adopted for, and the ligament and meniscal
damage it leaves unexamined.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A tibial plateau fracture is a break involving the articular surface of the
proximal tibia. 2.2.2 covers the tibial plateau.

Computed tomography shows which parts of the plateau are involved, how far the
surface is depressed, and where each fragment sits. 5.2.22 covers the CT
acquisition.

It matters because the approach and the fixation follow the fragment pattern,
which a radiograph understates. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

### How It Is Acquired

Start from the radiographs, which establish the injury and the alignment. 5.3.11
covers tibial plateau fractures.

Acquire thin slices through the whole plateau with coronal and sagittal
reformats. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

Assess the medial and the lateral surface separately, and the posterior part of
each. 2.2.1 covers the proximal tibia.

Assess the articular depression against the adjacent intact surface. 2.2.2
covers the tibial plateau.

Assess the metaphyseal extension and the fibular head. 2.2.4 covers Gerdy's
tubercle.

### What Normal Looks Like

A continuous articular surface across both plateaus. 2.2.2 covers the tibial
plateau.

An intact posterior cortex on the sagittal reformats. 2.2.1 covers the proximal
tibia.

No metaphyseal fracture line running down from the surface. 5.3.11 covers tibial
plateau fractures.

An intact tibial spine with its attachments. 2.2.3 covers the tibial spine.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Depression of one articular surface relative to the other. 2.2.2 covers the
tibial plateau.

A split fragment with its own displacement and its own cortical margins. 5.3.11
covers tibial plateau fractures.

A posterior fragment, medial or lateral, which the anteroposterior radiograph
does not show. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

Metaphyseal comminution separating the surface from the shaft. 2.2.1 covers the
proximal tibia.

A Segond fragment or a fibular head injury pointing at the lateral structures.
5.3.15 covers Segond fractures.

### Key Learning Points

- The approach follows the fragment pattern.
- Each surface and its posterior part are assessed separately.
- Depression is measured against the adjacent intact surface.
- A posterior fragment hides on the anteroposterior view.
- The fibular head and Gerdy's tubercle belong in the read.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer describes the fragments before naming a
classification.

The expected points are the radiographs, the thin slices and reformats, each
surface separately, the posterior fragments, and the metaphyseal extension.

Say that the classification follows the description rather than replacing it.
5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Say that the soft tissue injuries need a separate study. 5.5.1 covers the
standard protocol.

Say that the fibular head and the lateral structures are part of the injury.
5.3.15 covers Segond fractures.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the plateau is several regions loaded
differently rather than one surface, so a fracture's region predicts both the
approach and the load the repair will carry. 3.5.1 covers the contact areas.

A posterior fragment cannot be reduced or held through an anterior approach,
which is why its detection changed practice. 2.2.1 covers the proximal tibia.

Articular depression left unreduced alters contact pressure over a small area.
3.5.2 covers the contact pressures.

Metaphyseal comminution decides whether the fixation has to bridge rather than
compress. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

### How Good It Is

Computed tomography is the best available test of this fracture's architecture
and routinely leaves the knee's other injuries undocumented.

Its best contribution is the fragment map and the posterior involvement. 2.2.1
covers the proximal tibia.

It is limited because these injuries carry meniscal, cruciate and collateral
damage that the scan cannot show and that affects the outcome. 5.5.9 covers the
ligaments.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
depression thresholds or fragment size values appear either: those are published
quantities tied to particular classifications and this environment cannot check
them.

### Key Learning Points

- The plateau is several regions loaded differently.
- A posterior fragment cannot be held from the front.
- Unreduced depression alters contact pressure.
- Comminution decides whether the fixation bridges or compresses.
- The associated soft tissue injuries are left undocumented.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the scan sets the approach, the patient position and the
implant, so its description has to be a surgical one. 5.20.1 covers structured
reporting.

The posterior fragments decide whether a posterior approach is needed at all.
2.2.1 covers the proximal tibia.

The articular surface decides whether the reduction can be achieved indirectly.
2.2.2 covers the tibial plateau.

A magnetic resonance study changes the plan often enough in these injuries to be
worth the delay. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Alignment on the long leg film is what the fixation has to restore, and the scan
does not carry it. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating a complete fracture description as a complete
injury description. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

A meniscus displaced into the fracture is invisible on the scan and blocks the
reduction. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

A root avulsion accompanies these injuries and is not a scan finding. 5.6.9
covers root tears.

Traction or a splint at the time of scanning reduces fragments the surgeon will
meet unsupported. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Thick reformats smooth a depressed surface into a continuous one. 5.2.23 covers
the reconstruction.

### Key Learning Points

- A complete fracture description is not a complete injury description.
- A displaced meniscus blocks the reduction and is invisible here.
- A root avulsion is not a scan finding.
- A splint at scanning changes the fragment position.
- Thick reformats smooth a depressed surface.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level these knees arrive after fixation, with stiffness, pain or
deformity, and the question is what the surface looks like now. 5.12.8 covers
the post-operative imaging.

Metal artefact sits over the articular surface, which is the part the revision
question concerns. 5.2.19 covers the artefacts.

The original scan is the only record of the pre-operative anatomy and often does
not travel with the patient. 5.20.7 covers reporting the post-operative knee.

Post-traumatic arthritis follows the residual incongruity and the alignment
rather than the classification, so the follow-up imaging is radiographic. 5.3.6
covers the long leg alignment films.

Classification agreement between surgeons is a known weakness and is not
improved by a better scan. 5.1.7 covers evidence-based imaging.

The useful report gives the fragments with positions, the surfaces with their
references, the posterior involvement, the metaphyseal extension, and the soft
tissue question stated as open. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Metal artefact sits over the surface the revision concerns.
- The original scan is the only pre-operative record.
- Follow-up for arthritis is radiographic.
- Classification agreement is a weakness a better scan does not fix.
- The report should state the soft tissue question as open.
