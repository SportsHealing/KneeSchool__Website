# Chronic PCL Injury

The ligament heals in continuity, so the appearance records the history and the
examination records the competence.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A torn posterior cruciate often heals into a continuous structure, which is not
what the anterior cruciate does. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

The healed ligament is thickened, shortened and carries internal signal, and it
is continuous. 5.8.2 covers partial PCL tears.

It matters because a continuous ligament reads as an intact one, and the knee it
sits in can still allow posterior translation. 4.11.6 covers chronic PCL
deficiency.

### How It Is Acquired

Read the ligament for thickening and shortening rather than for a defect. 2.8.5
covers the MRI anatomy.

Look for the absence of marrow change and effusion, which separates an old injury
from a recent one. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

Read the medial and patellofemoral compartments, since chronic posterior laxity
alters where load falls. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

Read the corner, because a corner injury that went unrecognised acutely is a
common reason a knee stayed lax. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

Establish the interval since injury from the history, because the appearance
alone does not give it. 4.11.6 covers chronic PCL deficiency.

### What Normal Looks Like

A ligament of normal thickness with no internal signal and a smooth outline.
5.8.1 covers the normal posterior cruciate.

Normal cartilage in the medial and patellofemoral compartments. 5.11.2 covers
the chondral defects.

No marrow change at either attachment. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

An intact corner, which in a chronically lax knee is worth stating explicitly.
5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A thickened, shortened, continuous ligament with internal signal, which is the
healed appearance. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Cartilage loss in the medial compartment, which follows the altered load. 5.11.2
covers the chondral defects.

Patellofemoral cartilage change, which follows the same mechanism from the
extensor side. 5.12.7 covers patellofemoral arthritis.

An unrecognised corner injury, which explains laxity the ligament's appearance
does not. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

A posteriorly positioned tibia with the knee at rest, which is a sign and not a
structure. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

### Key Learning Points

- The posterior cruciate often heals in continuity.
- The healed ligament is thickened, shortened and carries signal.
- A continuous ligament reads as an intact one.
- The knee can still allow posterior translation.
- Cartilage change in the medial and patellofemoral compartments follows laxity.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer separates the appearance of healing from the
presence of function.

The expected points are the healed continuity, the thickening, the absence of
acute change, the compartmental cartilage, and the corner.

Say that this ligament's healing behaviour differs from the anterior cruciate's.
5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

Say that chronic laxity shows on the cartilage before it shows on the ligament.
5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

Say that gait changes follow chronic deficiency and are a clinical finding. 3.8.7
covers gait following PCL injury.

### How Good It Is

This is the page where the chapter's divergence is widest, because the scan can
be reassuring and the knee can be functionally deficient at the same time.

Its best contribution is the secondary evidence: cartilage change in the medial
and patellofemoral compartments is imaging's record of chronic laxity. 5.12.7
covers patellofemoral arthritis.

Its second contribution is the unrecognised corner injury, which the acute study
may have missed and which a chronic study can still show. 5.10.3 covers PLC
injuries.

It is limited because that secondary evidence appears later than the laxity, so
a normal looking chronic study does not exclude a deficient knee. 3.8.2 covers
the posterior stability.

It is limited again because the healed ligament's appearance does not grade the
residual translation, which the examination measures directly. 4.11.2 covers the
posterior drawer test.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
translation distance, grade, cartilage thickness, interval since injury or
ligament dimension appears either: those are published quantities measured
differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The scan can be reassuring while the knee is deficient.
- Compartmental cartilage change is imaging's record of chronic laxity.
- The unrecognised corner injury can still be shown late.
- The secondary evidence appears later than the laxity.
- A normal looking chronic study does not exclude a deficient knee.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the question in a chronic knee is whether the posterior laxity
is causing the damage being seen, and imaging supplies the damage rather than the
laxity.

Medial and patellofemoral cartilage loss in a knee with known posterior laxity
is the argument for reconstructing rather than observing. 5.11.2 covers the
chondral defects.

A corner injury found late changes a single ligament reconstruction into a
multiple ligament one. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

Alignment matters here as it does in the lateral chapter, because varus
alignment loads the structures a reconstruction has to protect. 5.3.6 covers the
long leg alignment films.

The laxity itself is graded clinically, so the report's job is to describe the
consequences and the structures and to leave the grading alone. 4.11.6 covers
chronic PCL deficiency.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting a healed ligament as normal. 5.8.1 covers the
normal posterior cruciate.

A healed thickened ligament is reported as an acute partial injury. 5.8.2 covers
partial PCL tears.

Compartmental cartilage change is reported as primary arthritis without the
laxity being connected to it. 5.12.7 covers patellofemoral arthritis.

The corner is not reassessed on the chronic study, so the reason for the laxity
is never found. 5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

Alignment is not assessed, so a reconstruction is planned into a knee that will
stretch it out. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

### Key Learning Points

- A healed ligament is reported as normal.
- A healed ligament is reported as an acute partial injury.
- Compartmental change is reported as primary arthritis.
- The corner is not reassessed late.
- Alignment is not assessed before a reconstruction is planned.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this page carries the chapter's organising claim in its
strongest form.

The appearance of this ligament records what happened to it and the examination
records what it can now resist, and in a chronic knee those are different facts
that a single report routinely conflates. 3.8.2 covers the posterior stability.

Healing in continuity is the mechanism that makes the conflation possible, and it
is also why this ligament is often managed without surgery, so the same property
explains both the treatment and the reporting error. 4.11.6 covers chronic PCL
deficiency.

The cartilage is the only part of the study that reports function, and it reports
it slowly and irreversibly, which makes a chronic study a record of damage
already done rather than a warning. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

The useful chronic report describes the ligament's healed state, both
compartments' cartilage, the corner, the alignment, and says that the laxity is
not an imaging measurement. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The appearance records history and the examination records competence.
- Healing in continuity makes the conflation possible.
- The same property explains the treatment and the reporting error.
- The cartilage reports function slowly and irreversibly.
- A chronic study records damage done rather than giving a warning.
