# Root Tears

A tear at the meniscus's bony anchor, which disables the whole meniscus and was
missed for years because nobody looked there.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The root is where the meniscus attaches to the tibia, and a root tear detaches
it so the circumferential fibres have nothing to pull against. 2.5.7 covers the
root attachments.

The meniscus can then extrude from the joint under load, which is why a root
tear behaves like a meniscectomy even though the meniscus is intact. 3.6.9
covers root biomechanics.

It matters because a root tear is a small lesion with a large mechanical
consequence, and it is easy to overlook. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

### How It Is Acquired

Look at the root attachments deliberately, on the coronal images where the root
inserts near the centre of the tibia. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Follow the root on consecutive sagittal images, since the posterior medial root
is the commonest site and is small. 2.5.7 covers the root attachments.

Look for the ghost sign, where the root is not visible on a slice it should
occupy. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Measure extrusion on the coronal image, because extrusion is the consequence and
is often more conspicuous than the tear. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Look for subchondral signal change in the same compartment, which accompanies
the sudden loss of load distribution. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Fluid signal interrupting the root at its tibial attachment, or a root that
cannot be seen where it should be.

Extrusion of the meniscal body beyond the tibial margin on the coronal image,
which is the mechanical consequence. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Subchondral marrow signal in the compartment, sometimes with a subchondral
insufficiency fracture, in a knee that has suddenly lost its load distribution.
5.13.4 covers insufficiency fractures.

A knee that is otherwise not very degenerate, because a root tear can occur in a
compartment with reasonable cartilage. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

### Key Learning Points

- The root is the meniscus's bony anchor.
- A root tear leaves the circumferential fibres nothing to pull against.
- The meniscus then extrudes and behaves like a meniscectomy.
- Extrusion is the consequence and is often more conspicuous than the tear.
- Subchondral marrow signal accompanies the sudden loss of protection.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the lesion to name when asked what is easy to miss, and the
expected answer links it to extrusion.

The expected points are the deliberate look at the roots, the coronal and
sagittal assessment, the ghost sign, extrusion, and the subchondral marrow.

Say that the posterior medial root is the commonest site. 2.5.7 covers the root
attachments.

Say that extrusion is the finding to measure. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Say that a root tear behaves mechanically like a meniscectomy. 3.6.9 covers root
biomechanics.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the meniscus resists load by developing
circumferential tension, and tension needs an anchor at both ends. 3.6.2 covers
hoop stress.

Detaching one end converts the meniscus from a load bearing ring into a free
structure that is pushed out of the joint. 3.6.11 covers the effects of root
tears.

The compartment then loads a smaller area at higher pressure, which is the
mechanism of the rapid cartilage loss and the subchondral change. 3.6.5 covers
load sharing.

That is why a root tear can produce a sudden clinical deterioration in a knee
that was previously tolerable. 5.13.4 covers insufficiency fractures.

### How Good It Is

Root tears are detected much better than they used to be, and the improvement
came from looking rather than from better sequences.

Its best contribution is detecting a lesion with a specific operative answer in
a compartment that may still be worth saving. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair
imaging.

It is missed when the roots are not assessed specifically, and the extrusion is
then reported without its cause. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Circumferential tension needs an anchor at both ends.
- Detaching one end makes the meniscus a free structure that is pushed out.
- The compartment loads a smaller area at higher pressure.
- A root tear can produce sudden deterioration in a tolerable knee.
- The improvement in detection came from looking, not from better sequences.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a root tear is one of the few meniscal findings with a clear
and time sensitive operative answer.

A root tear with preserved cartilage and a correctable alignment is a repair
candidate, and the imaging supplies the cartilage and the extrusion. 5.6.13
covers meniscal repair imaging.

The same tear in a worn varus compartment is part of established disease, and
the decision is the compartment's. 5.3.16 covers Kellgren-Lawrence grading.

Alignment is central here, because a varus knee loads a medial root repair and
an osteotomy is often combined. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Subchondral insufficiency fracture with a root tear changes the timetable,
because the bone is failing now. 5.13.4 covers insufficiency fractures.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not looking at the roots. 5.5.1 covers the standard
protocol.

Extrusion is reported as a finding in its own right and the root is not
examined. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

The oblique course of a normal root is read as a tear. 2.5.7 covers the root
attachments.

A root tear in a degenerate compartment is treated as a repairable lesion when
the compartment has gone. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

The alignment is not assessed, so a medial root repair is planned into a varus
limb. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

### Key Learning Points

- Not looking at the roots is the commonest error.
- Extrusion reported without examining the root loses the cause.
- A normal root's oblique course mimics a tear.
- A root tear in a lost compartment is not a repairable lesion.
- A medial root repair planned into a varus limb ignores the load.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the root tear is the chapter's clearest example of a lesion
whose recognition changed practice, and of the limits that remain.

Detection improved because the roots became part of the search, which is a
reporting change rather than a technical one, and it is not uniform between
centres. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The distinction between a degenerate root and a torn root is a continuum, and the
imaging grades it less reliably than the extrusion it causes. 5.6.11 covers
meniscal extrusion.

Repair restores the anchor and does not reliably reverse extrusion, so the
mechanical correction is partial and the imaging after repair reflects that.
5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

Whether repair changes the compartment's long term course is a literature
question, and this page states no figure for it. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal
imaging literature.

The lesion coexists with alignment and cartilage problems that decide the
outcome, so the root tear is the nameable part of a larger mechanical failure.
5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

### Key Learning Points

- Detection improved through a reporting change, which is not uniform between centres.
- A degenerate root and a torn root are a continuum.
- Repair restores the anchor and does not reliably reverse extrusion.
- Whether repair changes the long term course is a literature question.
- The root tear is the nameable part of a larger mechanical failure.
