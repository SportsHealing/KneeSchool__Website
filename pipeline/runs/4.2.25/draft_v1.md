# Rugby

A contact sport where the knee is loaded by other people. Higher energy, more
combined injuries, and a different consent conversation.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A rugby history tests how much external force was involved, because rugby knee
injuries are more often contact injuries than football ones. 3.15.3 covers the
mechanics.

That matters because an external force is not limited by what the patient can
generate, so the energy is higher and combined injuries are commoner.

Asking about position also matters: a front row forward, a back row forward
and a winger have different exposures.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the position and the level, and whether they are paid.

Ask what was happening: a tackle, a ruck, a maul, a scrum, or running in open
play.

Ask whether anybody was on the leg, and where, because a player falling across
a planted leg generates a very large valgus or varus force. 4.2.17 covers
contact mechanisms.

Ask whether the knee went out of shape and came back, which raises a
dislocation.

Ask about numbness or weakness in the foot afterwards, since a peroneal
symptom indicates a serious lateral side injury.

### What a Positive Means

A tackle or a ruck with somebody falling across the outside of the knee is a
medial structure injury, frequently combined with a cruciate. 4.12.7 covers
separating the two.

A force from the inside is a lateral and posterolateral injury, less common
and more serious. 4.13.8 covers combined lateral injuries.

A non-contact turn in open play is a cruciate mechanism like any other.

Any rugby knee injury with multidirectional laxity is a dislocation until
excluded and the vascular assessment comes first. 4.23.6 covers the
assessment.

A peroneal nerve symptom after a rugby injury indicates a high energy lateral
injury whatever the examination initially suggests.

### Key Learning Points

- Rugby knee injuries are more often contact injuries, so the energy is higher.
- External force is not limited by what the patient can generate.
- Somebody falling across the outside of a planted leg is a medial injury, often combined.
- Any multidirectional laxity is a dislocation until excluded.
- A peroneal symptom indicates a high energy lateral injury.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning is from energy to breadth of injury.

Low energy contact produces an isolated collateral injury, which usually
heals. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Higher energy produces a collateral and a cruciate together, which does not.

Higher energy still produces a multiligament injury, and the assessment
sequence becomes vascular, neurological, then ligamentous.

Scrum and ruck injuries can produce an axial load on a flexed knee, which
raises chondral and meniscal injury rather than ligamentous instability.

### How Good It Is

The rugby history performs well for energy, which is reliably recalled, and
poorly for direction, which is not. That pattern recurs throughout this
chapter's contact pages.

Witnesses and video are frequently available at any level above casual, and
asking about them is a legitimate part of the history.

It performs well for predicting combined injury, which is the main clinical
value.

It performs poorly for predicting return, because return in rugby depends on
contact tolerance as well as on knee function and the two are assessed
separately. 4.21.8 covers return to play decisions.

### Key Learning Points

- Reason from energy to breadth: isolated collateral, collateral plus cruciate, then multiligament.
- An isolated collateral injury usually heals; a combined one does not.
- Scrum and ruck axial loading raises chondral and meniscal injury.
- Energy is reliably recalled and direction is not.
- Return in rugby depends on contact tolerance as well as knee function.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the rugby history changes both the assessment breadth and
the consent conversation.

A player intending to return to contact is accepting a reinjury risk that
should be stated, and the history of their intended level is what makes that
conversation concrete.

A combined medial and cruciate injury in a rugby player is a common pattern
and the medial component is the one most often under-treated. 4.12.8 covers
post-reconstruction assessment.

A multiligament injury changes the timing completely: assessed early,
reconstructed in a planned sequence, and with a return that may not happen.

Saying that last part early is kinder than saying it late, and it is a
history-led conversation because it depends on what the player does.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is a cruciate-focused assessment in a contact injury,
which misses the collateral and rotational components that the mechanism
predicts.

The second is accepting the player's account of a tackle direction, which is
unreliable.

The third is under-estimating energy because the player continued, which
happens in rugby more than in most sports.

And an academy or schoolboy player has a growth plate that may fail instead of
the ligament, and the history of a contact injury in a skeletally immature
player should prompt that assessment. 4.19.1 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- A player returning to contact accepts a reinjury risk that should be stated explicitly.
- The medial component of a combined rugby injury is the one most often under-treated.
- A multiligament injury is assessed early, reconstructed in sequence, and may not allow return.
- A cruciate-focused assessment in a contact injury misses what the mechanism predicts.
- Continuing to play does not mean the energy was low.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the rugby player appears with a chronic combined injury,
most often a medial or posterolateral component that was never addressed.

The history of the original mechanism is the most useful record, because the
acute examination was performed on a guarding knee and under-reported the
laxity. 4.1.5 covers the timing problem.

A chronic posterolateral injury presenting with a varus thrust traces back to
a varus or hyperextension contact injury assessed as a cruciate injury alone.
4.13.4 covers the assessment.

A player with recurrent instability after reconstruction, in a contact sport,
raises both an unaddressed rotational component and the simple fact of
repeated high energy exposure, and the two have different answers.

No figure here supports their relative frequency, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- The chronic presentation is usually a medial or posterolateral component never addressed.
- The original mechanism is more useful than the acute examination, which under-reported laxity.
- A varus thrust traces back to a varus or hyperextension injury assessed as cruciate alone.
- Recurrent instability in a contact athlete may be exposure rather than a surgical failure.
