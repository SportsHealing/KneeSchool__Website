# Popliteofibular Ligament Function

A short band from the popliteus tendon to the fibular styloid. It is the
passive half of the popliteus complex and a major external rotation restraint.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A muscle's tendon can be tethered to a nearby bone partway along its course.
That tether carries load whether the muscle is contracting or not.

Tethering a tendon turns part of it into a ligament. The muscle's pull and the
tether's resistance are separate mechanical facts.

A short tether close to a joint resists rotation, as any short structure near
a joint line does.

### At the Knee

The popliteofibular ligament runs from the popliteus tendon to the styloid
process of the fibula. 2.10.3 covers the anatomy.

It is the passive component of the popliteus complex, carrying load without
the muscle contracting. 3.10.4 covers the complex.

Conventional teaching gives it a major share of the restraint to external
rotation of the tibia. 3.10.2 covers external rotation.

It also contributes to varus restraint, more in flexion than in extension,
which is where the lateral collateral has slackened. 3.10.3 covers that
ligament.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes its tension.

Fibular styloid integrity, since that is its anchor.

Popliteus tendon integrity, since that is its other attachment.

Whether the corner is injured as a unit, which is usual. 3.10.6 covers the
corner.

### Clinical Relevance

A fibular styloid avulsion disconnects this ligament, which is why that
fracture matters out of proportion to its size. 6.99 covers avulsion
fractures.

It is a target in anatomic corner reconstruction, and reconstructing the
corner without it leaves rotation lax. 3.10.8 covers the construct.

Its contribution explains why varus laxity in flexion implicates the corner
rather than the lateral collateral. 3.10.1 covers varus stability.

It is not palpable and has no isolated test, so it is assessed through the
corner's findings. Section 4 covers the tests.

### Key Learning Points

- Tethering a tendon to bone turns part of it into a ligament.
- The popliteofibular ligament runs from the popliteus tendon to the fibular styloid.
- It is the passive component of the popliteus complex.
- Conventional teaching gives it a major share of external rotation restraint.
- It contributes to varus restraint more in flexion than in extension.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Its mechanical identity is a short, obliquely oriented passive restraint
anchored to the fibula, which is why it resists external rotation efficiently:
the fibula moves posteriorly as the tibia rotates externally.

Being short, it strains more for a given displacement, which makes it
vulnerable at the same rotation that a longer structure tolerates. 3.7.6
covers that principle.

Its dependence on two separate structures, the popliteus tendon and the
fibular styloid, gives it two routes to failure.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching describes anterior and posterior divisions, with the
posterior division the larger, and gives the ligament a major share of the
external rotation restraint at 30 degrees of flexion.

Sectioning it increases external rotation measurably, and sectioning it after
the lateral collateral increases varus opening in flexion. 3.10.1 covers varus
stability.

Its styloid attachment is immediately adjacent to the biceps femoris and the
lateral collateral attachments, which makes the fibular head a crowded
fixation site. 2.10.7 covers the surgical anatomy.

The peroneal nerve passes around the fibular neck just distal to it, which is
the anatomical reason corner surgery carries a nerve risk. 2.10.6 covers the
nerve.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes its tension.

Styloid and tendon integrity, its two anchors.

Whether the sectioning order in a quoted study put it before or after the
collateral.

Chronicity, since it stretches rather than heals.

### How It Is Measured

Selective sectioning with external rotation torque gives its share, with order
dependence.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates it inconsistently, which is a
recognised limitation. 5.10 covers imaging.

Plain radiography identifies a styloid avulsion, which is the indirect sign of
its disruption.

No clinical test isolates it.

### Clinical Relevance

Look for a fibular styloid avulsion as the sign of its disruption.

Expect inconsistent demonstration on magnetic resonance imaging.

Account for the crowded fibular head when planning fixation.

Respect the peroneal nerve's course just distal to the styloid.

### Key Learning Points

- A short oblique passive restraint anchored to the fibula resists external rotation efficiently.
- Being short, it strains more for a given displacement.
- It has two anchors and therefore two routes to failure.
- Conventional teaching describes anterior and posterior divisions, the posterior larger.
- The peroneal nerve passes around the fibular neck just distal to it.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat this ligament as the component whose reconstruction distinguishes an
anatomic corner procedure from a tenodesis, and treat the fibular head as the
crowded and fragile part of the operation. 3.10.8 covers the construct.

Conventional teaching reconstructs it with a limb passing from the fibular
head to the femur, reproducing its obliquity, because a limb in the wrong
orientation restrains the wrong motion.

A styloid avulsion changes the operation: the attachment has to be reattached
or the reconstruction anchored elsewhere, and bone quality decides which. 6.99
covers avulsion fractures.

### What Changes It

Styloid integrity and fibular head bone quality.

Whether the reconstruction reproduces the native obliquity.

Number of limbs being fixed to the fibular head.

Alignment, which loads the construct. 3.13 covers alignment.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares reconstructions with and without this limb for
restored rotational restraint.

The dial test measures the clinical result without an instrument.

Postoperative imaging confirms the fibular fixation and the limb orientation.

No measurement confirms this limb is carrying the load intended.

### Clinical Relevance

Include this limb in an anatomic corner reconstruction.

Reproduce the native obliquity, since orientation decides which motion is
restrained.

Plan fibular fixation around the crowding and the bone quality.

Reattach or re-anchor where the styloid is avulsed.

### Key Learning Points

- Reconstructing it distinguishes an anatomic corner procedure from a tenodesis.
- A limb in the wrong orientation restrains the wrong motion.
- The fibular head is crowded and fragile.
- A styloid avulsion changes the operation.
- No measurement confirms this limb carries the intended load.
