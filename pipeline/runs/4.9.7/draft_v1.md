# Ege Test

A squat with the feet rotated. The least used meniscal test and the one that
separates medial from lateral most directly.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The Ege test is a weight bearing squat performed with the feet deliberately
rotated, which loads one meniscus preferentially depending on the direction.

Feet turned outwards loads the medial meniscus. Feet turned inwards loads the
lateral.

So the test attempts to do under load what the McMurray test does unloaded:
separate the two menisci by the direction of rotation. 4.9.3 covers the
unloaded version.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the patient to stand with the feet about shoulder width apart and then to
turn both feet outwards, conventionally to around forty-five degrees.

Ask them to squat slowly and then to stand up slowly, noting any pain, click
or catch and where it occurs.

Repeat with the feet turned inwards.

Compare the two directions and ask which reproduced their pain.

Provide support and do not persist if the patient cannot squat safely, as with
the Childress test. 4.9.6 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

Medial joint line pain or a click during the externally rotated squat is a
positive for the medial meniscus.

Lateral joint line pain or a click during the internally rotated squat is a
positive for the lateral meniscus.

A positive in both directions is non-localising and more likely a degenerate
or irritable joint.

Anterior pain in either direction is patellofemoral, because a squat loads the
patellofemoral joint whichever way the feet point. 4.15.9 covers it.

An inability to perform the test is a finding rather than a negative.

### Key Learning Points

- Feet turned outwards loads the medial meniscus; inwards loads the lateral.
- It does under load what the McMurray test does unloaded.
- A positive in both directions is non-localising.
- Anterior pain in either direction is patellofemoral.
- An inability to perform the test is a finding rather than a negative.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected points are the two foot positions, the slow squat and
rise, the site of the symptom, and the comparison between directions.

The candidate should say why the foot rotation changes which meniscus is
loaded, which is that tibial rotation shifts the compressive load between the
compartments.

Say that the squat is slow in both directions, because a fast squat reduces
the time the meniscus is loaded at any given angle.

Say that you would record which direction was positive, because that is the
test's whole contribution.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning is that external tibial rotation with compression loads the
medial compartment and internal rotation loads the lateral, which is the same
logic as the McMurray and Apley tests applied under body weight.

That makes the Ege test a loaded localising test, and localisation is what it
adds.

A direction-specific positive is therefore more informative than a general
positive on any other test in this chapter.

A positive in both directions removes that advantage and tells you the joint
is irritable rather than which compartment is at fault.

### How Good It Is

The Ege test is the least studied of the tests in this chapter and its
reported performance is correspondingly uncertain.

The honest position is that it shares the group's pattern, more specific than
sensitive, and that its particular contribution is compartment localisation
under load.

It has the same availability limitation as the Childress test: a patient who
cannot squat cannot be tested, which excludes much of the degenerate
population.

It is rarely performed in practice, which means most clinicians have little
personal calibration for it, and that is a practical limitation separate from
its accuracy.

### Key Learning Points

- External rotation with compression loads the medial compartment; internal loads the lateral.
- Its contribution is compartment localisation under load.
- A direction-specific positive is more informative than a general positive elsewhere.
- It is the least studied test in this chapter.
- Most clinicians have little personal calibration for it, which is a practical limitation.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the Ege test is useful in one situation: a knee with imaging
findings in both compartments where the question is which is symptomatic.

That question matters for a partial procedure and for an osteotomy, and the
test answers it by load rather than by palpation. 4.20.7 covers partial
replacement assessment.

It is also a functional test, so a patient who can squat in both directions
without symptoms has demonstrated something useful.

It contributes nothing to repairability or urgency, like the other provocative
tests.

Its practical place is as a confirmatory localising test rather than as part
of the routine sequence, and saying that is more honest than listing it as
standard.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating an unfamiliar test as more informative than a
familiar one because it is less often wrong in one's own experience, which is
a sampling artefact.

The second is recording a positive without recording the direction, which
discards the only thing the test adds.

The third is attributing a positive to the meniscus without asking where the
pain was, because the squat loads several structures.

And performing it in a patient who cannot squat safely, which is a fall risk
rather than an examination.

### Key Learning Points

- Useful where imaging shows findings in both compartments and the question is which is symptomatic.
- It answers that by load rather than by palpation.
- Recording a positive without the direction discards the test's only contribution.
- Its honest place is confirmatory rather than routine.
- An unfamiliar test seeming more informative is usually a sampling artefact.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

After surgery this test has the same standing as the other weight bearing
tests: more useful as a functional measure than as a provocative one. 4.9.4
and 4.9.6 reach the same conclusion.

Its localising property retains some value in the knee that has had one
compartment operated on and now has symptoms, because a positive in the
untouched direction points away from the operated compartment.

That is a narrow use and it is the only one this page claims.

After a root repair or a transplant, deep flexion is restricted and the test
is a protocol decision rather than a routine. 4.20.3 covers root repair.

At this level the honest summary of the whole provocative group is that they
were designed to detect a tear, imaging now detects tears better, and what the
examination contributes is attribution and function. These tests were not
designed for that and are used for it anyway.

No figure here supports that summary, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- After surgery it is a functional measure rather than a provocative test.
- A positive in the untouched direction points away from the operated compartment.
- After a root repair or transplant the test is a protocol decision rather than a routine.
- The provocative group was designed to detect tears; imaging now does that better.
- What the examination contributes is attribution and function, which these tests were not designed for.
