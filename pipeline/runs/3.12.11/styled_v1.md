# Effects of Malalignment

What coronal and rotational malalignment do to the patellofemoral joint.
Chapter 3.5 covers the same question for the tibiofemoral joint.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

If a pulley and its rope are not in line, the pulley is pushed sideways every
time the rope is pulled.

The misalignment can be in the plane you can see from the front, or it can be
a twist you cannot see at all.

A twist is the harder problem, because nothing about the leg looks wrong from
the front.

### At the Knee

A knock kneed leg moves the hip and the thigh inwards relative to the knee,
which increases the outward push on the patella. 3.13.4 covers valgus.

A bow legged leg does the opposite and reduces that push. 3.13.3 covers varus.

A thigh bone twisted so the knee points inwards has the same effect as a knock
knee, without looking like one.

A shin bone twisted so the foot points outwards moves the tibial tubercle
outwards, which also increases the push. 3.12.12 covers the tubercle.

### What Changes It

The shape the bones grew into, which is fixed in adults. 2.4.2 covers
ossification.

Previous fracture that healed in a rotated position.

Previous surgery to correct alignment. 7.68 covers the principles.

Position during movement, which can add to the fixed problem. 3.12.7 covers
dynamic valgus.

### Clinical Relevance

Rotation is missed because it is invisible from the front and needs a scan to
measure. 5.12.4 covers the imaging.

Two patients with the same knee x ray can have very different mechanics at the
patella.

It explains why some instability does not improve after a soft tissue
operation. 3.12.5 covers the ligament.

Chapter 3.5 covers what malalignment does to the main weight bearing part of
the knee.

### Key Learning Points

- A pulley out of line with its rope is pushed sideways every time the rope is pulled.
- Valgus increases the outward push on the patella; varus reduces it.
- Femoral internal twist acts like a valgus knee without looking like one.
- Tibial external twist moves the tubercle outwards and increases the push.
- Rotation is invisible from the front and needs imaging to measure.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Malalignment acts on this joint through the direction of the extensor vector,
which has components in the coronal and the transverse plane.

Coronal malalignment is visible and measurable on a long leg film. Transverse
malalignment is neither. 3.13.1 covers the mechanical axis.

The clinical consequence is that the measurable component is routinely
assessed and the under recognised component is not.

### At the Knee

Genu valgum increases the lateral vector on the patella by moving the proximal
line of pull medially relative to the tubercle. 3.13.4 covers it.

Femoral anteversion rotates the trochlear groove internally beneath the
extensor mechanism, which is mechanically equivalent to lateralising the
tubercle.

External tibial torsion lateralises the tubercle directly, which is why the
two rotational abnormalities summate. 3.12.12 covers the tubercle.

Both rotational abnormalities raise the tubercle to groove distance without
any coronal abnormality, which is the common trap.

Severe rotational malalignment can produce instability with a normal trochlea
and an intact ligament, which is the case that confuses the four factor model.
3.12.8 covers it.

### What Changes It

Coronal alignment, which is measured on a long leg standing radiograph. 5.3.2
covers the imaging.

Femoral version, which requires axial imaging through the hip and knee.

Tibial torsion, which requires axial imaging through the knee and ankle.

Previous malunion or osteotomy, which may have corrected one plane and
worsened another. 7.68 covers the principles.

Dynamic contribution, which adds to the fixed geometry during loading. 3.12.7
covers it.

### How It Is Measured

Long leg standing radiographs for coronal alignment, which is the reliable
part. 3.13.1 covers the axis.

Computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging with hip and ankle included
for rotational profile, which is not a routine study.

Tubercle to groove distance, which captures the net transverse effect without
saying where it came from. 3.12.12 covers the index.

Clinical rotational profile testing, which estimates version and torsion
crudely. 4.5.6 covers it.

The net vector is not measured by any single study, which is the recurring
problem in this chapter.

### Clinical Relevance

Measure the rotational profile where instability persists despite normal
coronal alignment and normal morphology. 3.12.8 covers the assessment.

Read a raised tubercle to groove distance as a net result, then look for its
cause rather than correcting it blind. 3.12.12 covers the index.

Expect a soft tissue procedure to underperform where the vector abnormality is
rotational. 7.51 covers the operation.

Distinguish this page's question from the tibiofemoral one, because the same
malalignment has different consequences in the two compartments. 3.5.6 covers
that.

### Key Learning Points

- Malalignment acts through the direction of the extensor vector in two planes.
- The coronal component is measurable and routinely assessed; the transverse is neither.
- Femoral anteversion is mechanically equivalent to lateralising the tubercle.
- Femoral and tibial rotational abnormalities summate.
- Severe rotational malalignment can cause instability with a normal trochlea and intact ligament.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The transverse plane is where this chapter's under recognised pathology sits,
and the reason is historical rather than mechanical: the coronal plane was
measurable first.

Femoral anteversion above the normal range is reported in a substantial
minority of patients with recurrent instability, and it is rarely measured
before a first operation.

It matters because no soft tissue or tubercle procedure addresses it. A
derotation osteotomy does, and it is a larger operation than either. 7.68
covers the principles.

Coronal malalignment, by contrast, has a modest effect on this joint and a
large effect on the tibiofemoral compartments, which is the opposite emphasis
to the rotational case. 3.5.6 covers the tibiofemoral side.

Conventional teaching has moved towards measuring the rotational profile in
revision and in severe primary cases, and the threshold for intervening on it
is not agreed. 6.153 covers the controversies.

### What Changes It

Femoral version, which is the dominant under measured factor.

Tibial torsion, which contributes and is smaller in effect.

Coronal alignment, which matters more for the tibiofemoral joint than for this
one. 3.13.8 covers that.

Previous surgery, which may have corrected the measurable plane only.

Dynamic valgus, which adds a modifiable rotational component on top of the
fixed one. 3.12.7 covers it.

### How It Is Measured

Axial imaging from hip to ankle, which is the only way to measure version and
torsion properly and is not obtained routinely.

Three dimensional reconstruction, which measures the net vector and is largely
a research method. 3.16.5 covers it.

Tubercle to groove distance, which is the clinical proxy and conflates the
sources.

Patellar tendon to trochlear groove distance, which removes tubercle rotation
from the measurement and is reported less often.

Normal ranges for femoral version are wide and population dependent, so the
threshold question is partly a question about the reference range.

### Clinical Relevance

Obtain a rotational profile before revising a failed stabilisation, because an
unmeasured rotational cause is a common reason for failure. 3.12.8 covers the
factors.

Avoid correcting a raised tubercle to groove distance without knowing whether
its cause is tubercle position or femoral version. 3.12.12 covers the index.

State the reference range alongside a version measurement, since thresholds
depend on it.

Expect coronal correction to do less for this joint than for the tibiofemoral
compartments. 3.5.6 covers the comparison.

Weigh the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations before offering a
derotation osteotomy, which is a large operation for a geometric finding.

### Key Learning Points

- The transverse plane is under recognised because the coronal plane was measurable first.
- Femoral anteversion is reported in a substantial minority of recurrent dislocators.
- No soft tissue or tubercle procedure addresses femoral version; a derotation osteotomy does.
- Coronal malalignment matters more for the tibiofemoral joint than for this one.
- Normal ranges for version are wide, so the threshold question is partly about the reference range.
