# AMRI Assessment

Anteromedial rotatory instability: the pattern a patient describes as the knee
opening when they turn, and the one a valgus grade cannot see.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Anteromedial rotatory instability is a pattern rather than a structure. The
medial side of the tibia rotates forwards under load, which is a different
movement from the whole tibia translating forwards or the joint opening in
valgus.

It requires the posteromedial restraints to be deficient, and in most
descriptions the anterior cruciate as well, because one allows the rotation and
the other allows the anterior component. 3.9.7 covers the biomechanics.

Assessing it means adding rotation to the examination rather than performing a
new manoeuvre. 4.12.5 covers the structure it usually implicates.

### How It Is Performed

Flex the knee to about ninety degrees with the foot on the couch and sit on the
foot to hold it, as for an anterior drawer. 4.10.5 covers that test.

Rotate the tibia externally by about fifteen degrees, so the medial side is
presented forwards.

Draw the tibia anteriorly and watch the medial tibial plateau rather than the
tibial tubercle or the foot.

Note whether the medial plateau rotates forwards around an axis rather than the
whole tibia sliding, and compare the movement with the other knee.

Repeat the anterior drawer in neutral rotation, because the difference between
the two is the finding rather than either reading on its own.

### What a Positive Means

More anterior movement of the medial plateau with the tibia externally rotated
than in neutral indicates anteromedial rotatory instability.

The pattern means the posteromedial restraints are not holding the medial side
back, and it is usually found alongside an anterior cruciate rupture.
4.14.4 covers the pattern among the others.

A knee with increased straight anterior translation and no extra rotation has
an anterior cruciate injury without the medial component. 4.10.5 covers that.

A knee with valgus laxity at thirty degrees and no rotational component has an
isolated superficial ligament injury, and this test is what separates the two.
4.12.3 covers that grade.

The patient's description is often better than the test. A knee that opens when
they pivot away from the affected side describes this pattern more clearly than
any manoeuvre reads it. 4.2.2 covers instability as a symptom.

### Key Learning Points

- A pattern rather than a structure: the medial plateau rotates forwards under load.
- It needs the posteromedial restraints deficient, and usually the anterior cruciate too.
- The finding is the difference between the drawer in external rotation and in neutral.
- Watch the medial plateau, not the tubercle or the foot.
- The patient's description of the knee opening on a pivot is often clearer than the test.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the test to name when asked how you would assess a combined
medial and cruciate injury, and naming the pattern rather than a structure is
the mark.

The expected points are the ninety degree position, the fifteen degrees of
external rotation, drawing the tibia forward, watching the medial plateau, and
comparing with the neutral drawer.

Say that you would also test with the tibia internally rotated, since internal
rotation tightens the posteromedial structures and a reduction in translation
there indicates they are intact.

Say that you would perform the extension valgus test as well, because the two
findings corroborate each other and the extension test is the more reliable.
4.12.2 covers it.

Say that the classification of rotatory instability is a description of
patterns rather than a grading system, and that recording the findings is
better than recording the label. 4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that rotation of the tibia is restrained by different
structures on each side, and the medial restraint to internal rotation is the
posteromedial complex. 3.9.2 covers rotational stability.

Removing it allows the medial plateau to rotate anteriorly, and that movement
requires an axis, which the intact lateral side supplies. 3.9.7 covers the
biomechanics.

The anterior cruciate resists the anterior component of the same movement,
which is why the pattern is usually a combined diagnosis rather than a medial
one. 3.9.10 covers the combination.

The mirror pattern on the lateral side is anterolateral rotatory instability,
and the two are assessed by the same logic applied to the opposite corner.
4.14.3 covers it.

### How Good It Is

The assessment of this pattern is the least reproducible part of the medial
examination, and that is a property of the pattern rather than of the examiner.

Separating rotation from translation by hand is a judgement, and examiners
disagree about it more than they disagree about any valgus grade.

Its value is that no other test reads the pattern at all. A valgus examination
is blind to it, and a cruciate examination attributes it to the cruciate.

The pattern also matches what the patient reports more closely than the valgus
grade does, which makes it the examination finding most likely to explain the
complaint. 4.2.6 covers giving way.

No figures appear for any of these comparisons, under decision 023, and the
comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The least reproducible part of the medial examination, because separating rotation from translation is a judgement.
- Internal rotation tightens the posteromedial structures, so reduced translation there means they are intact.
- Removing the medial restraint lets the plateau rotate about an axis the intact lateral side supplies.
- No other test reads this pattern: valgus is blind to it and the cruciate tests absorb it.
- It matches the patient's complaint more closely than the valgus grade does.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this pattern is what converts a medial injury from a bracing
problem into a reconstruction, and it does so where the valgus grade would not
have.

A patient with a repaired or reconstructed anterior cruciate, an acceptable
valgus grade and this pattern has an unaddressed posteromedial corner, and
that is the commonest reason to reconstruct the medial side in a chronic knee.
4.12.8 covers the assessment.

In an acute combined injury the pattern decides whether the medial side is
addressed at the cruciate operation, and the examination after a period of
bracing is what makes that decision safely. 3.9.10 covers the combination.

The pattern also changes the cruciate operation's risk. A cruciate graft in a
knee with uncontrolled anteromedial rotation carries load in rotation, and the
stretched graft that follows is the consequence the examination exists to
prevent.

Where the examination is equivocal and the decision is real, examination under
anaesthesia with the other knee for comparison is the way to settle it.
4.14.6 covers that setting.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not looking for the pattern, which leaves it attributed
to the cruciate and the medial side untreated.

The second is reading the whole tibia moving as a rotation, which turns every
anterior cruciate rupture into a rotatory instability.

The third is examining in external rotation alone, with no neutral comparison,
so there is no baseline to read the difference against.

The fourth is recording the label rather than the findings. The classification
of rotatory instability is contested, and a letter saying anteromedial rotatory
instability without the findings behind it is hard for the next clinician to
use.

### Key Learning Points

- The pattern converts a medial injury from a bracing problem into a reconstruction.
- In a chronic knee it is the commonest reason to reconstruct the medial side.
- A cruciate graft in a knee with uncontrolled anteromedial rotation carries load in rotation.
- Reading the whole tibia moving as rotation turns every cruciate rupture into a rotatory instability.
- Record the findings rather than the label, because the classification is contested.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this pattern is found in the knee that has had a
technically sound cruciate reconstruction and still feels unsafe on turning.

That knee usually has an uncontrolled posteromedial corner, and the examination
that identifies it is this one performed against the other side. 4.12.5 covers
the structure.

A patient with a stretched cruciate graft and this pattern has the explanation
for the stretch rather than a second problem, and treating the graft alone
repeats the failure. 4.10.12 covers revision assessment.

The pattern is easy to produce in a hypermobile patient and easy to miss in a
muscular one, so the comparison with the other knee carries more weight here
than the absolute finding does. 4.23.3 covers hypermobile patients.

Under anaesthesia the pattern becomes readable in a knee that could not be
assessed awake, and in a multiligament knee that is usually where it is first
established. 4.11.7 covers that assessment.

In a knee with a lateral extra-articular procedure the lateral side is no
longer a free axis, so the rotation this test depends on is constrained and a
negative result says less than it would otherwise. 3.11.7 covers the procedure.

### Key Learning Points

- It is found in the knee with a sound cruciate reconstruction that still feels unsafe on turning.
- A stretched graft with this pattern has its explanation rather than a second problem.
- Easy to produce in a hypermobile patient and easy to miss in a muscular one.
- Under anaesthesia the pattern becomes readable where the awake examination failed.
- A lateral extra-articular procedure constrains the rotation the test depends on.
