# Previous Physiotherapy

Whether it was tried, what it involved, and whether the patient did it. Three
different questions that get one answer.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Asking about physiotherapy tests whether non-operative treatment has genuinely
been tried, which is the question most often answered loosely.

A patient who attended twice and stopped has not had physiotherapy in the
sense the decision requires.

It also tests what the symptom responded to, which is diagnostic information
as well as treatment history.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether they have had physiotherapy, then ask how many sessions and over
how long.

Ask what it involved: exercises, manual treatment, acupuncture, machines, or
advice.

Ask whether they were given exercises to do at home, and then ask whether they
did them, and how often. This is the question that changes the answer most.

Ask whether anything helped, anything made it worse, and why they stopped.

Ask whether they were discharged, stopped attending, or ran out of
appointments, since these are different.

### What a Positive Means

A symptom that improved with a loading programme and relapsed on stopping has
been diagnosed as well as treated, and the treatment is to resume it.

A symptom unchanged by a properly delivered programme is more likely to be
structural, which strengthens a surgical argument.

A programme the patient did not do tells you nothing about the symptom, and
the honest next step is to do it rather than to escalate.

A symptom made worse by exercise, particularly loading exercise, raises an
inflammatory or a bone cause rather than a mechanical one. 4.2.14 covers the
inflammatory pattern.

### Key Learning Points

- A patient who attended twice has not had physiotherapy in the sense a decision requires.
- Ask whether they did the home exercises, and how often.
- Improvement with loading and relapse on stopping is a diagnosis as well as a treatment.
- An unchanged symptom after a proper programme strengthens a surgical argument.
- A programme not done tells you nothing about the symptom.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that non-operative treatment is a trial with a
result, and the result only counts if the treatment happened.

For patellofemoral pain, a progressive hip and quadriceps programme is the
primary treatment and most patients improve, so a failure to improve should
first prompt a check of whether the programme was followed. 4.15.9 covers the
presentation.

For tendinopathy, progressive loading is the treatment and it takes months, so
a six week course is not a failed trial. 4.16.3 covers one version.

For a degenerate knee, exercise improves pain and function and does not change
the radiographs, which is worth saying to a patient who expects the second.

For instability, quadriceps rehabilitation changes function substantially and
does not change laxity, which is the same distinction. 4.2.2 covers
instability.

### How Good It Is

This history performs well when asked properly and badly when asked as a yes
or no.

The compliance question is the single most informative one and the one most
often omitted, because it feels like an accusation and is not.

Duration matters: many of these programmes need months and are abandoned at
weeks.

A documented, completed, supervised programme that failed is strong evidence
and should be sought rather than assumed.

### Key Learning Points

- Non-operative treatment is a trial, and the result only counts if the treatment happened.
- A six week course of loading for tendinopathy is not a failed trial.
- Exercise improves pain and function in a degenerate knee and does not change the radiographs.
- Quadriceps rehabilitation changes function substantially and does not change laxity.
- The compliance question is the most informative and the most often omitted.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this history determines whether the non-operative route has
been exhausted, which most elective decisions depend on.

A patient who has completed a supervised programme without benefit has a
stronger case for surgery, and the history is the only record of it.

A patient who has not is being offered an operation in place of a treatment,
which is a worse offer even when it is what they want.

The exception is a mechanical block or a structural injury with a repair
window, where waiting for rehabilitation costs the repair. 4.2.5 covers
locking.

Saying that the programme has to happen first, with a specific duration and a
review date, is a clinical decision and should be recorded as one. 4.1.8
covers the record.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting failed physiotherapy at face value, which
converts an untried treatment into a justification for surgery.

The second is prescribing more of the same when the first programme was wrong
rather than insufficient. A patellofemoral problem treated with knee exercises
alone and no hip work has not had the right programme.

The third is treating a patient's reluctance as non-compliance when it is a
reasonable response to pain during the exercises, which is a reason to change
the programme.

And in an elite athlete, physiotherapy has usually been extensive and the
history is genuinely a record of failed treatment. 4.23.2 covers elite
athletes.

### Key Learning Points

- This history determines whether the non-operative route has been exhausted.
- An operation offered in place of an untried treatment is a worse offer even when wanted.
- The exception is a mechanical block or a repair window, where waiting costs the repair.
- More of the same is wrong when the first programme was wrong rather than insufficient.
- Pain during exercises is a reason to change the programme rather than a compliance failure.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the question is usually whether a further programme is
worth trying before a further operation, and the history decides it.

A patient who has had many courses of physiotherapy with no clear programme
and no progression has not had a trial, however many sessions are recorded.

A patient who has had a structured, progressed, documented programme delivered
by someone familiar with the condition, and has not improved, has had a trial.

The distinction is in the progression rather than the number of sessions, and
it is frequently not recorded anywhere. 4.2.34 covers rehabilitation in more
detail.

In a multiply operated knee a further programme is often the better option,
and framing it as treatment rather than as delay is part of the consultation.

### Key Learning Points

- Many courses with no progression are not a trial, however many sessions are recorded.
- A structured, progressed, documented programme that failed is a trial.
- The distinction is in the progression rather than the session count.
- In a multiply operated knee a further programme is often the better option.
