# Combined Ligament Injury Assessment

The examination where the first job is not the ligaments, and the one where
missing a structure changes the operation rather than the diagnosis.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A combined ligament assessment establishes which of the knee's restraints are
deficient, in a knee where more than one of them is.

The reason it is a separate skill rather than a longer list of tests is that
the tests interfere with each other. A deficient posterior cruciate changes the
anterior drawer, and a deficient corner changes the rotational tests.
4.11.1 covers the first of those.

Before any of that, a knee with multiple ligament injuries may have been
dislocated, and a dislocated knee can injure the artery and the nerve. The
vascular and neurological examination comes first and is not optional.

### How It Is Performed

Examine the circulation first. Feel both foot pulses, compare the two limbs for
temperature and colour, and record what you find.

Treat absent or reduced pulses as an arterial injury until it has been excluded
properly, rather than as a finding to review later.

Examine the common peroneal nerve next, testing ankle dorsiflexion, great toe
extension and sensation over the dorsum of the foot, because this nerve is the
one at risk in the posterolateral injury.

Then look for skin dimpling medially, which suggests an irreducible
dislocation, and record the limb's resting position.

Only then begin the ligament sequence: inspection and sag, posterior drawer in
neutral and in internal rotation, Lachman and anterior drawer, valgus stress at
zero and thirty degrees, varus stress at zero and thirty degrees, the dial test
at thirty and ninety degrees, and the rotational tests last. 4.12.1 covers
valgus stress testing.

Record every test on both knees, because a combined injury is defined by a
pattern and a pattern needs a comparison.

### What a Positive Means

Increased laxity to a stress at thirty degrees with a firm endpoint at zero
suggests the collateral alone; laxity at both suggests the collateral and a
cruciate. 4.12.2 covers the assessment in extension.

A positive sag with increased external rotation at thirty degrees but not at
ninety suggests the posterolateral corner without the posterior cruciate.
4.13.5 covers the dial test.

Increased external rotation at both thirty and ninety degrees suggests both the
corner and the posterior cruciate, which is the combination the reverse pivot
shift detects. 4.11.4 covers it.

A third degree posterior drawer should be read as a combined injury until the
corner has been examined, because the corner resists the last part of that
translation. 4.11.2 covers the grade.

### Key Learning Points

- The vascular and neurological examination comes before the ligaments and is not optional.
- Treat absent or reduced foot pulses as arterial injury until properly excluded.
- The common peroneal nerve is the nerve at risk in the posterolateral injury.
- Laxity at thirty degrees alone suggests the collateral; laxity at zero as well suggests a cruciate too.
- External rotation increased at both thirty and ninety degrees implicates the corner and the posterior cruciate.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the multiligament knee is the station where sequence is the answer,
and the candidate who starts with the Lachman has already lost the mark.

Say pulses, nerve, skin, then ligaments, and say it in that order without being
prompted. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Say that you would repeat the vascular examination rather than record it once,
because an arterial injury can declare itself hours after a normal first
assessment.

Say that you would examine the hip and the ankle, since a high energy mechanism
rarely injures one joint. 4.2.17 covers contact injury.

Say that you would not force a knee that feels grossly unstable into ninety
degrees of flexion, and that examination under anaesthesia answers what the
clinic cannot. 4.23.1 covers it.

Say that you would document the pattern rather than a list of positive tests,
because the pattern is what the operation is planned from. 4.1.8 covers the
record.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that each restraint has a flexion angle and a plane
in which it dominates, so the pattern across angles localises the injury in a
way that no single test can. 3.8.3 covers bundle function.

The superficial medial ligament dominates valgus restraint at thirty degrees,
and laxity in full extension requires a cruciate or the posteromedial corner as
well. 4.12.5 covers the posterior oblique ligament.

The lateral collateral dominates varus restraint at thirty degrees, with the
same logic applying in extension. 4.13.2 covers it.

External rotation at thirty degrees is resisted principally by the
posterolateral corner and at ninety degrees principally by the posterior
cruciate, which is why the dial test is read at two angles rather than one.
4.13.5 covers that reading.

Hyperextension and a recurvatum deformity on lifting the great toe implicate
the corner rather than either cruciate. 4.13.7 covers the recurvatum test.

### How Good It Is

The clinical examination of a multiligament knee localises the injured
structures well and grades them poorly, and that combination decides how it is
used.

Localisation is what the operation needs, so the examination contributes more
to planning in these knees than in any other group in this chapter.

It is unreliable in the acute high energy knee, where pain, guarding and
effusion defeat most of the sequence, and that is exactly the knee in which the
information is most urgently needed.

Examination under anaesthesia is therefore part of the assessment rather than a
luxury, and in these knees it is usually the examination the plan is built on.
4.23.6 covers complex injuries.

Magnetic resonance imaging identifies the torn structures more completely and
says less about which laxity matters, so the two are read together rather than
one instead of the other. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any of these comparisons, under decision 023, and the
comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Each restraint dominates at a particular angle and plane, so the pattern across angles localises the injury.
- The superficial medial ligament dominates valgus restraint at thirty degrees.
- External rotation is resisted by the corner at thirty degrees and the posterior cruciate at ninety.
- The examination localises well and grades poorly, and planning needs localisation.
- Examination under anaesthesia is part of the assessment in these knees, not a luxury.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the combined assessment determines the operation, which is
not true of any other examination in this chapter.

Whether the corner is reconstructed alongside the cruciate, whether the medial
side is repaired or reconstructed, and whether the knee is staged all follow
from the pattern the examination establishes.

The single most consequential finding is an unrecognised posterolateral corner
injury, because a cruciate graft placed into a knee with an uncontrolled corner
stretches, and that is the commonest avoidable failure in this group.
4.13.4 covers the corner.

Stress radiographs convert the grades into numbers and are worth obtaining
where the plan turns on a grade, since the examination's weakness is grading
rather than localisation.

Alignment matters as much here as in the chronic knee, because a varus limb
loads a lateral reconstruction continuously. 4.11.6 covers that argument.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is a complete and careful ligament examination with no
vascular examination recorded, which is the one omission in this chapter that
can cost a limb.

The second is reading each test in isolation rather than as a pattern, which
produces a list of positives and no diagnosis.

The third is examining a grossly unstable knee aggressively in the clinic,
which is uninformative and occasionally harmful.

The fourth is accepting an imaging report as the assessment. An image names
torn structures; the examination says which laxity the patient has, and the
operation is planned from the second.

The fifth is examining one knee. In a bilaterally hypermobile patient the
uninjured side is the only thing that makes a grade interpretable. 4.23.3
covers those patients.

### Key Learning Points

- The pattern determines the operation, which is not true elsewhere in this chapter.
- An unrecognised posterolateral corner injury is the commonest avoidable cause of graft failure here.
- Stress radiographs are worth obtaining where the plan turns on a grade.
- A ligament examination with no recorded vascular examination is the omission that can cost a limb.
- An imaging report names torn structures; the examination says which laxity the patient has.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred and revision practice the combined knee arrives having been
operated on once already, and the examination is reconstructing what was missed
rather than what was torn.

The commonest finding is a stretched posterior cruciate graft with an
uncontrolled corner, and the two are separated by the dial test at thirty and
ninety degrees rather than by the drawer. 4.13.5 covers it.

Previous surgery changes what the tests mean. A reconstructed lateral side may
be tight in a way that masks rotational laxity, and a healed medial repair can
give a firm endpoint over a lax joint. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

A knee with multiple reconstructions and persistent instability needs alignment
and the tibial slope assessed before any further soft tissue work, because a
slope that favours posterior translation will defeat a posterior cruciate graft
whatever the technique.

A common peroneal nerve palsy that recovered incompletely changes the dynamic
examination permanently, and attributing the resulting gait to ligament laxity
is a recognised error in this group. 4.11.5 covers dynamic assessment.

The examination also carries the decision about staging, which no imaging
supports. A knee that cannot be examined because of pain and effusion is
usually a knee to examine under anaesthesia and stage, and that judgement is
clinical. 4.23.6 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- In revision practice the examination reconstructs what was missed rather than what was torn.
- A stretched cruciate graft and an uncontrolled corner are separated by the dial test, not the drawer.
- A tight lateral reconstruction can mask rotational laxity.
- Alignment and tibial slope are assessed before further soft tissue work.
- An incompletely recovered nerve palsy changes the dynamic examination permanently.
