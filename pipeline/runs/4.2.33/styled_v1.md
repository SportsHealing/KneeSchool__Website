# Previous Imaging

What has been scanned, when, and what the patient was told. The last question
is the one that changes the consultation.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Asking about previous imaging tests what exists, what it showed, and what the
patient believes it showed.

The third of those matters most, because the explanation a patient has been
given becomes part of their problem and shapes everything they say afterwards.
4.1.3 covers the contamination.

It also tests how old the imaging is, since a knee changes and a scan from
three years ago describes a knee that no longer exists.

### How It Is Performed

Ask what imaging they have had: radiographs, ultrasound, magnetic resonance,
computed tomography, or a scan they cannot name.

Ask when, and whether it was before or after any treatment.

Ask whether the images are available or only the report, because a report is a
interpretation and the images can be reviewed.

Ask what they were told, in their words, and listen for the phrase that has
stuck.

Take the history before reading the report wherever possible, which is awkward
and is the only way to get an uncontaminated account. 4.1.3 covers the
principle.

### What a Positive Means

A scan showing a finding that matches the history supports the diagnosis.

A scan showing a finding that does not match the history is a finding and not
a diagnosis, and degenerate changes in a middle aged knee are the commonest
example. 4.9.11 covers it.

A normal scan in a patient with convincing mechanical symptoms does not
exclude a cause, and small flap tears and chondral lesions are missable. 4.2.7
covers mechanical symptoms.

Imaging from before a treatment cannot describe the current knee, and this
matters most after surgery.

A patient who has been told their knee is worn out carries that sentence into
every consultation, and part of this history is finding out what they were
told.

### Key Learning Points

- What the patient was told matters as much as what the scan showed.
- A finding that does not match the history is a finding and not a diagnosis.
- A normal scan does not exclude a cause of convincing mechanical symptoms.
- A scan from three years ago describes a knee that no longer exists.
- Take the history before reading the report where possible.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The expected reasoning is about base rates. Degenerate meniscal change,
cartilage signal change and small effusions are common in asymptomatic middle
aged knees, so their presence carries limited weight alone. 4.1.6 covers the
point.

In a young knee after a defined injury the base rate is lower and a finding
means more.

An old radiograph is still useful for alignment and joint space, and a
previous radiograph compared with a current one gives a rate of change that
neither gives alone.

Imaging performed at another institution may use different sequences and
different reporting conventions, which is a reason to review the images rather
than the report.

A report is not the finding. The images can be reviewed and frequently should
be.

### How Good It Is

This history performs well for establishing what exists and poorly for
establishing what it showed, since patients recall conclusions rather than
findings.

The date is reliably recalled, which matters because it determines relevance.

The most useful question is what they were told, and it is useful for a reason
that is about the consultation rather than the knee.

Comparing serial imaging is among the more useful things available in
degenerative disease and depends entirely on knowing that the earlier imaging
exists.

### Key Learning Points

- Degenerate findings are common in asymptomatic middle aged knees and carry limited weight alone.
- Comparing an old radiograph with a new one gives a rate of change neither gives alone.
- A report is an interpretation; the images can be reviewed.
- Patients recall conclusions rather than findings.
- Serial comparison depends on knowing the earlier imaging exists.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the imaging history determines whether anything further is
needed, which is a question that should be asked more often.

A patient with recent adequate imaging that answers the clinical question
needs no more, and repeating it is a cost without a benefit.

A patient with imaging that does not answer the question, or that predates a
relevant change, needs it repeated and the history establishes which.

In planning, the imaging requirement follows from the operation rather than
from the diagnosis: an osteotomy needs long leg alignment views, a revision
reconstruction needs tunnel assessment, and neither is supplied by a standard
scan. 4.20.4 covers osteotomy assessment.

Establishing what the patient has been told is also a planning matter, because
the consent conversation has to start from where they are.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is letting the imaging lead. A consultation that begins
with the scan has already lost the uncontaminated history.

The second is accepting a report of a finding as a diagnosis, which this
chapter returns to because it is the commonest error in knee practice. 4.1.1
covers it.

The third is repeating imaging because it was done elsewhere rather than
because it does not answer the question.

And failing to correct a wrong explanation the patient is carrying, which is a
clinical task rather than a courtesy, because that explanation will determine
what they expect and therefore whether they are satisfied.

### Key Learning Points

- Ask whether anything further is needed, which should be asked more often.
- The imaging requirement follows from the operation rather than from the diagnosis.
- A consultation that begins with the scan has lost the uncontaminated history.
- Repeat imaging because it does not answer the question, not because it was done elsewhere.
- Correcting a wrong explanation the patient carries is a clinical task.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the patient usually arrives with several scans and an
explanation built from the most recent one.

The useful task is to establish the sequence of imaging against the sequence
of operations and symptoms, which is the timeline 4.2.30 describes, with
imaging added to it.

Post-operative imaging is difficult to interpret and a report written without
the operative detail is frequently misleading, which is a reason to review
images alongside the notes rather than reading reports.

In a painful arthroplasty or a failed reconstruction the imaging that answers
the question is frequently not the imaging that has been done, and the history
is what identifies the gap. 4.20.9 covers revision assessment.

The most important part of this history at this level is what the patient has
been told by each previous clinician, because a referred patient is frequently
carrying several incompatible explanations and the consultation has to address
that before anything else.

### Key Learning Points

- Establish the sequence of imaging against the sequence of operations and symptoms.
- A post-operative report written without the operative detail is frequently misleading.
- The imaging that answers the question is often not the imaging that has been done.
- A referred patient frequently carries several incompatible explanations.
