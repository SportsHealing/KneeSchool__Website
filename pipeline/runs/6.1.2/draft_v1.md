# Functions of the ACL

The ligament does a mechanical job and a sensory one, and a reconstruction
restores the first far better than the second.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

The ACL has two jobs. The obvious one is mechanical: it holds the shin bone back
so it cannot slide forward under the thigh bone. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

The less obvious one is that it senses. Nerve endings inside it tell the brain
where the knee is and how fast it is moving, without anyone having to look.
2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

That second job is why a knee can feel wrong even when it is being held
together properly.

### How It Presents

Someone with a torn ACL can walk and run in a straight line and still not trust
the knee to turn. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The feeling people describe is the knee giving way, usually on a turn rather
than on a step. 4.10.3 covers chronic ACL deficiency.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL holds the shin bone back from sliding forward.
- It also senses where the knee is and how it is moving.
- Both jobs are lost when it tears.
- A knee can be held together and still feel wrong.
- Giving way happens on a turn more often than on a step.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

The ACL's main mechanical job is to stop the shin bone sliding forward relative
to the thigh bone. 3.7.8 covers the anterior stability.

It also helps control rotation, which is the part most people notice when it is
gone. 3.7.7 covers the rotational stability.

Alongside both, it carries nerve endings that feed the brain information about
the knee's position and speed. 2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

### How It Presents

Without the ligament, the shin can shift forward and rotate under the thigh
during a turn, and that shift is what people feel as giving way. 3.7.9 covers
the pivot shift mechanics.

Some people adapt well and some do not, and how much sport someone plays is a
better predictor than the scan. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

After a reconstruction, the knee is usually held properly again, and confidence
can take longer to return than strength. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The main job is stopping the shin sliding forward.
- The ligament also helps control rotation.
- It carries nerve endings that report the knee's position.
- Giving way is the shift and rotation that happen without it.
- Confidence can take longer to return than strength.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** If other ligaments are intact, why does the knee still feel unstable?
Because the others restrain different directions. They hold the knee against
side to side force and against the shin sliding backwards, and none of them is
positioned to stop it sliding forward under rotation.

**Q.** Can muscles do the ACL's job?
Partly. Strong hamstrings pull the shin backwards and help, which is why
rehabilitation works for some people. Muscles react after a movement has
started, so they are a slower substitute than a ligament.

**Q.** Will a reconstruction make the knee feel normal again?
Usually it restores the mechanical job well. The sensory side is restored less
completely, and that is one reason a knee can test as stable while still feeling
unfamiliar.

**Q.** Why does the knee feel fine walking?
Walking in a straight line does not ask the ligament to do much. The job it was
doing shows up when the knee decelerates, lands or turns.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

The ACL is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation, taking most of
the restraining force in that direction. 3.7.2 covers the primary restraints.

It is a secondary restraint to internal rotation, which places it alongside the
anterolateral structures rather than alone. 3.7.3 covers the secondary
restraints.

Its two bundles are not redundant: they tighten at different parts of the arc,
the anteromedial nearer flexion and the posterolateral nearer extension. 3.7.5
covers the AM bundle function.

It is also a sensory organ, carrying mechanoreceptors that contribute to
proprioception and to reflex muscle control. 2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

### Causes and Risk Factors

The ligament is loaded most when the knee is near extension and the tibia is
driven forward or rotated inwards. 3.7.10 covers the ACL strain during activity.

Landing from a jump loads it, and so does cutting, which is why both appear in
every prevention programme. 3.7.12 covers the ACL loading during landing.

Quadriceps contraction near extension pulls the tibia forward and raises the
load, which is the mechanical basis for hamstring work in rehabilitation. 3.7.11
covers the ACL loading during running.

### Examination

Each test asks about one of the ligament's functions, which is why more than one
is used. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

Anterior translation is tested directly and is the most reproducible of the
functions to assess. 4.10.5 covers the anterior drawer test.

Rotational control is tested indirectly, by reproducing the shift the patient
complains of. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Proprioception is not tested at the bedside at all, which is the function
imaging and examination both miss. 4.10.8 covers the dynamic assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- The ACL is the primary restraint to anterior translation.
- It is a secondary restraint to internal rotation.
- The bundles tighten at different parts of the arc.
- Quadriceps contraction near extension raises the load on it.
- Proprioception is the function no bedside test reports.

## MRCS Level

### Examination

In a viva the expected answer pairs each test with the function it reports.

The expected points are translation against the Lachman test, rotation against
the pivot shift, the bundles' differing tension through the arc, and
proprioception as the function nothing tests. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift
test.

Say that the pivot shift correlates with what the patient complains of better
than the Lachman does, and is harder to elicit in a guarded knee. 4.10.9 covers
the rotational instability.

Say that a test under anaesthesia removes guarding and is the reason it is done.
4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

### Investigations and Imaging

Imaging reports the ligament's structure and not its function, which is the
division this whole section turns on. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

A scan can show a continuous ligament in a knee that is functionally deficient,
and a disrupted one in a knee the patient manages well. 6.3.4 covers partial
tears.

Secondary signs on a scan are consequences of the function having been lost,
which is why they are read alongside the ligament rather than after it. 5.7.4
covers acute ACL injury.

### Key Learning Points

- Pair each test with the function it reports.
- The pivot shift matches the complaint better than the Lachman test.
- A guarded knee hides rotation more than translation.
- Imaging reports structure and not function.
- Secondary signs are consequences of lost function.

## FRCS Level

### Examination

At examination level the candidate is expected to explain why restoring
translation is not the same as restoring the knee.

A reconstruction that controls the Lachman test and leaves a positive pivot
shift has restored one function and not the other. 3.7.9 covers the pivot shift
mechanics.

That residual rotation is the argument for addressing the anterolateral
structures alongside, and the argument is live rather than settled. 6.4.4 covers
ALL injuries.

Graft choice and tunnel position change how well rotation is controlled, which
is why an anatomical footprint matters beyond tidiness. 2.7.6 covers the femoral
footprint.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first question is how much rotational control the anterolateral structures
supply, and therefore when they should be reconstructed too. 6.4.4 covers ALL
injuries.

The second is whether a double bundle reconstruction restores function better
than a single bundle, and whether any difference survives into what patients
report. 3.7.4 covers the bundle function.

The third is whether proprioceptive loss can be restored at all, by graft
selection, by preserving remnant tissue, or by rehabilitation. 2.7.10 covers the
proprioception.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
here. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Restoring translation is not restoring the knee.
- A controlled Lachman with a positive pivot shift is a partial result.
- Residual rotation drives the anterolateral reconstruction argument.
- Double bundle reconstruction's advantage in patient reported outcome is open.
- Whether proprioceptive loss can be restored is unresolved.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the gap between a mechanically sound knee and a knee the
patient uses is this page's subject, and it is the commonest disappointment
after a technically good operation.

A graft can be correctly placed, correctly tensioned and correctly healed, and
the patient can still not return to their sport. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The usual explanation offered is psychological, and the sensory role gives a
second explanation that is mechanical in origin and not measured by any test in
use. 2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

Both explanations point the same way clinically, which is that rehabilitation
has to rebuild confidence and control rather than strength alone. 6.5.1 covers
instability.

The honest position is that the two cannot be separated with what is measured
today, and that a fellow should not present either as established. 6.1.4 covers
the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- A sound graft and a knee the patient avoids using can coexist.
- The usual explanation is psychological.
- The sensory role offers a second explanation nothing measures.
- Both point at confidence and control rather than strength.
- The two cannot be separated with current measurement.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question here is what the operation is being sold as, and whether
that matches what it reliably restores.

Restoring anterior translation is reliable. Restoring rotational control is less
so, and restoring the sensation of a trustworthy knee is least reliable of all.
3.7.7 covers the rotational stability.

Counselling that promises a return to the previous level without naming that
gradient is promising the part that is least reliably delivered. 6.1.4 covers
the natural history.

What to say depends on the same five things. The patient's age and physiology,
which set healing and tissue quality. Their medical comorbidities, because
smoking, diabetes and body mass change graft healing without changing the
mechanics. The demands they put on the knee, which decide whether rotational
control is the deciding function for them at all. Their psychological state,
because fear of reinjury governs what the knee is used for. And their own ideas,
concerns and expectations, which decide what a good result would even mean to
them.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and counsel against them
rather than against a cohort. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

### Key Learning Points

- Translation is restored reliably and confidence least reliably.
- Counselling that omits the gradient promises the least reliable part.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide what to say.
- Rotational control is the deciding function only for some patients.
- A good result is defined by the patient rather than by the cohort.
