# Patellar Fractures

A fracture of the extensor mechanism's sesamoid, where the functional question is
separate from the radiographic one.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A patellar fracture breaks the bone that transmits the extensor mechanism across
the knee. 2.4.1 covers the patellar anatomy.

The radiograph shows the fracture line, its displacement and whether the fragments
are separated. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

It matters because the clinical question is whether the extensor mechanism still
works, and the radiograph answers a different question. 4.7.8 covers the extension
lag.

### How It Is Acquired

Obtain a lateral view, which shows displacement and separation best. 5.3.2 covers
the lateral view.

Obtain a skyline view where a vertical or marginal fracture is suspected. 5.3.3
covers the skyline view.

Assess the patellar height, since a displaced fracture or a tendon rupture
changes it. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

Distinguish a fracture from a bipartite patella, which has a corticated margin
rather than a sharp edge. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

Assess the extensor mechanism clinically, because the imaging cannot. 4.7.1 covers
active motion.

### What Normal Looks Like

A continuous cortical outline on the lateral and skyline views.

A patella of normal height for the flexion angle. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

A bipartite fragment, where present, with smooth corticated margins at the
superolateral corner. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

Clear soft tissue planes around the tendons. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A transverse fracture line with separation of the fragments. 5.3.2 covers the
lateral view.

A vertical or marginal fracture, best seen on the skyline view. 5.3.3 covers the
skyline view.

A high riding patella with a soft tissue gap below it, which indicates a patellar
tendon rupture rather than a fracture. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

A comminuted patella with multiple fragments.

### Key Learning Points

- The patella transmits the extensor mechanism across the knee.
- The lateral view shows displacement and separation best.
- A vertical fracture needs the skyline view.
- A bipartite patella has corticated margins, not a sharp edge.
- Extensor function is a clinical question, not an imaging one.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer separates the radiographic finding from the
functional assessment within the first sentence.

The expected points are the lateral, the skyline, the patellar height, the
bipartite distinction, and the clinical extensor assessment.

Say that displacement and extensor function are two different questions. 4.7.1
covers active motion.

Say that an undisplaced fracture with an intact mechanism can be managed
non-operatively. 4.7.8 covers the extension lag.

Say that patellar height change suggests tendon rather than bone. 5.12.2 covers
patella alta.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the patella is held by the quadriceps above and the
patellar tendon below, so separation of the fragments means the retinaculum has
failed as well. 2.4.5 covers the patellar surgical relevance.

An undisplaced fracture implies intact retinacular fibres, which is why
displacement rather than the fracture line predicts function. 3.12.4 covers the
trochlear function.

A bipartite patella is a failure of ossification rather than of bone, which is why
its margins are corticated. 2.4.2 covers the patellar ossification.

Patellar height changes when the tendon fails, so a change in height redirects the
diagnosis from the bone to the tendon. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

### How Good It Is

The radiograph identifies a patellar fracture reliably and predicts extensor
function only through displacement.

Its best contribution is displacement and comminution, which decide the fixation.
5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

It is limited for the marginal and vertical patterns without a skyline, and for
articular surface involvement. 5.3.3 covers the skyline view.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
displacement thresholds appear either: the operative thresholds are published
quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- Separation of the fragments means the retinaculum has failed too.
- Displacement rather than the fracture line predicts function.
- A bipartite patella is a failure of ossification.
- A change in patellar height redirects the diagnosis to the tendon.
- Articular surface involvement is not well shown.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the radiograph supplies the fixation plan and the examination
supplies the indication.

A displaced transverse fracture with a functional deficit is the standard
operative picture. 4.7.8 covers the extension lag.

Comminution changes the fixation strategy, so fragment number matters more than
the overall displacement. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

An articular surface step needs cross sectional assessment, which is often
omitted for the patella. 5.16.1 covers the fracture assessment.

An intact extensor mechanism with an undisplaced fracture can be managed without
surgery regardless of the radiographic appearance. 4.7.1 covers active motion.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is confusing a bipartite patella with a fracture. 5.15.7
covers developmental variants.

A vertical fracture is missed because no skyline view was obtained. 5.3.3 covers
the skyline view.

A tendon rupture is read as a fracture because the patella is displaced. 5.12.2
covers patella alta.

The articular surface is not assessed. 5.16.1 covers the fracture assessment.

Operative indication is taken from the radiograph rather than from the
examination. 4.7.8 covers the extension lag.

### Key Learning Points

- A bipartite patella is confused with a fracture.
- A vertical fracture is missed without a skyline view.
- A tendon rupture is read as a fracture.
- The articular surface goes unassessed.
- The indication is taken from the radiograph rather than the examination.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the patella is the knee's most radiographically obvious bone
and its articular surface the least assessed.

Articular step and comminution are what determine later patellofemoral arthritis,
and neither is measured well on plain films or routinely imaged cross
sectionally. 5.12.7 covers patellofemoral arthritis.

The functional outcome depends on extensor mechanism restoration, which no
imaging measures, so the radiographic result and the clinical result diverge
regularly. 4.21.7 covers return to sport testing.

Fixation constructs are assessed radiographically for position and not for the
articular reduction they achieved, which is the parameter that predicts the
compartment. 5.17.5 covers cartilage procedures.

A bipartite patella can become symptomatic, so the distinction from a fracture is
not always the end of the question. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

The useful discipline is to assess the articular surface deliberately and to
treat extensor function as a separate measurement made clinically. 4.7.1 covers
active motion.

### Key Learning Points

- Articular step and comminution determine later patellofemoral arthritis.
- Neither is measured well on plain films nor imaged routinely.
- Functional outcome depends on something no imaging measures.
- Fixation is assessed for position rather than for articular reduction.
- A bipartite patella can itself become symptomatic.
