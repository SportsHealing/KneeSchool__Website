# Basketball

Jumping and landing repeated hundreds of times a session, on a hard floor,
with cutting between each one.

## For Patients

### The Principle

Doing a demanding thing occasionally and doing it hundreds of times a session
are different problems.

The first risks a single failure. The second wears a structure down until it
fails at an ordinary load.

Basketball manages to do both, which is why it produces two separate kinds of
knee problem.

### At the Knee

Jumping to shoot, rebound and block happens repeatedly throughout a game and a
training session. 3.14.7 covers jumping.

Landing from those jumps, often on one leg and often while being bumped, is
the risky half. 3.14.8 covers landing.

The floor is hard and does not give, so the leg does all the absorbing.

Between jumps the game asks for constant stopping and changing direction.
3.14.6 covers cutting.

### Clinical Relevance

Pain in the tendon just below the kneecap is common in jumping athletes and is
a loading problem. 6.64 covers it.

A knee that gives way during a landing or a turn is a different problem and
needs assessing promptly.

Landing technique can be trained and reduces injuries. Section 11 covers it.

No injury rates are given on this page, because no source was available to
check them against.

### Key Learning Points

- A demanding thing done hundreds of times is a different problem from doing it once.
- Jumping happens throughout a game; landing is the risky half.
- The floor does not give, so the leg does all the absorbing.
- Cutting happens constantly between jumps.
- Pain below the kneecap in a jumping athlete is a loading problem.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why do so many basketball players get jumper's knee?
The tendon below the kneecap takes very high loads at takeoff, and basketball
repeats that takeoff hundreds of times a session on a hard floor. It is the
volume as much as the load.

**Q.** Does landing softly actually help?
Yes. Bending the hips, knees and ankles more spreads the same energy over more
movement, which lowers the force. It is one of the few things with measurable
effect.

**Q.** Are cushioned shoes enough?
They help a little. Most of the absorption is done by the legs, not the shoe,
so technique and strength matter more than footwear.

**Q.** My knee hurts when I jump but not when I walk. Is that serious?
It usually means the problem shows up only at high load, which fits a tendon.
It is worth assessing early, because tendon problems are easier to manage
before they are entrenched.

See a clinician if your knee gives way on landing, swells, locks, or if pain
stops you jumping.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A sport with both high peak loads and high repetition produces acute and
overuse injuries in parallel rather than one or the other.

Their histories differ: the acute injury has a moment, the overuse injury has
a trend.

Asking for the moment, and finding there is not one, is diagnostically useful.

### At the Knee

Takeoff loads the extensor mechanism concentrically and produces very high
patellar tendon force. 3.14.7 covers jumping.

Landing loads it eccentrically, and single leg landings roughly double the
demand on that limb. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Patellar tendinopathy is the characteristic overuse presentation and is
strongly associated with jumping volume. 6.64 covers it.

The acute injury is the cruciate, by the usual landing and cutting mechanism,
often while contested. 3.7.14 covers the mechanism.

A hard indoor surface does not attenuate impact, so the limb absorbs
essentially all of it. 3.4.10 covers shock absorption.

### What Changes It

Jump count per session and across a season, which is the cumulative term.

Landing strategy, meaning how the absorption is shared between ankle, knee and
hip.

Single leg against double leg landings, which differ roughly twofold in
demand.

Fatigue, which stiffens landings measurably late in a session. 3.14.8 covers
it.

Growth stage, since adolescent jumpers develop apophyseal rather than tendon
problems.

### Clinical Relevance

Ask whether there was a single moment, since its absence points to an overuse
presentation.

Take a jump volume history in a player with anterior knee pain.

Expect apophyseal problems rather than tendinopathy in a skeletally immature
player. 2.4.2 covers ossification.

Describe mechanism rather than rates, as this page does, because no source was
available.

### Key Learning Points

- High peak load and high repetition produce acute and overuse injuries in parallel.
- The acute injury has a moment; the overuse injury has a trend.
- Takeoff produces very high patellar tendon force; landing loads it eccentrically.
- Single leg landings roughly double the demand on that limb.
- Skeletally immature jumpers develop apophyseal rather than tendon problems.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Basketball is the clearest sporting example of load management as the primary
intervention, because the exposure is countable.

Jumps can be counted by a wearable, which makes the dose measurable in a way
that cutting or tackling is not.

A countable dose is the precondition for a dose response relationship, and
that is what makes this sport's overuse problem tractable.

### At the Knee

Patellar tendon force at takeoff is among the highest the extensor mechanism
experiences, and it rises with countermovement depth. 3.14.7 covers jumping.

Tendinopathy localises to the deep posterior fibres of the proximal patellar
tendon, which is where modelling predicts stress concentration. 3.16.4 covers
modelling.

Landing mechanics determine peak force, and peak knee flexion is its best
single predictor. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Contested landings remove anticipation and are where the cruciate injuries
occur. 3.14.6 covers anticipation.

Court hardness and shoe cushioning alter loading rate rather than total load,
which is a distinction that matters for tendon.

### What Changes It

Jump count and its rate of change, which is the dose.

Landing strategy and peak knee flexion.

Skeletal maturity, which determines whether the tendon or the apophysis fails
first.

Training surface, since practice floors and competition floors differ.

Prior tendinopathy, which recurs and is the strongest individual predictor.

### How It Is Measured

Wearable jump counting, which gives the dose and is in routine use in elite
programmes. 3.16.8 covers it.

Force plate countermovement jump testing, which gives strategy and asymmetry.
3.14.7 covers it.

Ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging of the tendon, which show structural
change that correlates imperfectly with symptoms.

Laboratory drop landing protocols for the acute mechanism, which measure a
task rather than a contested landing.

No measurement of tendon stress in vivo exists, so load management works on
jump count as a proxy.

### Clinical Relevance

Use jump count as the manageable dose, since it is countable where other
exposures are not.

Distinguish apophyseal from tendon presentation by skeletal maturity rather
than by site of pain alone.

Expect imaging change and symptoms to correlate imperfectly in tendinopathy.
6.64 covers the condition.

State that jump count is a proxy and that tendon stress is not measured.

Weigh the player's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, because load restriction
in a selected squad player is a harder conversation than it looks.

### Key Learning Points

- Jumps are countable, which makes the dose measurable and the problem tractable.
- Patellar tendon force at takeoff is among the highest the extensor mechanism sees.
- Tendinopathy localises where modelling predicts stress concentration.
- Court hardness alters loading rate rather than total load.
- Jump count is a proxy; tendon stress is not measured.
