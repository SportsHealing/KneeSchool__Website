# OCD

A lesion at one classic site, and the question is whether it is stable rather
than whether it is there.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Osteochondritis dissecans is a lesion of subchondral bone with its overlying
cartilage, and in the knee it has a characteristic site on the lateral aspect of
the medial femoral condyle. 2.1.3 covers the medial femoral condyle.

It appears in the growing knee and is distinguished from the irregular
ossification of normal development, which occupies the same region. 5.15.7
covers developmental variants.

It matters because a stable lesion in a growing knee can heal and an unstable
one will not, so the imaging question is stability. 4.19.5 covers the paediatric OCD
examination.

### How It Is Acquired

Image the condyles in all three planes, because the lesion's interface is what
is being judged and one plane shows it tangentially. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Use a fluid sensitive sequence to look for fluid tracking behind the fragment.
5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

Use a cartilage sensitive sequence to assess the overlying surface, since an
intact surface and a separated fragment are different problems. 5.11.1 covers
the normal cartilage.

Compare with the other knee, because these lesions are often bilateral and the
contralateral side shows the same stage. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

### What Normal Looks Like

An irregular ossification front at the condylar margin, with no interface behind
it and no cartilage abnormality over it. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric
anatomy.

Normal marrow behind that irregularity rather than a rim of change. 5.2.13
covers T1 imaging.

Continuous overlying cartilage of the expected paediatric thickness. 5.11.1
covers the normal cartilage.

Symmetry with the other side, which is the most useful single discriminator.
5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A defined fragment with an interface separating it from the parent bone. 5.11.5
covers the osteochondral lesions.

Fluid signal tracking fully behind the fragment, which is the instability sign
the report is written around. 5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

Breach of the overlying cartilage, which changes the lesion from contained to
not. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

Cysts at the interface, which accompany a lesion that is not healing. 5.11.7
covers the bone marrow lesions.

A displaced fragment in the joint, which is a mechanical finding and a different
problem. 5.6.6 covers the bucket handle tears.

### Key Learning Points

- The lesion has a characteristic site on the medial femoral condyle.
- It is distinguished from normal irregular ossification in the same region.
- A stable lesion in a growing knee can heal.
- Fluid behind the fragment is the instability sign.
- Breach of the overlying cartilage changes the problem.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer gives the site, then the stability signs, then the
developmental mimic.

The expected points are the classic location, the interface, the fluid behind
the fragment, the cartilage surface, and the contralateral comparison.

Say that stability rather than presence governs management. 4.19.5 covers the
paediatric OCD examination.

Say that the lesion is frequently bilateral and the other knee is worth imaging
when the diagnosis is in doubt. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

Say that normal ossification irregularity is the commonest mimic. 5.15.7 covers
developmental variants.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging is the modality that answers the stability question,
which is the only question about this lesion that changes what is done.

Its best contribution is the interface, since a film shows the lesion and cannot
show what lies behind the fragment. 5.11.5 covers the osteochondral lesions.

Its second contribution is the cartilage surface, which decides whether a lesion
is contained. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

It is limited because the stability signs were described in a growing knee whose
interface normally contains fluid at some stages, so the sign and the stage can
overlap. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

It is limited again because healing potential depends on skeletal stage, which
the study shows and the report usually omits. 5.20.1 covers structured
reporting.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no lesion
size, fragment dimension, interface length or staging number appears either:
those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this
environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The modality answers the stability question.
- A film shows the lesion and not what lies behind the fragment.
- The cartilage surface decides whether the lesion is contained.
- The stability signs and the normal developmental stage can overlap.
- Healing potential depends on a skeletal stage the report usually omits.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the open physis is the variable that decides the approach, and
it is a finding on the same study.

A stable lesion in a knee with an open plate is managed differently from the
same lesion in a closed one, so the report names the plate's state alongside the
lesion. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

An unstable lesion is reported with the features that make it unstable rather
than with the conclusion alone, because the surgeon is deciding between
procedures. 5.11.5 covers the osteochondral lesions.

A lesion with intact cartilage and no interface change is a reason to watch
rather than to intervene. 5.1.4 covers the imaging pathways.

The adult form of this lesion is a different problem with a different outlook and
the two are reported in the same words, which is the chapter's recurring
confusion. 5.11.6 covers OCD in the mature knee.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting normal ossification irregularity as a lesion.
5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

Fluid at a normal interface is read as instability. 5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

The lesion is reported without the state of the physis. 5.15.2 covers the growth
plates.

A bilateral developmental appearance is reported as bilateral disease. 5.3.4
covers the tunnel view.

The overlying cartilage is not assessed, so containment is not addressed. 5.11.2
covers the chondral defects.

### Key Learning Points

- Normal ossification irregularity is reported as a lesion.
- Fluid at a normal interface is read as instability.
- The lesion is reported without the physis.
- A bilateral developmental appearance is called bilateral disease.
- Containment is not addressed because the cartilage is not assessed.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this lesion is where the chapter's two errors meet, because
the same region of the same condyle produces both the commonest over-call and a
diagnosis that is missed.

The discriminators are the interface, the cartilage and the symmetry, and none of
them is a measurement, which is why experience rather than a threshold separates
the two. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

A child's lesion is reported in the vocabulary of the adult lesion, and the
outlook differs enough that the shared vocabulary misleads the referrer rather
than the radiologist. 5.11.6 covers OCD in the mature knee.

Serial imaging answers what a single study cannot, since a lesion that is
healing and a lesion that is static look similar on one occasion and different
across two. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

The useful report gives the site, the interface, the cartilage, the physis and
the comparison with the other knee, and says which appearances it regards as
developmental. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- One region produces both the commonest over-call and a missed diagnosis.
- None of the discriminators is a measurement.
- The child's lesion is reported in the adult lesion's vocabulary.
- Healing and static lesions differ across two studies rather than on one.
- The report says which appearances it regards as developmental.
