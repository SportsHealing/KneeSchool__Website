# MCL Sprains

The injury that heals, which is why it is reported loosely, and the site along
the ligament is the useful fact imaging gives.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A medial collateral ligament sprain is injury to the superficial ligament,
graded clinically by the laxity it produces. 2.9.1 covers the superficial MCL.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows oedema around and within the ligament, with or
without discontinuity. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

It matters because most of these heal without an operation, which is why the
reporting is loose and why the exceptions are missed. 5.20.4 covers reporting
MCL injuries.

### How It Is Acquired

Assess the superficial ligament along its whole length on coronal images. 2.9.1
covers the superficial MCL.

State the site: femoral, mid substance or tibial. 3.9.3 covers the superficial
MCL function.

Assess the deep layer separately in the same read. 5.9.3 covers deep MCL
injuries.

Assess the posterior oblique ligament, which decides the management more than
the superficial grade does. 5.9.4 covers POL injuries.

Assess the cruciates and the lateral compartment bone bruising. 3.9.10 covers
the combined ACL and MCL injury.

### What Normal Looks Like

A continuous superficial ligament of even thickness. 2.9.1 covers the
superficial MCL.

No oedema in the soft tissue around it. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

A thin bursa with no more than trace fluid. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

A deep layer attached to the meniscus. 2.9.2 covers the deep MCL.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Oedema around an intact ligament, which is the mildest appearance. 5.5.9 covers
the ligaments.

Partial disruption with some fibres continuous. 2.9.1 covers the superficial
MCL.

Complete discontinuity, usually with retraction. 3.9.3 covers the superficial
MCL function.

Injury at the tibial attachment, which is slower to settle. 2.9.7 covers the
surgical anatomy.

Displacement of the ligament's distal end over the pes tendons, which prevents
healing. 2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

### Key Learning Points

- Most of these injuries heal without an operation.
- The site along the ligament is the useful imaging fact.
- A tibial sided injury is slower to settle.
- A displaced distal end prevents healing.
- The posterior oblique ligament matters more than the grade.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer gives the site and then the exceptions.

The expected points are the whole length, the site, the deep layer, the
posterior oblique ligament, and the cruciates.

Say that the clinical grade comes from the valgus examination. 4.12.1 covers the
valgus stress testing.

Say that laxity at full extension is the finding that changes management. 4.12.2
covers the assessment in full extension.

Say that a displaced distal end is the one appearance arguing against healing.
2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the ligament has a good blood supply and a broad
attachment, so it heals in most patients, and the exceptions are anatomical
rather than a matter of degree. 3.9.3 covers the superficial MCL function.

A tibial sided injury heals more slowly than a femoral one, which is a
prognostic statement the imaging supports. 2.9.7 covers the surgical anatomy.

A distal end displaced superficial to the pes cannot appose, and is the
recognised reason a sprain does not heal. 2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

Combined injury with the anterior cruciate changes the whole management, and the
medial injury's grade becomes secondary. 3.9.10 covers the combined ACL and MCL
injury.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging shows this ligament reliably and adds little to a
diagnosis the examination already makes.

Its best contribution is the site, the displaced distal end, and the other
structures. 2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

It is limited because the grade is clinical, because most of these heal
regardless, and because the appearance lags the recovery. 4.12.1 covers the
valgus stress testing.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no grading
thresholds or healing intervals appear either: those are clinical grades and
published quantities and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament heals in most patients.
- The exceptions are anatomical rather than a matter of degree.
- A tibial sided injury heals more slowly.
- A displaced distal end cannot appose.
- The appearance lags the recovery.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the scan is requested for the other structures rather than for
this ligament. 3.9.10 covers the combined ACL and MCL injury.

A displaced distal end is the one finding here that changes the operation. 2.9.5
covers the semimembranosus.

A posterior oblique injury alongside takes the knee out of the healing group.
5.9.4 covers POL injuries.

The medial meniscus and its attachment belong in the same read. 5.9.3 covers
deep MCL injuries.

Chronic valgus laxity with a thickened ligament is a reconstruction discussion.
5.9.7 covers the post-operative medial reconstructions.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting a grade from the images. 4.12.1 covers the
valgus stress testing.

Bursal fluid is counted as ligament injury. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

Resolving oedema is read as healing in a ligament that is lax. 4.12.3 covers the
assessment in flexion.

A chronic thickened ligament is reported as normal. 2.9.1 covers the superficial
MCL.

The posterior structures are not assessed, because the superficial injury
explained the pain. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- A grade cannot be read from the images.
- Bursal fluid is counted as injury.
- Resolving oedema is not restored competence.
- A chronic ligament reads as normal.
- The posterior structures go unassessed.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the sprain healed and the knee is still unstable. 3.9.2
covers the rotational stability.

That points at the posterior oblique ligament and the corner rather than at this
ligament. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

A combined injury treated as an isolated cruciate reconstruction leaves medial
laxity that loads the graft. 3.9.10 covers the combined ACL and MCL injury.

The original study usually shows the layers that were not reported. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

Ultrasound can follow the superficial ligament's healing and is operator
dependent. 5.4.9 covers the MCL.

The useful report gives the site, the layers, the displaced end if present, the
meniscal attachment and the cruciates. 5.20.4 covers reporting MCL injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Persisting instability points behind this ligament.
- Medial laxity loads a cruciate graft.
- The original study shows the unreported layers.
- Ultrasound follows healing and is operator dependent.
- The report names the site and the layers.
