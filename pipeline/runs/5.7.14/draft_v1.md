# Revision ACL Imaging

The knee being imaged before a revision, where the study has to answer questions
the primary study never asked.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A revision study is done to plan a second reconstruction, so it describes the
existing tunnels, the existing hardware and everything that might have caused the
failure. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

It covers more than the graft: the tunnels, the menisci, the cartilage, the
collaterals, the corners and the alignment. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner
injuries.

It matters because a revision fails for the same reasons a primary does, and the
study is where those reasons are found before the operation rather than after.
6.8.1 covers graft failure.

### How It Is Acquired

Establish the previous operations and the hardware in place, because the study
cannot be planned without them. 5.17.1 covers post-operative cruciate
reconstruction.

Use a protocol that accommodates metal, since hardware artefact sits over the
tunnels. 5.5.3 covers the post-operative protocol.

Assess tunnel position and width, which decide whether the revision can be
single stage. 5.7.12 covers tunnel assessment.

Assess the menisci and cartilage, because their state limits what a revision can
achieve. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Obtain the alignment and slope from radiographs, which the magnetic resonance
study cannot supply. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Tunnels wider than their apertures, which is widening and affects the plan.
5.7.12 covers tunnel assessment.

Tunnels positioned where the new tunnel needs to go, which is the convergence
problem. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Meniscal loss, extrusion and cartilage loss in the compartments. 5.6.11 covers
meniscal extrusion.

Injury or laxity in the collaterals and corners, which is a cause of failure
rather than an incidental finding. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- A revision study describes tunnels, hardware and the causes of failure.
- It covers the menisci, cartilage, collaterals, corners and alignment.
- Tunnel position and width decide whether a revision can be single stage.
- Alignment and slope come from radiographs.
- A revision fails for the same reasons a primary does.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the study where the expected answer lists what the surgeon
needs rather than what the scanner shows.

The expected points are the previous operations, the metal protocol, tunnel
position and width, the compartments, and the alignment radiographs.

Say that you would ask for computed tomography for the tunnels. 5.16.8 covers
three dimensional reconstructions.

Say that the secondary restraints are assessed as causes of failure. 5.10.3
covers posterolateral corner injuries.

Say that the tibial slope needs a lateral radiograph. 5.3.2 covers the lateral
view.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a revision has to put a graft somewhere the bone
will hold it, so the existing tunnels are an obstacle with a geometry that must
be known in advance. 5.7.12 covers tunnel assessment.

Converging or widened tunnels mean the new tunnel has no sound bone, which is
why a staged bone graft exists as an option. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

An unaddressed secondary restraint or an abnormal slope reproduces the loading
that failed the first graft, so correcting the cruciate alone repeats the
experiment. 3.7.3 covers the secondary restraints.

Compartment state sets the ceiling on what the revision can deliver, which is a
conversation with the patient rather than a technical point. 5.11.2 covers
chondral defects.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging supplies the soft tissue half of the revision plan and
computed tomography supplies the bone half.

Its best contribution is the menisci, the cartilage and the secondary restraints,
which are what no other study shows. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

It is limited by hardware artefact over the tunnels, and by tunnel geometry being
answered better elsewhere. 5.2.19 covers artefacts.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no tunnel
width threshold appears either, because the thresholds that separate single stage
from staged revision are published quantities this environment cannot check.

### Key Learning Points

- A revision needs a graft placed where bone will hold it.
- Converging or widened tunnels leave no sound bone for the new tunnel.
- An unaddressed restraint or slope repeats the loading that failed the graft.
- Compartment state sets the ceiling on what the revision can deliver.
- Magnetic resonance supplies the soft tissue half and computed tomography the bone.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the revision study decides the number of operations as well as
the operation.

Widened or converging tunnels make the revision staged, and that is settled
before the patient is listed rather than in theatre. 6.8.2 covers tunnel
malposition.

An abnormal tibial slope or a varus limb makes the alignment part of the plan,
and no cruciate revision alone addresses it. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

An unaddressed corner makes the revision a combined procedure. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

Established compartment degeneration changes the conversation from a revision to
what the knee can be expected to do. 5.3.16 covers Kellgren-Lawrence grading.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading the study as a graft study, so the causes of
failure go unreported. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

Tunnel width is judged through hardware artefact instead of on computed
tomography. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional reconstructions.

The slope and the alignment are not requested, so two recognised causes of
failure are absent from the assessment. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

The corners are not examined, and a combined injury is revised as an isolated
one. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

The compartment state is noted without being connected to what the revision can
achieve. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

### Key Learning Points

- Read as a graft study, it omits the causes of failure.
- Width judged through hardware artefact is not a measurement.
- Slope and alignment are recognised causes and go unrequested.
- A combined injury gets revised as an isolated one.
- Compartment state is noted without being connected to the plan.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the revision study is the point at which this chapter's
argument becomes a practical failure rather than an academic one.

The information the surgeon needs is spread across three studies, magnetic
resonance, computed tomography and standing radiographs, and no convention
requires all three before a revision is planned. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional
reconstructions.

The thresholds that decide staged against single stage revision come from small
series with different measurement methods, so the decision is made on judgement
dressed as a measurement. 5.21.2 covers the cruciate imaging literature.

Rotational laxity is the commonest failure mode and the least imaged, so the
cause of the first failure is frequently unmeasured when the second operation is
planned. 3.7.7 covers rotational stability.

Tibial slope's contribution to repeated failure is increasingly accepted and the
lateral radiograph that measures it is still not routine with a revision work
up. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

The honest summary is that revision planning asks more of imaging than any other
decision in this chapter and receives the least organised answer. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The needed information is spread across three studies with no convention binding them.
- The staging thresholds come from series with different measurement methods.
- The commonest failure mode is the least imaged.
- Tibial slope is accepted as a cause and its radiograph is not routine.
- Revision planning asks the most of imaging and gets the least organised answer.
