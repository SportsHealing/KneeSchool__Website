# Location of Pain

Where the patient points, what each region implies, and why the answer is less
reliable than it feels.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Asking where the pain is tests which compartment or structure is likely to be
involved, and it is the first question that narrows the differential.

The knee divides usefully into anterior, medial, lateral and posterior pain,
and each region has a short list of causes.

The reliability of the answer varies with how it is obtained, which is why the
method below matters more than on most pages.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the patient to point with one finger to where it hurts most.

Do not offer regions. A patient asked whether it is on the inside will
frequently agree.

Ask whether the pain stays there or moves, since migrating pain behaves
differently from fixed pain.

Ask whether there is more than one site, and if so which came first.

Then palpate the site they indicated and compare it with where tenderness
actually is. 4.6.1 covers systematic palpation.

### What a Positive Means

Anterior pain, worse on stairs, squatting and rising from sitting, points to
the patellofemoral joint. 4.15.9 covers it.

Medial joint line pain with a twisting mechanism points to the medial
meniscus. 4.9.2 covers joint line tenderness.

Lateral pain in a runner points more often to the iliotibial band than to the
lateral meniscus.

Posterior pain points to a cyst, a hamstring origin, or a posterior meniscal
lesion, and needs the popliteal fossa examined. 4.6.10 covers it.

Diffuse pain the patient cannot localise points away from a single structural
lesion.

### Key Learning Points

- Ask the patient to point with one finger, and never offer a region.
- Anterior pain on stairs and rising points to the patellofemoral joint.
- Medial joint line pain with a twisting mechanism points to the medial meniscus.
- Lateral pain in a runner is more often the iliotibial band than the meniscus.
- Diffuse pain the patient cannot localise points away from a single lesion.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

At this level the location is combined with the mechanism and the age to
produce a working diagnosis before the knee is touched.

Medial pain in a young patient after a twist is a meniscal tear. Medial pain
in an older patient with stiffness is degenerative disease. The location is
identical.

Anterior pain in an adolescent is patellofemoral or, with tenderness over the
tubercle, an apophysitis. 4.16.4 covers it.

Lateral pain after a varus injury raises the lateral collateral and the
posterolateral structures. 4.13.2 covers the collateral.

Pain in a region with no tenderness in that region should raise referral from
the hip or the spine. 4.2.15 covers referred pain.

### How Good It Is

Location performs moderately. It narrows the differential reliably and
identifies the structure poorly, because several structures share each region.

It performs best for the patellofemoral joint, where anterior pain with the
characteristic provoking activities is reasonably discriminating.

It performs worst in the degenerate knee, where pain is commonly diffuse and
the compartment with the worst radiographic change is not reliably the painful
one.

Tenderness on examination outperforms the patient's report of location for
identifying the structure, which is the opposite of the instability case.

### Key Learning Points

- Combine location with mechanism and age before touching the knee.
- The same medial pain means a tear in a young patient and degeneration in an older one.
- Location narrows the differential reliably and identifies the structure poorly.
- In a degenerate knee the worst radiographic compartment is not reliably the painful one.
- Examination tenderness outperforms the reported location for identifying the structure.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

The decision this question informs is which compartment to treat, and in
unicompartmental disease it carries real weight.

A patient with medial radiographic change and medial pain is a candidate for a
medial procedure. The same radiographs with diffuse or anterior pain are a
different conversation. 4.18.3 covers varus disease.

Pain in a compartment the imaging says is normal is a reason to look again
rather than a reason to dismiss the patient.

Pain in more than one compartment, in a knee being considered for a partial
replacement or an osteotomy, is among the more useful predictors of
dissatisfaction. 4.20.7 covers partial replacement assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting a region rather than a point. Medial covers
the joint line, the collateral, the pes anserinus and the posteromedial
corner, and these have different treatments. 4.16.7 covers pes anserinus
pathology.

The second is the leading question, which is why the one finger instruction
appears on this page and on 4.2.1.

The third is ignoring the hip. Anterior thigh and knee pain from the hip is
common and the knee examination will be normal.

And pain that has moved from one region to another is usually a compensation
rather than a second diagnosis, which is a history finding with no imaging
equivalent.

### Key Learning Points

- In unicompartmental disease the site of pain carries real weight for which compartment to treat.
- Pain in more than one compartment predicts dissatisfaction after a partial procedure.
- Medial covers four different structures with different treatments, so take a point not a region.
- Anterior knee pain from the hip is common and the knee will examine normally.
- Pain that has migrated is usually a compensation rather than a second diagnosis.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

After surgery the location of pain is frequently the most useful single piece
of history, because it points at what was done rather than at what was there.

Pain over a graft harvest site, a tunnel, a fixation device or a scar is
attributable to the operation and is frequently reported as knee pain without
qualification. 4.3.7 covers surgical scars.

Anterior pain after a reconstruction raises the harvest site, patellofemoral
loading from quadriceps deficit, or a cyclops lesion, and these need
separating before anything structural is pursued.

Pain at the joint line after a meniscal repair may be the repair rather than a
new tear, and the timing relative to rehabilitation milestones usually settles
it.

In a painful arthroplasty, the location is one of the few history features
that discriminates, and diffuse pain across the whole knee is more suggestive
of infection or sensitisation than of a mechanical cause. 4.20.8 covers the
assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- After surgery the site of pain points at what was done rather than at what was there.
- Harvest site, tunnel, device and scar pain are all reported simply as knee pain.
- Joint line pain after a repair may be the repair, and the rehabilitation timing settles it.
- In a painful arthroplasty, diffuse pain suggests infection or sensitisation over a mechanical cause.
