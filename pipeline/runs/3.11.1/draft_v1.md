# Iliotibial Band Function

A sheet running the length of the thigh to the tibia. At the knee it resists
varus and rotation, and its line of action reverses as the knee bends.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A long sheet of tissue crossing a joint on one side resists that side opening,
like a ligament with a much wider attachment.

If the sheet passes in front of a joint's axis in one position and behind it
in another, its pull reverses as the joint moves.

A pull that reverses is doing opposite things at different joint angles, which
is unusual and clinically important.

### At the Knee

The iliotibial band runs from the iliac crest and the tensor fascia lata down
the lateral thigh to Gerdy's tubercle on the tibia. 2.11.1 covers the anatomy.

At the knee it contributes to varus restraint through much of the arc, more in
flexion than in extension. 3.10.1 covers varus stability.

Its line of action passes anterior to the flexion axis near extension and
posterior to it in flexion, so it reverses at around 20 to 30 degrees. 3.3.7
covers the instant centre.

That reversal is what reduces a subluxated knee during the pivot shift. 3.7.9
covers the pivot shift.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which is what the reversal depends on.

Tension in the band, which the tensor fascia lata and gluteus maximus control.

Attachment integrity at Gerdy's tubercle and the deeper fibres. 3.11.2 covers
the Kaplan fibres.

Rotation, which the band resists in combination with varus.

### Clinical Relevance

The band is what makes the pivot shift a reduction rather than only a
subluxation, which is why the test depends on it. Section 4 covers the tests.

It is the tissue used in a lateral tenodesis, which is why its anatomy matters
surgically. 3.11.7 covers the tenodesis.

Its deeper attachments to the femur are a separate structure functionally,
which is the subject of the next page. 3.11.2 covers them.

Iliotibial band syndrome is a different problem, concerning friction rather
than restraint. 6.69 covers tendinopathy around the knee.

### Key Learning Points

- A long sheet crossing a joint resists that side opening.
- The iliotibial band runs to Gerdy's tubercle on the tibia.
- Its line of action reverses at around 20 to 30 degrees of flexion.
- That reversal is what reduces a subluxated knee in the pivot shift.
- It is the tissue used in a lateral tenodesis.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The band is best described as having a superficial and a deep component with
different mechanical roles. The superficial layer runs to the tibia and the
deep fibres anchor to the femur. 3.11.2 covers the deep fibres.

Only a structure anchored to the femur can restrain tibial rotation relative
to the femur. A structure running from pelvis to tibia restrains the limb
rather than the joint.

That distinction is the mechanical content of the anterolateral debate: which
part of the complex is the restraint.

### At the Knee

The superficial band inserts at Gerdy's tubercle and is continuous with the
lateral retinaculum anteriorly. 2.11.1 covers the anatomy.

Conventional teaching gives it a contribution to varus restraint that
increases with flexion as the lateral collateral slackens. 3.10.3 covers that
ligament.

Its reversal of line of action about the flexion axis is the mechanism of the
pivot shift's reduction phase, and the reduction angle corresponds to the
crossover. 3.7.9 covers the test.

Tension is modulated actively by the tensor fascia lata and gluteus maximus,
so the band is a dynamic as well as a passive structure.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, through the reversal.

Proximal muscle activity, which sets the tension.

Whether the deep femoral attachments are intact. 3.11.2 covers them.

Previous harvest for a tenodesis or a graft, which removes part of it.

### How It Is Measured

Sectioning studies with varus and rotational loads apportion its contribution,
with the superficial and deep components often divided together, which
confounds the figures.

Cadaveric length change measurement maps the reversal about the flexion axis.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates the band and its attachments. 5.10
covers imaging.

Electromyography of the proximal muscles describes the dynamic component
indirectly.

### Clinical Relevance

Separate the superficial band from the deep femoral attachments when reading a
study.

Expect its varus contribution to rise with flexion as the collateral slackens.

Attribute the pivot shift's reduction to the band's reversal about the flexion
axis.

Account for the dynamic component when the band's tension is in question.

### Key Learning Points

- Superficial and deep components have different mechanical roles.
- Only a femoral anchor can restrain tibial rotation relative to the femur.
- That distinction is the mechanical content of the anterolateral debate.
- The reduction angle in the pivot shift corresponds to the line of action crossover.
- Sectioning studies often divide both components together, which confounds the figures.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the band as the tissue a tenodesis uses and the deep fibres as the
structure a tenodesis is trying to reproduce, which are two different
statements that get conflated. 3.11.7 covers the tenodesis.

Conventional teaching holds that a tenodesis using a strip of the band, routed
deep to the lateral collateral and fixed to the femur, restrains internal
rotation effectively, and that the restraint depends on the femoral fixation
rather than on the tissue used. 3.11.5 covers internal rotation control.

Harvesting a strip does not produce a measurable varus deficit in reported
series, which is the mechanical reason the harvest is acceptable.

### What Changes It

Whether the construct is fixed to the femur, which decides what it restrains.

Routing relative to the lateral collateral, which changes the line of action.

Graft tension and the flexion angle at fixation.

The width of strip harvested.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares tenodesis constructs for restored rotational
restraint against the intact knee.

Varus stress radiography measures whether the harvest produced a coronal
deficit.

Instrumented pivot shift measures the clinical effect without a threshold.

Registry and trial reinjury data measures the outcome the mechanism predicts.

### Clinical Relevance

Fix a tenodesis to the femur, since femoral fixation is what makes it a joint
restraint.

Route deep to the lateral collateral to reproduce the intended line of action.

Expect no measurable varus deficit from a strip harvest.

Keep the tissue used and the structure reproduced as separate statements.

### Key Learning Points

- The tissue a tenodesis uses and the structure it reproduces are different statements.
- The restraint depends on the femoral fixation rather than on the tissue used.
- Routing deep to the lateral collateral sets the line of action.
- A strip harvest does not produce a measurable varus deficit in reported series.
- Femoral fixation is what makes a tenodesis a joint restraint.
