# Principles of Knee Assessment

Five principles that make a knee examination worth doing, and the one that is
ignored most often.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Five principles govern every examination in this section, and learning them is
worth more than learning any individual test.

Compare with the other side. The unaffected knee is the only normal you have
for this patient, and published normal ranges are populations rather than
people.

Examine the joint above and below. Hip pathology refers to the knee reliably,
and in a child it is the first thing to exclude. 4.19 covers that version.

Work from least to most uncomfortable, because pain and guarding destroy the
information every later test depends on.

Put the patient in a position where the muscle can relax. A test that requires
a relaxed hamstring and is performed on a tense one measures the hamstring.

And test one thing at a time. A manoeuvre that loads three structures at once
tells you that something moved.

### How It Is Performed

Expose both knees fully and look at the patient standing, walking and lying,
because each position shows something the others hide.

Standing shows alignment under load and any thrust during stance. 4.5 covers
alignment.

Walking shows what the knee does when the patient is not thinking about it,
which is often different from what it does when they are. 4.4 covers gait.

Lying lets the muscle relax and is where laxity testing becomes possible.

Stop and position again rather than pressing on through a tense examination,
because the information does not improve with persistence.

### Key Learning Points

- The unaffected knee is the only normal you have for this patient.
- Examine the hip and the ankle, because the knee is a common site of referred pain.
- Work from least to most uncomfortable, since guarding destroys later findings.
- A laxity test on a tense muscle measures the muscle.
- Standing, walking and lying each show something the other two hide.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

At viva level the examination is expected to be a sequence rather than a list,
and the sequence is what is being marked.

Introduce, expose, inspect standing, assess gait, then move to the couch for
palpation, range of motion, effusion and the special tests relevant to the
history.

Say what you are looking for as you do it. An examiner cannot mark a silent
examination and will assume you did not know.

Finish by stating what you would do next, which is usually to examine the hip,
the other knee, or the neurovascular status rather than to request a scan.

### How Good It Is

The principle that most improves a knee examination's reliability is side to
side comparison, and it is the one most often skipped under time pressure.

Absolute laxity measurements vary widely between normal knees, so a figure
means little and a difference means a great deal.

The second most valuable principle is relaxation. The difference between a
relaxed and a tense hamstring on an anterior drawer is larger than the
difference between an intact and a torn ligament.

That is why the Lachman, performed in slight flexion where the hamstrings are
shortened and less able to resist, outperforms the anterior drawer so
consistently.

### Key Learning Points

- A knee examination is marked as a sequence, so run it as one and narrate it.
- Absolute laxity varies between normal knees; a side to side difference is the finding.
- Hamstring tension affects an anterior drawer more than ligament status does.
- The Lachman outperforms the anterior drawer largely because of the position it uses.
- Finish by stating the next step, which is usually another examination rather than a scan.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

The principles matter more at this level rather than less, because the
examinations that change management are the ones where the findings are
subtle.

Grading laxity is where discipline pays. A grade is a comparison against the
other side with an endpoint described, and a grade recorded without an
endpoint is almost uninterpretable later.

Flexion angle is the second discipline. Every laxity test in this section has
an angle attached and the angle is part of the finding rather than part of the
technique. 4.12.3 and 4.14.3 both turn on it.

The third is timing. An examination at presentation, at two weeks and at six
weeks in the same knee produces three different pictures, and the one that
should drive a reconstruction decision is rarely the first.

### Where It Misleads

A principle applied mechanically misleads as readily as one ignored.

Side to side comparison fails where both knees are abnormal, which is common
in generalised laxity and in bilateral degenerative disease, and the clinician
who relies on it alone concludes that both knees are normal.

Examining the joint above fails to help when the hip is also degenerate, which
in an older patient it frequently is. Two positive findings do not tell you
which one is producing the symptom.

And testing one thing at a time is impossible for rotational instability,
where the informative tests are deliberately combined loads. 4.14 is built
around that exception.

### Key Learning Points

- A laxity grade without a described endpoint is close to uninterpretable later.
- Flexion angle is part of the finding, not part of the technique.
- The examination that should drive a reconstruction decision is rarely the first one.
- Side to side comparison fails when both knees are abnormal, which is common.
- Rotational instability testing deliberately breaks the one thing at a time principle.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

The principle that breaks down most completely at this level is that an
examination finding belongs to one structure.

In a multiligament knee the structures have failed together and each test is
now loading an unknown combination, so the examination describes a pattern of
laxity rather than a list of torn structures.

The useful discipline is to record what the knee does rather than what you
think is torn: translation at which angle, rotation at which angle, and where
the endpoint is. The structural attribution can be revised and a well recorded
finding cannot be redone.

Generalised laxity is the other breakdown. A patient whose normal is loose
makes every comparison in this section unreliable, including the side to side
one, and the assessment becomes functional rather than structural.

In both situations the examination's job shifts from diagnosis to
documentation, and the examination under anaesthetic becomes the one that
decides. 4.14.6 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- In a multiligament knee each test loads an unknown combination of failed structures.
- Record what the knee does rather than what you think is torn, because the attribution can be revised and the finding cannot be redone.
- Generalised laxity makes every comparison in this section unreliable, side to side included.
- In both cases the examination's job moves from diagnosis to documentation.
