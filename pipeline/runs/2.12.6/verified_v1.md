# Surgical Anatomy

Where the landmarks are for patellofemoral surgery, how little room there is
at the femur, and which errors the anatomy makes easy.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

Patellofemoral surgery uses three groups of landmarks: the medial femoral
prominences, the patella itself, and the tibial tubercle.

The medial femoral prominences are the adductor tubercle and the medial
epicondyle, the same two 2.9.7 describes. The medial patellofemoral ligament
attaches between them.

The patella's medial border carries the ligament's broad attachment over its
upper third to half.

The tibial tubercle is the distal landmark, where the patellar tendon attaches
and where a realignment procedure works. 2.2.5 covers it.

The trochlea is the fourth landmark and it is cartilage rather than bone,
which makes it harder to use. 2.12.1 covers it.

### Relations

The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses every anterior and
medial approach to this joint.

The medial collateral ligament's femoral attachment lies close to the
patellofemoral ligament's, which 2.9.7 covers.

The genicular arteries run in both retinacula, so a release on either side
divides vessels. 2.12.4 covers them.

The suprapatellar pouch lies deep to the quadriceps tendon above the patella.

### Function

Almost every error in this field is a femoral error. The ligament's length
behaviour through flexion is set at the femur, and a few millimetres changes
it.

The patella is small and sometimes fragile, so what is done to it is
constrained by the bone rather than by the ligament.

Operative technique is Section 7, chapters 7.51 to 7.57. This page covers what
is where.

### Clinical Relevance

The femoral point is confirmed on imaging rather than by feel, because the
landmarks are close together and the consequence of error is large.

Patellar fixation competes with patellar strength, especially in a small or
previously drilled patella.

The saphenous nerve branch crossing the field means numbness is the commonest
minor complication of this surgery.

### Key Learning Points

- Three landmark groups: the medial femoral prominences, the patella, and the tibial tubercle.
- The ligament attaches between the adductor tubercle and the medial epicondyle.
- Almost every error in this field is a femoral error.
- The patella is small and its strength constrains what can be done to it.
- The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses every approach here.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Learn the femoral point as a set of offsets, as on the medial side.
Conventional teaching places it about 10 mm distal and 1 mm anterior to the
adductor tubercle, and about 5 mm proximal and posterior to the medial
epicondyle.

On a true lateral radiograph the point lies just anterior to the posterior
cortical line, between the posterior origin of the medial condyle and the
level of the posterior point of Blumensaat's line. That is the radiographic
description, and it is the one used at operation.

On the patella, the attachment spans the upper third to half of the medial
border. Conventional teaching gives a width of about 20 mm.

The tibial tubercle's position relative to the trochlear groove is the
measurement a distal realignment addresses, and 5.12 owns its imaging.

### Relations

The three medial femoral structures, the collateral ligament, the
patellofemoral ligament and the adductor magnus tendon, lie within about 15 mm
of each other.

The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve runs transversely across the
medial field, as 2.9.7 describes.

The lateral retinaculum carries the lateral genicular vessels, so a lateral
procedure has a vascular cost. 2.12.4 covers it.

The patella's blood supply enters mainly at its inferior pole and from the
anastomosis around it, so anterior and lateral surgery together can compromise
it.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The patella is supplied by the genicular anastomosis, with the main
contribution entering at the inferior pole through the fat pad.

A lateral release divides part of that anastomosis, and a tubercle osteotomy
can affect the inferior pole supply, so the two together are a greater insult
than either alone.

Nerve supply to the anterior knee is diffuse, from medial and lateral
articular branches, which is why anterior knee pain is poorly localised.

### Function

Femoral position sets the graft's length behaviour: too proximal and it
tightens in flexion, too distal and it slackens. 3.12.5 covers the mechanics.

Patellar fixation sets the load distribution across a small bone, so the
number and size of tunnels is a strength question.

Tubercle position sets the direction of the extensor pull, so a distal
procedure changes the vector that the proximal soft tissue has to resist.

### Clinical Relevance

Confirm the femoral point on a true lateral image before fixing anything.

Count the holes in the patella across all operations a patient has had, since
each one reduces the bone's strength.

Consider patellar vascularity before combining a lateral release with anterior
or distal surgery.

Protect the infrapatellar branch, and warn the patient about numbness in
advance rather than afterwards.

### Key Learning Points

- The femoral point is about 10 mm distal and 1 mm anterior to the adductor tubercle.
- On a true lateral it lies just anterior to the posterior cortical line.
- Three medial femoral structures sit within about 15 mm of each other.
- Patellar blood supply enters mainly at the inferior pole through the fat pad.
- Too proximal tightens the graft in flexion; too distal slackens it.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

The femoral field is the crowded one, as it is medially and laterally. Three
attachments within 15 mm, and a radiographic method exists because palpation
is not accurate enough.

The patella is the fragile one. Its size, its supply at the inferior pole and
its history of previous tunnels together set what can be done at the anterior
end.

The tubercle is the one that changes everything else. Moving it changes the
vector the soft tissue resists, so a proximal and a distal procedure are not
independent.

### Function

Treat femoral accuracy as the single most consequential variable. Every other
error here is recoverable and this one is not, because it changes the
construct's behaviour through the whole range.

Combination procedures compound their vascular cost. Lateral release, anterior
work and tubercle osteotomy each take part of the anastomosis, and the patella
has one supply.

Soft tissue cannot correct bone, which 2.12.2 states for the ligament and
which applies to every soft tissue procedure in this chapter.

### Clinical Relevance

Image the femoral point intraoperatively rather than relying on anatomy by
feel.

Plan the whole construct before starting, since proximal and distal procedures
interact through the extensor vector.

Assess previous surgery for holes in the patella and for lateral release, and
weigh the vascular cost before adding to either.

Avoid combining a lateral release with distal realignment without a reason,
because the patella's supply is the thing being spent.

### Key Learning Points

- A radiographic method exists because palpation is not accurate enough at the femur.
- Femoral accuracy is the one error that is not recoverable.
- Combination procedures compound a vascular cost the patella can only pay once.
- Proximal and distal procedures interact through the extensor vector.
- Soft tissue cannot correct bone, in this chapter as in every other.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

The femoral point is the whole operation. Radiographic confirmation exists
because a point identified by palpation is wrong often enough to matter, and
the error is not visible at the time: a graft fixed too proximally feels
correct in extension and binds in flexion, which the patient discovers weeks
later. Checking takes one image.

Patellar fracture is a bone complication wearing a ligament's name. A small,
osteopenic or previously drilled patella is the risk factor, and the anatomy
offers an alternative in the quadriceps tendon attachment that 2.12.3
describes. That choice is made before the incision rather than at the moment
the drill is picked up.

Vascular arithmetic is rarely done. The patella has one anastomosis; lateral
release takes part of it, a tubercle osteotomy threatens the inferior pole
supply, and anterior work takes more. Each is defensible alone and the
combination is what produces an avascular patella, which 6.63 covers.

The soft tissue cannot fix the bone, and this is the point at which that has
to be said rather than implied. A trochlea that does not guide, a tubercle too
far lateral or a patella too high is a bony problem, and a 1 mm sheet
reconstructed perfectly will fail against it. 5.12 holds the measurements that
decide which problem a patient has.

Revision is where the anatomy has already been spent. Previous tunnels, a
divided lateral retinaculum, an altered tubercle and scar across the medial
field together mean the landmarks that remain are the adductor tubercle and
the epicondyle. Plan from those, and 7.57 carries the rest.

Over-constraint presents as pain rather than as stiffness. A construct
tensioned to hold the patella loads the medial facet through every degree of
flexion, and the patient reports anterior knee pain in a knee that no longer
dislocates. That is a successful operation by one measure and a failure by the
one that matters.

### Key Learning Points

- A femoral error is invisible at the time and discovered weeks later; checking takes one image.
- Patellar fracture is a bone complication, and the tendon attachment is the anatomical alternative.
- The patella has one anastomosis and combination procedures spend it.
- A perfectly reconstructed 1 mm sheet fails against a bony problem.
- Over-constraint presents as anterior pain in a knee that no longer dislocates.
