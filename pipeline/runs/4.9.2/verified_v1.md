# Joint Line Tenderness

The most sensitive meniscal finding and the least specific. How to elicit it
properly, and what else is tender in the same place.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Joint line tenderness tests whether the structures at the tibiofemoral joint
line are irritated, and the meniscus is one of several.

It is the most sensitive single finding for a meniscal tear and the least
specific, which makes it a screening finding rather than a diagnostic one.

Its value is in its negative: a knee with no joint line tenderness at all is
less likely to have a symptomatic meniscal tear, with the exception of a root
tear. 4.9.9 covers roots.

### How It Is Performed

Flex the knee to about ninety degrees with the patient supine, which opens the
joint line and brings it under the finger.

Identify the line by palpating from the patellar tendon laterally and medially
until the step between the femur and the tibia is felt.

Palpate along the whole line on both sides, from front to back, and compare
with the other knee.

Press with one finger rather than a hand, because the point matters and the
structures are a centimetre apart.

Ask the patient to say when it is tender rather than watching their face, and
ask them whether that is their pain, which is a different question from
whether it hurts.

### What a Positive Means

Tenderness localised to one point on one joint line, reproducing the patient's
pain, is the useful positive.

Tenderness along the whole line on both sides is unlikely to be a tear and
more likely a generally irritable or degenerate joint.

Tenderness above the line is the femoral condyle or the collateral origin.
Tenderness below it is the tibial attachment or the pes anserinus. 4.16.7
covers the latter.

Posterior tenderness in deep flexion raises the posterior horn or the root.
4.9.9 covers it.

No tenderness anywhere on the line makes a symptomatic tear of the body less
likely.

### Key Learning Points

- The most sensitive meniscal finding and the least specific.
- Flex the knee to about ninety degrees, which opens the line.
- Press with one finger, because the structures are a centimetre apart.
- Ask whether that is their pain, not whether it hurts.
- Tenderness along both whole lines is a degenerate joint rather than a tear.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected technique is flexion to open the line, one finger, the
whole line, both sides, and comparison with the other knee.

The candidate is also expected to name what else is tender there: the
collateral ligament, the coronary or meniscotibial attachment, the pes
anserinus medially, and the iliotibial insertion laterally.

Say that you would distinguish them by the exact level and by whether a valgus
or varus stress reproduces the pain. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Say that joint line tenderness alone is insufficient and name the combination
you would build.

### How Good It Is

Joint line tenderness is the most sensitive clinical finding in the meniscal
group, and that makes it the best single screening finding in this chapter.

It is poorly specific, and the reason is anatomical rather than technical:
several structures share the line and a degenerate joint is tender without a
symptomatic tear.

It performs better medially than laterally, because the lateral line has more
structures crossing it and lateral tenderness has a wider differential.

It performs better in a young knee after an injury than in an older degenerate
knee, which is the pattern throughout this chapter.

No figures are quoted, under decision 023, and the ranking against the
provocative tests is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The best single screening finding in this chapter, and poorly specific.
- Several structures share the joint line, which is why specificity is low.
- Name the collateral, the meniscotibial attachment, the pes anserinus and the iliotibial insertion.
- It performs better medially than laterally and better in young knees than degenerate ones.
- Distinguish the structures by exact level and by whether a stress test reproduces the pain.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level joint line tenderness matters most for localisation rather
than for diagnosis.

A patient with a medial tear on imaging and tenderness only laterally has a
mismatch, and the mismatch is the finding.

A patient with imaging findings in both compartments and tenderness in one has
been given the answer to which compartment is symptomatic, which is the
question a partial procedure depends on. 4.20.7 covers partial replacement
assessment.

Tenderness that has moved between visits points away from a fixed structural
lesion.

In the degenerate knee, tenderness over the whole line is a reason for caution
about any mechanical intervention. 4.9.11 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting tenderness as diagnostic, which the
specificity does not support.

The second is palpating with the knee extended, which closes the line and
makes the examination both harder and less interpretable.

The third is not comparing sides, since many degenerate knees are tender
bilaterally and the comparison is the only thing that makes the finding mean
anything. 4.1.2 covers the principle.

And attributing medial joint line tenderness to the meniscus in a patient
whose pain is reproduced by valgus stress, where the collateral is more
likely. 4.12.7 covers the separation.

### Key Learning Points

- The finding localises rather than diagnoses.
- Tenderness in one compartment with imaging findings in two answers which is symptomatic.
- Tenderness that moves between visits points away from a fixed lesion.
- Palpating with the knee extended closes the line and wastes the examination.
- If valgus stress reproduces the pain, the collateral is more likely than the meniscus.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

After meniscal surgery joint line tenderness loses most of its value, and
knowing that prevents a common error.

Tenderness after a resection is frequent, persists for a long time, and does
not distinguish a new tear from the altered mechanics of a deficient meniscus.
4.9.10 covers deficiency.

Tenderness after a repair during the healing period is expected, and its
presence at six weeks means little while its persistence at six months means
more.

In a knee with a meniscal transplant or scaffold, joint line tenderness is
part of the expected course early and a concern late.

The useful discipline at this level is to record where and when rather than
whether, so the next examination has something to compare against. 4.1.8
covers the record.

### Key Learning Points

- After resection, tenderness is frequent, persistent and non-discriminating.
- After a repair, tenderness at six weeks means little and at six months means more.
- Record where and when rather than whether, so the next examination can compare.
- In a transplant or scaffold, early tenderness is expected and late tenderness is a concern.
