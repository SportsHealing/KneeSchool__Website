# Giving Way

The same words from three different knees. What direction, activity and
aftermath each tell you.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Giving way is the knee failing to support the patient. It is the most common
way instability is reported and it has several mechanisms.

Asking about it tests which mechanism: a joint surface shifting, the extensor
mechanism failing, the patella subluxing, or pain inhibiting the muscle.

4.2.2 covers instability as a whole. This page is about the episode itself and
what its details reveal.

### How It Is Performed

Ask for one specific episode in detail rather than a general account.

Ask what they were doing: turning, stepping down, running in a straight line,
or standing still.

Ask which way the knee went, accepting that many patients cannot say and that
a guess is worse than an admission.

Ask whether they fell, whether the knee swelled afterwards, and how long it
took to settle.

Ask whether they expected it, because a knee that gives way without warning is
a different problem from one that fails during a movement the patient knew was
risky.

### What a Positive Means

Giving way while turning or pivoting points to rotational instability and
therefore to the cruciate or the lateral structures. 4.14.3 covers the
pattern.

Giving way while stepping down stairs or a slope points to the extensor
mechanism or the posterior cruciate. 4.11.6 covers chronic posterior
deficiency.

Giving way with the kneecap moving, usually with the knee bending under load,
points to patellar instability. 4.15.3 covers it.

Giving way in a degenerate knee, without a clear direction and with pain, is
usually pain inhibition rather than structural failure. 4.18.6 covers
functional limitation.

Swelling after the episode suggests a structural event rather than a muscular
one.

### Key Learning Points

- Giving way has several mechanisms and the episode's details separate them.
- Ask for one specific episode rather than a general account.
- Turning points at rotation; stepping down at the extensor mechanism or posterior cruciate.
- The kneecap moving under load points at patellar instability.
- Swelling afterwards suggests a structural event rather than a muscular one.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is from the activity to the loaded structure.

A pivot loads the anterior cruciate and the anterolateral structures, so
giving way on a pivot implicates them.

A step down loads the quadriceps eccentrically and the posterior cruciate, so
giving way there implicates those.

A flexed, loaded, rotating knee loads the patellofemoral joint, so giving way
with a sensation at the front implicates the patella.

Standing still loads almost nothing, so giving way while standing is rarely
structural and points to pain, to a neurological cause, or to something other
than the knee.

### How Good It Is

A clear mechanical episode during a defined activity is strong information and
performs better than any laxity measurement for whether the knee is
functionally unstable.

The patient's report of direction is the weak part. People are poor at
describing which way a joint moved during a frightening event, and a confident
wrong answer is worse than no answer.

So the activity is reliable and the direction is not, which means the history
selects the structures to test and the examination decides between them.
4.14.5 covers the patterns.

The aftermath is reliable: swelling, inability to continue, and time to settle
are all well recalled and all informative.

### Key Learning Points

- Reason from the activity to the structure it loads.
- Giving way while standing still is rarely structural.
- The activity is reliably recalled; the direction is not.
- The history selects which structures to test and the examination decides between them.
- Swelling, inability to continue and time to settle are all well recalled.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the value of this question is that it identifies the
instability a patient actually experiences, which is what an operation is for.

A knee that gives way during work, or during an activity the patient cannot
reasonably give up, is a stronger indication than any grade of laxity.

A knee that gives way only during a sport the patient has already stopped is a
weaker one, and the conversation is about whether they intend to return.

Episodes that are becoming more frequent, or occurring during less demanding
activities, indicate a knee that is deteriorating, and that trajectory matters
more than the current frequency.

An episode with swelling in a knee already known to be cruciate deficient
usually means a new meniscal or cartilage injury rather than simply another
episode.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating all giving way as ligamentous.

Pain inhibition in a degenerate knee produces convincing buckling and does not
respond to stabilisation, and this is the single most common misattribution in
elective practice.

Quadriceps weakness produces the same complaint and responds to
rehabilitation, which is why a wasted thigh changes the interpretation of the
history. 4.3.9 covers muscle wasting.

A neurological cause is rare and catastrophic to miss, and a patient who gives
way without warning in several directions deserves a neurological examination.

And the patient who has stopped giving way because they have stopped moving is
the under-reporting trap this chapter returns to repeatedly. Ask what they no
longer do.

### Key Learning Points

- Instability during an unavoidable activity is a stronger indication than any laxity grade.
- A deteriorating trajectory matters more than the current frequency.
- An episode with swelling in a cruciate deficient knee usually means a new meniscal injury.
- Pain inhibition in a degenerate knee is the commonest misattribution in elective practice.
- A patient who has stopped giving way may simply have stopped moving.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

Giving way after a reconstruction is a referral where the history does most of
the work, and 4.2.2's four causes apply directly: graft failure, an
unaddressed rotational component, quadriceps deficit, and a patellofemoral
cause.

The distinguishing question is whether the episode resembles the original
symptom. A returned original symptom after a defined event suggests graft
failure. A new symptom during pivoting suggests the rotational component was
never addressed.

In a multiligament knee the complaint is usually not giving way at all but a
knee the patient cannot trust in any direction, and that account is more
informative than any attempt to identify a plane. 4.23.6 covers the
assessment.

In a hypermobile patient the account has to be weighed against a baseline that
was never normal, and the useful comparison is with their own other knee and
their own past function. 4.23.3 covers it.

Every discriminating claim here is standard teaching with no figure attached,
under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- Ask whether the episode resembles the original symptom: a return suggests graft failure.
- A new pivoting symptom suggests a rotational component that was never addressed.
- In a multiligament knee the complaint is distrust in every direction, not a plane.
- In a hypermobile patient compare against their own other knee and their own past function.
