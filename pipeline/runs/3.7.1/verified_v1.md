# Functions of the ACL

One primary job and three secondary ones. It stops the tibia sliding forward,
and everything else it does follows from being in the way.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A ligament is a rope between two bones. It does nothing until it is tight, and
then it stops the bones separating any further in that direction.

A rope running diagonally across a joint limits more than one motion, because
moving in several directions can stretch the same rope.

So a ligament has a primary direction, the one it limits first, and secondary
ones it contributes to.

### At the Knee

The anterior cruciate runs from the back and outside of the femoral notch to
the front and middle of the tibia. It therefore resists the tibia moving
forward. 2.7.1 covers the anatomy.

That is its primary function and it is the one worth remembering: the primary
restraint to anterior translation of the tibia. 3.7.2 covers it.

It contributes secondarily to rotational control, because rotation also
stretches it. 3.7.7 covers that.

It carries nerve endings, so it contributes to position sense and to reflex
muscle control. 2.7.5 covers the innervation.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes how tight the ligament is and which part of it is
tight. 3.7.4 covers the bundles.

Muscle activity, since the quadriceps pulls the tibia forward and the
hamstrings pull it back.

The state of the other structures, which share the secondary restraint roles.
3.7.3 covers them.

Bony shape, particularly the backward slope of the tibia, which affects how
much the tibia tends to slide.

### Clinical Relevance

A torn anterior cruciate leaves a knee that can walk and gives way on turning,
which follows from the primary and secondary functions.

The sensation patients describe is rotational rather than a forward slide,
because the coupled rotation is what they feel. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

Reconstruction aims at the primary function and achieves the secondary ones
less completely. 3.7.20 covers the construct.

Knowing the primary function makes the examination findings predictable.
Section 4 covers the tests.

### Key Learning Points

- A ligament does nothing until it is tight.
- The anterior cruciate is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation.
- It contributes secondarily to rotational control.
- It carries nerve endings and contributes to position sense.
- Patients feel the coupled rotation rather than a forward slide.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Primary restraint means the structure that takes the load first in a given
direction at a given angle. Secondary means the structures that take it once
the primary has gone. 3.2.10 covers the distinction.

A ligament's function is therefore directional and angle dependent, and both
have to be stated for a claim about it to mean anything.

Function is measured by taking the structure away and remeasuring, which is
why the apportionment literature is a sectioning literature.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the anterior cruciate roughly 85 per cent of the
restraining force to an anterior drawer in the intact knee, which is the
figure the primary designation rests on.

It is a secondary restraint to internal rotation and to varus and valgus
angulation, which is why a complete tear changes more than one examination
finding.

It guides kinematics as well as limiting them. It contributes to the screw
home mechanism and to the coupled rotation of flexion. 3.3.5 covers the
mechanism.

Its mechanoreceptors contribute to reflex hamstring activation, so the
functional loss after rupture is partly neuromuscular. 3.6.8 covers the
comparable meniscal role.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, through bundle tension. 3.7.5 and 3.7.6 cover the bundles.

Quadriceps and hamstring activity, which load and unload it respectively.

Posterior tibial slope, which increases the anterior shear for a given
compressive load.

Meniscal and anterolateral integrity, which share the secondary roles. 3.7.3
covers them.

### How It Is Measured

Sequential sectioning in cadavers with a robotic or manual rig measures the
apportionment, with the sectioning order affecting the figures.

In vivo strain measurement with an implanted transducer measures what the
ligament actually experiences, in a small number of volunteer studies. 3.7.10
covers strain.

Instrumented laxity testing measures the clinical consequence of losing it
rather than its function.

Histology demonstrates the mechanoreceptors, establishing the sensory role
without quantifying it.

### Clinical Relevance

State a direction and a flexion angle with any claim about what the ligament
does.

Expect a complete tear to change several examination findings, through the
secondary roles.

Treat the functional loss as partly neuromuscular rather than purely
mechanical.

Read an apportionment figure with its sectioning order in mind.

### Key Learning Points

- Primary restraint is directional and angle dependent; state both.
- Conventional teaching gives the anterior cruciate roughly 85 per cent of anterior drawer restraint.
- It is a secondary restraint to rotation and to coronal angulation.
- It guides kinematics as well as limiting them.
- The functional loss after rupture is partly neuromuscular.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the four functions as unequally restorable. A graft restores the
anterior restraint well, the rotational contribution partly, the kinematic
guidance less, and the sensory role hardly at all.

That ranking explains the commonest disappointment after a technically
successful reconstruction: a stable knee that does not feel normal. 3.7.20
covers the construct.

It also explains why outcome depends on rehabilitation as much as on fixation.
The functions a graft does not restore are the ones muscle has to cover. 3.4.7
covers internal moments.

### What Changes It

Tunnel position, which decides how much of the native function the graft
reproduces.

Graft choice and its material properties. 3.7.16 covers grafts.

Concurrent injury to the secondary restraints, which changes what the graft is
being asked to do alone.

Rehabilitation and neuromuscular recovery, which cover the functions the graft
cannot.

### How It Is Measured

Robotic testing of reconstructed cadaveric knees compares a construct against
the intact state in all six degrees of freedom.

In vivo kinematics measures whether motion was restored, in research cohorts
performing prescribed tasks.

Arthrometry and pivot shift measure the clinical restraint, the first
reproducibly and the second without an agreed threshold.

Patient reported outcome measures whether the knee feels normal, which is the
function no instrument captures.

### Clinical Relevance

Counsel that stability and normality are different outcomes, and name which
the operation restores.

Address the secondary restraints where they are also injured, rather than
relying on the graft.

Invest in neuromuscular rehabilitation, since it covers what the graft does
not.

Read a construct study by which of the four functions it measured.

### Key Learning Points

- The four functions are unequally restorable by a graft.
- Anterior restraint restores well, rotation partly, guidance less, sensation hardly at all.
- A stable knee that does not feel normal is the predictable disappointment.
- Muscle has to cover the functions a graft does not restore.
- No instrument captures whether the knee feels normal.
