# Mechanical Symptoms

Catching, locking and giving way from a cartilage lesion, and why the word
locking has to be pinned down before it means anything.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A mechanical symptom is something physically interfering with the joint's
movement: catching, a sense of something moving, a true block to extension, or a
sudden giving way without the knee being unstable.

The history chapter covers these as symptoms. This page covers them as the
finding that changes a cartilage lesion from an observation into a surgical
problem. 4.2.7 covers the history.

The word locking is the problem. Patients use it for stiffness, for pain and for
a true block, and only the last is mechanical. 4.9.8 covers that distinction.

### How It Is Performed

Ask the patient to describe exactly what happens, in their own words, rather
than offering the word locking. 4.2.5 covers locking in the history.

Establish whether the knee has ever been stuck and unable to straighten, and
whether they or somebody else had to do something to free it.

Establish whether anything catches intermittently, and in what position the
catch occurs.

Measure the passive extension and note the end feel, since a springy block is
the examination finding that corresponds to a displaced fragment. 4.7.5 covers
the end feel.

Assess the effusion, since a loose fragment irritates the joint and produces one.
4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Ask about a loose body being felt moving, which is unusual and specific when
described clearly.

### What a Positive Means

A true episode of the knee being stuck, with somebody having to free it,
indicates a mechanical block and that is the finding that matters.

A springy block to passive extension indicates something displaced in the joint
now, and that is urgent. 4.7.5 covers the end feel.

Intermittent catching without a block suggests an unstable flap or a loose
fragment that is not currently interposed. 4.17.3 covers osteochondral lesions.

A description of locking that turns out to mean stiffness is not a mechanical
symptom, and recording it as one sends the patient towards an operation they do
not need. 4.2.5 covers that trap.

Giving way with a mechanical description, in a knee with normal ligaments, can
be a fragment rather than instability. 4.2.6 covers giving way.

### Key Learning Points

- A mechanical symptom is something physically interfering with movement.
- The word locking covers stiffness, pain and a true block, and only the last is mechanical.
- A true episode of being stuck, needing to be freed, is the finding that matters.
- A springy block to passive extension means something is displaced now, and that is urgent.
- Giving way with a mechanical description in a normal ligament knee can be a fragment.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is a history station and the expected answer refuses the word
locking until the patient has described what happens.

The expected points are the patient's own description, the history of a true
block, the position of any catch, the passive extension with its end feel, and
the effusion.

Say that you would not use the word locking in the question, and say why.
4.2.5 covers it.

Say that a springy end feel with a current block is an urgent arthroscopy, which
is the clinically consequential finding. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

Say that a mechanical symptom in a knee with a cartilage lesion changes the
decision, where pain alone frequently does not. 4.17.5 covers that argument.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a displaced fragment physically obstructs the
joint's movement, so the symptom is a mechanical event rather than a pain
response. 4.7.5 covers the end feel it produces.

A fragment that is unstable but not displaced catches intermittently, which is
why the symptom is episodic and the examination between episodes is normal.

An osteochondral fragment is stiffer than a meniscal one, which is why its block
can feel firmer and why the end feel is read rather than assumed. 4.7.5 covers
that reading.

Giving way from a fragment is a reflex response to a sudden mechanical event
rather than a ligamentous failure, which is why the ligament examination is
normal. 4.2.6 covers giving way.

### How Good It Is

The history is the diagnostic instrument here and the examination confirms it
only when a fragment is currently displaced.

A clear description of a true block is specific and is the most useful
information available.

A normal examination between episodes is expected and excludes nothing, which is
why the history carries the weight. 4.2.5 covers it.

Imaging identifies the fragment, and this is a case where the history, the
examination and the imaging each contribute a different part. 4.1.6 covers that
relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- A displaced fragment obstructs movement, so the symptom is a mechanical event rather than a pain response.
- An unstable but undisplaced fragment catches intermittently, so the examination between episodes is normal.
- An osteochondral fragment is stiffer than a meniscal one, so its block can feel firmer.
- Giving way from a fragment is a reflex response rather than a ligamentous failure.
- A clear description of a true block is the most useful information available.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a mechanical symptom is what converts a cartilage lesion into
an operation, and pain alone frequently does not.

A current springy block is an urgent arthroscopy, and the examination makes that
decision. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

A clear history of true locking episodes with an unstable lesion on imaging is a
surgical indication, and the examination's contribution is establishing that the
locking is real. 4.17.3 covers the lesion.

Pain without mechanical symptoms in a patient with a lesion is a much weaker
indication, and the alignment and the functional assessment carry that decision
instead. 4.17.6 covers the functional side.

A reported mechanical symptom that turns out to be stiffness has sent patients
to arthroscopy, which is the specific error this page exists to prevent.
4.2.5 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting the word locking without a description.

The second is reading an extension deficit as a block without the end feel.
4.7.5 covers it.

The third is attributing giving way to a fragment without examining the
ligaments.

The fourth is expecting the examination to be abnormal between episodes.

The fifth is operating on an imaging finding in a patient whose mechanical
history does not hold up.

### Key Learning Points

- A mechanical symptom converts a cartilage lesion into an operation; pain alone frequently does not.
- A current springy block is an urgent arthroscopy.
- Pain without mechanical symptoms is a weak indication, and alignment and function decide instead.
- An extension deficit without the end feel is not a block.
- Accepting the word locking without a description is the commonest error.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the mechanical symptom is the claim that has to be
re-examined, because it is the claim an operation was built on.

A patient with a normal arthroscopy after reported locking had a symptom that
was not mechanical, and the description rather than the imaging was where that
should have been caught. 4.2.5 covers it.

A patient with recurrent true locking after a cartilage procedure has an
unstable graft or an unaddressed fragment, and that is a different problem from
pain. 4.20.5 covers that assessment.

A patient with locking and a previous meniscectomy may have a meniscal rather
than a cartilage cause, and the two coexist in the same compartment. 4.9.10
covers meniscal deficiency.

A patient whose locking episodes stopped after an operation and whose pain did
not has had the mechanical problem treated and the pain problem left, which is a
result worth framing before the operation. 4.17.5 covers the defect.

In medicolegal work a recorded description of a true block is useful evidence,
and a recorded word locking without a description is not. 4.23.7 covers that
setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The mechanical symptom is the claim an operation was built on, so it is re-examined first.
- A normal arthroscopy after reported locking means the symptom was not mechanical.
- Recurrent true locking after a cartilage procedure is an unstable graft or an unaddressed fragment.
- Locking stopping and pain persisting is a result worth framing before the operation.
- A recorded description of a block is evidence; the recorded word locking is not.
