# LCL Assessment

The one knee ligament you can hold between your fingers, and the reason that
makes the lateral examination start with palpation.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The lateral collateral ligament runs from the lateral femoral epicondyle to the
head of the fibula as a discrete cord, outside the joint capsule.

Being a cord rather than a sheet, it can be palpated directly, which no other
major knee ligament allows. 3.10.3 covers its function.

Assessing it therefore has two parts that most ligament assessments do not
have: feeling whether the structure is there and under tension, and testing how
far the joint opens without it. 4.13.1 covers the stress test.

### How It Is Performed

Place the leg in the figure of four position. Flex the knee, externally rotate
the hip and rest the ankle of the affected leg on the opposite shin.

That position tightens the ligament, and it stands out as a cord across the
lateral side of the knee.

Follow it with a finger from the lateral femoral epicondyle down to the fibular
head, noting tenderness and whether the cord is palpable along its length.

Compare with the other knee in the same position, because the prominence of the
cord varies between people.

Then perform varus stress at thirty degrees and in extension, with your fingers
on the lateral joint line. 4.13.1 covers that test.

### What a Positive Means

An absent or non palpable cord in the figure of four position, where the other
side has one, indicates a disrupted ligament.

Tenderness along the cord with a palpable, tense structure and no increased
varus opening is a sprain, which is treated as pain rather than instability.

Increased varus opening at thirty degrees with a soft endpoint indicates a
complete injury.

Tenderness localised to the fibular head raises the question of an avulsion
with a bone fragment, which changes the treatment and is a radiographic
finding. 4.1.6 covers the relationship with imaging.

A disrupted ligament should prompt examination of the common peroneal nerve,
which lies just behind the fibular neck. 4.11.7 covers the sequence.

### Key Learning Points

- It is a cord outside the capsule, which makes it the one major knee ligament you can palpate.
- The figure of four position tightens it and makes it stand out.
- An absent cord where the other side has one indicates disruption.
- Tenderness over a tense cord with no increased opening is a sprain.
- A disrupted ligament prompts examination of the common peroneal nerve.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the figure of four position is the answer, and describing varus
stress without it is an incomplete assessment of this structure.

The expected points are the position, following the cord from epicondyle to
fibular head, the comparison, and then the varus stress at both angles.

Say that you would examine the fibular head and neck specifically, because an
avulsion and a nerve injury both live there.

Say that the ligament is extracapsular, which is why an isolated injury to it
produces little effusion and why a tense haemarthrosis points elsewhere.
4.2.3 covers swelling.

Say that you would perform the dial test, since the structures that matter most
laterally resist rotation rather than varus and this ligament is the lesser
part of the corner. 4.13.5 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the ligament is the principal varus restraint
through mid flexion and contributes less in extension, where the cruciates and
capsule share it. 3.10.1 covers varus stability.

It also contributes to external rotation restraint, which is why a complete
injury increases the dial test and why the corner is assessed as a unit rather
than structure by structure. 3.10.6 covers the corner.

Its extracapsular position means an isolated injury heals poorly, because there
is little to hold the ends in apposition, which is the anatomical reason the
lateral side behaves differently from the medial. 3.10.3 covers its function.

An avulsion from the fibular head with a fragment heals differently again,
because the repair is bone to bone rather than ligament to ligament.

### How Good It Is

Palpation in the figure of four position is the most specific finding in the
lateral examination, because an absent cord is hard to produce any other way.

It is less sensitive than it is specific. A partial injury, or a complete one
in a heavy limb, can leave a palpable structure.

Varus stress adds grading and does not add localisation, because it cannot tell
this ligament from the rest of the corner. 4.13.1 covers that limitation.

The combination of palpation and the dial test is more informative than varus
stress on its own, and that is the practical conclusion of this page.

No figures appear for any of these comparisons, under decision 023, and the
comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Palpation in the figure of four is the most specific finding in the lateral examination.
- It is more specific than sensitive: a partial injury can leave a palpable cord.
- Being extracapsular, it heals poorly, which is why the lateral side differs from the medial.
- An isolated injury produces little effusion, so a tense haemarthrosis points elsewhere.
- Palpation with the dial test is more informative than varus stress alone.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the palpation finding contributes to whether the lateral side
is reconstructed and the corner assessment decides what is reconstructed.

An isolated complete collateral injury is uncommon, and when it is genuinely
isolated it is still usually reconstructed rather than braced, because the
structure does not heal in a useful position.

A fibular head avulsion with a fragment is the one lateral injury with a
straightforward repair, and recognising it from the tenderness and the
radiograph changes the operation.

In the combined injury the collateral is reconstructed as part of the corner
rather than as a decision of its own, and the examination's contribution is to
confirm it is gone. 4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

Alignment is assessed in every case, because a varus limb will stretch any
lateral reconstruction and that is the commonest reason one fails.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not using the figure of four position, which discards
the only direct palpation available in the knee.

The second is reading a palpable cord as an intact corner. The cord is the
smaller part of the lateral problem.

The third is attributing lateral pain to the ligament when the structure is
tense and non tender on the other side too, which is normal variation.

The fourth is missing a fibular head avulsion, which is a radiographic finding
the examination should ask for rather than exclude.

The fifth is finding a disrupted ligament and not examining the nerve.

### Key Learning Points

- An isolated complete injury is usually reconstructed, because the structure does not heal usefully.
- A fibular head avulsion with a fragment is the one lateral injury with a straightforward repair.
- In a combined injury the collateral is reconstructed as part of the corner.
- A palpable cord does not mean an intact corner.
- A varus limb will stretch any lateral reconstruction, which is the commonest reason one fails.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this ligament is usually intact or already reconstructed,
and the examination is answering whether the rest of the corner was addressed.

A reconstructed collateral with a persistent rotational abnormality means the
popliteus complex was not reconstructed, and that is the commonest incomplete
lateral operation. 4.13.3 covers the popliteus.

A reconstructed collateral in an uncorrected varus limb stretches, and finding
it lax at two years with good surgery in the notes usually means the alignment
was the missing step.

In a knee with a previous lateral extra-articular tenodesis the lateral soft
tissue anatomy is altered and the cord may not be palpable where expected, so
an absent cord is less informative. 3.11.7 covers the procedure.

A patient with a healed fibular head avulsion can have a palpable but
non functional ligament, because the fragment united in a displaced position
and the ligament is slack. The palpation is reassuring and the varus test is
not, and the varus test is the one to believe.

In medicolegal work a recorded absent cord is strong evidence of a complete
injury at that time, and it is one of the few lateral findings that carries
that weight. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A reconstructed collateral with persistent rotational abnormality means the popliteus was not addressed.
- A reconstruction in an uncorrected varus limb stretches, whatever the surgery.
- Previous lateral extra-articular surgery makes an absent cord less informative.
- A united but displaced avulsion gives a palpable, slack ligament, and the varus test is the one to believe.
- A recorded absent cord is strong evidence of a complete injury at that time.
