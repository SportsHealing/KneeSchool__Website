# Paediatric ACL Injury

The cruciate injury in a growing knee, where the examination is the same and
the consequences of the finding are not.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A cruciate tear in a child presents as it does in an adult, with a twisting
injury, an immediate effusion and a feeling of the knee giving way. 4.10.1
covers the history of the injury.

The examination is the same set of tests, and the difference is that a positive
finding in a growing knee leads to a different discussion. 4.10.5 covers the
anterior drawer test.

The first task is separating a cruciate injury from a physeal injury, since
both follow a twisting injury and both produce an effusion. 4.19.1 covers the
growth plate assessment.

### How It Is Performed

Establish the age, the maturity and the mechanism, because a twisting injury in
a young child is more likely to involve the physis or a tibial spine. 4.19.6
covers developmental variants.

Assess the effusion, since an immediate haemarthrosis after a twisting injury
narrows the differential sharply. 4.8.7 covers the acute haemarthrosis.

Palpate the physeal lines before any stress test, as in any immature knee.
4.6.1 covers systematic palpation.

Perform the Lachman test, which is the most informative single test and the
gentlest of the anterior tests. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

Perform the anterior drawer if the Lachman is equivocal, accepting that it is
less sensitive. 4.10.5 covers it.

Defer the pivot shift in an acute injury, since it needs a relaxed knee and it
is uncomfortable. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift.

Assess the collaterals and the posterior structures gently, so a combined
injury is not missed. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

### What a Positive Means

An increased anterior excursion with a soft end point on the Lachman test
indicates a cruciate injury, in a child as in an adult. 4.10.4 covers the test.

A haemarthrosis with a twisting mechanism and a normal Lachman raises a tibial
spine avulsion instead, which is the paediatric equivalent. 4.10.1 covers the
history of the injury.

Physeal tenderness with anterior laxity means both structures may be injured
and the physis is examined first. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

A positive finding in a skeletally immature knee changes the treatment timing
and the technique rather than the diagnosis. 4.20.1 covers the reconstructed
knee.

A normal examination with a convincing history in a child deserves repeating,
since guarding hides laxity. 4.10.4 covers the test.

### Key Learning Points

- The tests are the same as in an adult and the consequences differ.
- A twisting injury in a young child may involve the physis or a tibial spine.
- The Lachman is the most informative and the gentlest anterior test.
- The physis is palpated before any stress test.
- A guarded examination hides laxity and deserves repeating.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the adolescent knee injury station and the expected answer
covers maturity, the physis and the tibial spine before the ligament.

The expected points are the age and maturity, the mechanism, the haemarthrosis,
the physeal palpation, the Lachman, the drawer, the deferred pivot shift, the
collaterals and the decision on imaging.

Say that you would assess skeletal maturity clinically and confirm it, since it
determines the treatment options. 4.19.6 covers developmental variants.

Say that you would consider a tibial spine avulsion in a younger child with the
same history. 4.10.1 covers the history of the injury.

Say that you would defer the pivot shift acutely and perform it later, with the
reason. 4.10.6 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the cruciate's bony attachments are stronger
than the ligament in an adult and relatively weaker in a child, so the failure
moves to the bone. 2.7.7 covers the tibial footprint.

A tibial spine avulsion is therefore the younger child's version of the same
injury, and it produces the same laxity with a different lesion. 4.10.1 covers
the history of the injury.

The Lachman tests the ligament at a position where the hamstrings cannot easily
resist, which is why it outperforms the drawer in a guarding patient. 4.10.4
covers the test.

Skeletal maturity governs the treatment because a reconstruction crossing an
open physis risks a growth disturbance. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate
assessment.

### How Good It Is

The Lachman test performs as it does in an adult in a cooperative child and
poorly in a guarding one.

Its best contribution is the same as in the adult knee, which is detecting
anterior laxity with a single gentle manoeuvre. 4.10.4 covers the test.

It cannot distinguish a midsubstance tear from a tibial spine avulsion, since
both produce anterior laxity.

Magnetic resonance imaging distinguishes them, so the examination establishes
the laxity and the imaging establishes the lesion. 4.1.6 covers that
relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The cruciate's bony attachment is relatively weaker in a child.
- A tibial spine avulsion is the younger child's version of the same injury.
- The Lachman works at a position where the hamstrings cannot easily resist.
- Skeletal maturity governs the treatment, not the diagnosis.
- The examination cannot separate a tear from an avulsion.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the examination establishes the laxity and the maturity, and
the combination of those two decides the pathway.

An immature knee with anterior laxity is a different decision from a mature
one, and the examination contributes the laxity while imaging contributes the
physeal status. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Associated meniscal injury changes the urgency, and the examination for joint
line tenderness and a block to extension is how it is raised. 4.9.3 covers the
McMurray test.

A tibial spine avulsion with a block to extension is urgent, and the block is a
clinical finding. 4.5.4 covers the flexion deformity.

Recurrent giving way in a child with laxity is a functional finding that moves
the decision, and it is asked for rather than assumed. 4.2.6 covers giving way.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is examining the ligament and not the physis, so a physeal
injury is called a cruciate injury. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Guarding in an acute paediatric knee produces false reassurance on the Lachman,
and the examination is repeated rather than trusted once. 4.10.4 covers the
test.

Physiological laxity is greater in children, so a soft end point is harder to
call abnormal than in an adult. 4.19.6 covers developmental variants.

A tibial spine avulsion is attributed to a midsubstance tear, and the two have
different urgency.

A combined meniscal injury is missed because the attention goes to the
ligament. 4.9.3 covers the McMurray test.

### Key Learning Points

- Examining the ligament and not the physis is the commonest error.
- Acute guarding produces false reassurance on the Lachman.
- Physiological laxity is greater in children, so the end point is harder to call.
- An avulsion attributed to a tear loses the difference in urgency.
- A combined meniscal injury is missed when attention goes to the ligament.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the examination's limits in the growing knee are about
degree rather than about presence.

Physiological laxity varies with age and with generalised laxity, so a given
excursion means different things at different ages and the examination has no
age adjusted reference. 4.19.6 covers developmental variants.

A partially torn cruciate in a child can produce a soft end point with a firm
stop, and the clinical distinction between partial and complete is unreliable
here as it is in adults. 4.10.4 covers the test.

The examination cannot assess the physeal status, which is the finding that
governs the operation rather than the laxity. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate
assessment.

Repeat examination under anaesthesia resolves the guarding problem and
introduces its own, because an anaesthetised child's laxity is not the laxity
being treated. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift.

Functional instability in a child is reported by a parent as often as by the
patient, and the two reports differ. 4.2.6 covers giving way.

### Key Learning Points

- Physiological laxity varies with age and the examination has no age adjusted reference.
- Partial and complete tears are not reliably separated clinically.
- The physeal status, not the laxity, governs the operation.
- Examination under anaesthesia removes guarding and changes what is measured.
- Parent and patient reports of instability differ.
