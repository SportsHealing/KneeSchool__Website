# Cartilage Injuries

The only companion in this chapter that is irreversible from the first moment,
which is why it outranks everything else on the report.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

The ends of the bones in the knee are covered with a smooth white surface called
cartilage. It lets the joint slide without friction. 1.2.2 covers the articular
cartilage.

When the knee twists badly, the bones can strike each other hard enough to chip
or bruise that surface. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Unlike a bruise in the bone, damaged cartilage does not grow back. 6.5.3 covers
cartilage degeneration.

### How It Presents

A loose piece of cartilage can make the knee catch or lock, which is a
mechanical feeling rather than a painful one. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Cartilage is the smooth surface covering the bone ends.
- It lets the joint slide without friction.
- A bad twist can chip or bruise it.
- Unlike bone bruising, it does not grow back.
- A loose piece can make the knee catch or lock.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

Articular cartilage covers the ends of the bones. It has no blood supply and
very limited capacity to repair itself. 1.2.2 covers the articular cartilage.

Injury to it at the moment of an ACL tear happens when the two bone surfaces
strike each other. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

### How It Presents

A fragment that has come loose can cause catching, locking or a sensation of
something moving in the joint. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Smaller injuries often cause nothing at the time and declare themselves years
later. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

### Management Overview

A loose fragment that is causing mechanical symptoms is usually dealt with. 6.5.1
covers instability.

A fragment with bone attached may be fixable back into place, which is better
than removing it, and that opportunity is time limited. 6.4.1 covers bone
bruising.

Smaller surface injuries are usually watched rather than treated, because the
available treatments are imperfect and carry their own recovery. 6.5.3 covers
cartilage degeneration.

### Key Learning Points

- Cartilage has no blood supply and limited capacity to repair.
- It is injured when the bone surfaces strike each other.
- A loose fragment can cause catching or locking.
- A fragment with bone attached may be fixable, and that chance is time limited.
- Smaller injuries are usually watched rather than treated.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why is this more serious than the bone bruising?
Because bone bruising heals and cartilage does not. The bruise is a sign of the
force; the cartilage damage is a permanent change to the joint surface.

**Q.** Will it definitely cause arthritis?
No. A small lesion in a well aligned knee with an intact meniscus may never
cause trouble. Risk rises with the size of the lesion, the loss of meniscus and
the loading of the knee.

**Q.** Can cartilage be repaired?
There are procedures that fill a defect with repair tissue or transplanted
tissue. They are not a restoration of normal cartilage, and they carry long
recoveries, so they are used selectively.

**Q.** Should it be treated at the same operation?
Sometimes. A fragment with bone attached is best fixed early. Other lesions are
often assessed at the operation and treated or watched depending on size,
position and what else is being done.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

Articular cartilage is avascular and aneural, with a zonal collagen architecture
that gives it its mechanical properties and no capacity for true regeneration.
5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

Acute chondral and osteochondral injury accompanies cruciate rupture through
direct impaction at the moment of translation. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

An osteochondral fragment carries subchondral bone and therefore has fixation
potential; a purely chondral fragment does not. 5.11.5 covers the osteochondral
lesions.

### Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging identifies defects and assesses the overlying surface
above a bone bruise. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

Delamination, where cartilage separates at its deep zone, is easily missed and
is the pattern most often under-reported. 5.11.4 covers the delamination.

### Management Overview

Fix an osteochondral fragment where feasible, and early. 5.11.5 covers the
osteochondral lesions.

Address mechanical symptoms from a loose body. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Treat or observe a chondral defect according to size, depth and location, and
alongside correction of instability and alignment. 6.5.3 covers cartilage
degeneration.

### Key Learning Points

- Cartilage is avascular and aneural with no true regeneration.
- Injury accompanies rupture through direct impaction.
- An osteochondral fragment has fixation potential and a chondral one does not.
- Delamination is the pattern most often under-reported.
- Treatment follows size, depth, location, instability and alignment.

## MRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer distinguishes the reversible from the
irreversible.

The expected points are the avascular nature, the impaction mechanism, the
osteochondral against chondral distinction, delamination, and the dependence of
any treatment on a stable, aligned knee. 5.11.4 covers the delamination.

Say that this is the only associated injury here that is permanent from the
moment it happens. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Say that cartilage treatment into an unstable knee fails, which is why the
cruciate is addressed with it or before it. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Management Overview

Fix what can be fixed early; treat mechanical symptoms; observe the rest with
instability and alignment corrected. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

### Complications and Prognosis

The long term consequence is compartmental degeneration, and its strongest
co-determinant is meniscal status. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

A knee with cartilage injury and meniscal loss carries a materially different
outlook from one with either alone. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- Distinguish the reversible from the irreversible.
- This is the only associated injury permanent from the moment it happens.
- Cartilage treatment into an unstable knee fails.
- Fix what can be fixed early and observe the rest.
- Cartilage injury with meniscal loss is a different outlook from either alone.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to sequence cartilage treatment
against the other injuries rather than discuss it in isolation.

Instability is corrected first or simultaneously, because no cartilage procedure
survives a knee that gives way. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Alignment is assessed, because a procedure placed into an overloaded
compartment is being asked to fail. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Meniscal status is established, because a compartment without a meniscus loads
any repair tissue differently. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Only then does the lesion's own size, depth and location select the technique.
5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

### Complications and Prognosis

Repair tissue differs from native cartilage by design, so imaging after a
cartilage procedure is read against a different expectation. 5.11.1 covers the
normal cartilage.

Failure of a cartilage procedure in an uncorrected knee is a failure of
selection rather than of technique. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether treating an incidental chondral lesion at
reconstruction improves anything. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The second is whether the cartilage injury found with a cruciate rupture is
caused at impact or develops afterwards. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

The third is which lesions justify the recovery that a restoration procedure
demands. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Sequence cartilage treatment against the other injuries.
- No cartilage procedure survives a knee that gives way.
- A procedure in an overloaded compartment is asked to fail.
- Only then does the lesion select the technique.
- Failure in an uncorrected knee is a selection failure, not a technical one.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this page is where the chapter's hierarchy becomes clear,
and it is not the hierarchy the report implies.

Bone bruising resolves. The medial collateral heals. The ramp and the root can
be repaired. The anterolateral complex can be reconstructed. The cartilage
cannot be restored, only substituted for. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

So the item that appears late in most reports, after the ligament and the
menisci, is the one with the longest consequence, and the reader reaches it
last. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The practical discipline is to read the cartilage surfaces deliberately in every
acute cruciate study, particularly above a severe bone bruise and at the deep
zone where delamination hides. 5.11.4 covers the delamination.

The fellow's error to avoid is treating cartilage as the chronic knee's problem,
because the injury that produces it happens in the acute one. 6.5.4 covers
osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- The report's order is not the hierarchy of consequence.
- Everything else here resolves, heals, is repaired or is reconstructed.
- Cartilage cannot be restored, only substituted for.
- Read the surfaces deliberately, above a bruise and at the deep zone.
- Cartilage is not the chronic knee's problem; the injury happens acutely.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question is how much to say about a finding whose consequence is
decades away and whose course cannot be predicted for an individual.

Saying too much converts a young patient's sports injury into a conversation
about arthritis they did not come for and cannot act on. 6.5.4 covers
osteoarthritis.

Saying nothing leaves them to discover it later, usually from a report or a
search, and to conclude it was concealed. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The workable version names the finding, states that it does not determine the
future, and identifies the two things that do and are partly within reach: the
meniscus and the knee's loading. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The five things set the pitch. The patient's age and physiology, which decide
how many decades the surface must serve. Their medical comorbidities,
particularly body mass, which is the modifiable loading factor. The demands they
put on the knee. Their psychological state, because this is the finding most
likely to frighten. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which often
already include a fear of arthritis from something they have read.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural
history.

### Key Learning Points

- The consequence is decades away and unpredictable individually.
- Saying too much reframes a sports injury as an arthritis conversation.
- Saying nothing invites the conclusion that it was concealed.
- Name it, say it does not determine the future, and name what does.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations set the pitch.
