# Observation from the Door

The part of the examination that happens before you have washed your hands, and
the part most often skipped in favour of the part that is marked.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Observation from the door tests whether the patient's knee limits what they do,
which is a different question from how the joint behaves on a couch.

A patient rising from a chair, walking to the couch and getting onto it performs
three tasks that load the knee in ways no manual test reproduces. 4.4.1 covers
normal gait.

The information is free and it is perishable. Once the patient knows they are
being examined they move differently, which is why this part comes first and
cannot be done later. 4.1.5 covers the common pitfalls.

### How It Is Performed

Watch the patient rise from the chair in the waiting area or the consulting
room, and note whether they use their arms and which leg they load.

Watch them walk towards you, noting the stance time on each side and whether the
knee reaches full extension.

Watch them turn, because turning loads rotation and most knee complaints are
rotational. 4.2.2 covers instability as a symptom.

Watch them get onto the couch, which is a single leg loaded task with a
rotational component, and note which leg leads.

Note the walking aid, the footwear, the brace and the strapping, all of which
say something the history may not.

### What a Positive Means

An asymmetrical rise from a chair indicates that one knee is being avoided, and
avoidance is what the patient's complaint consists of.

A shortened stance time on one side is an antalgic pattern and points at pain
rather than instability. 4.4.2 covers it.

A knee held in flexion through stance suggests either a flexion deformity or an
effusion holding the joint in its most comfortable position. 4.5.4 covers
flexion deformity.

A patient who turns in several small steps rather than pivoting is protecting
rotation, which is the behaviour a cruciate deficient knee produces. 4.10.3
covers the chronic knee.

A brace or strapping worn to a clinic appointment is a statement about
confidence as much as about mechanics, and it is worth asking who advised it.
4.2.32 covers previous physiotherapy.

### Key Learning Points

- Rising, walking, turning and getting onto the couch load the knee in ways no manual test reproduces.
- The information is perishable: a patient who knows they are watched moves differently.
- An asymmetrical rise from a chair indicates a knee being avoided.
- A shortened stance time points at pain; protected turning points at rotation.
- The walking aid, footwear and brace say something the history may not.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the examiner has usually already decided whether you looked, because
the instruction to examine the knee is often given while the patient is
walking in.

The expected points are the rise, the gait, the turn, the transfer to the couch,
and the aids.

Say that you would observe before exposing and before touching, and say why,
because the reason is the content of the answer. 4.1.4 covers the structured
approach.

Say that you would observe both legs and compare, since every finding in this
section is a comparison. 4.1.2 covers the principle.

Say that you would note what the patient does spontaneously, since the
instructed version of a movement is a different measurement from the
spontaneous one. 4.4.10 covers functional screening.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that walking and rising are the knee's real loading
conditions, so the behaviour under those conditions is the closest thing in the
examination to the patient's experience. 3.3.9 covers kinematics during
walking.

A knee that avoids terminal extension in stance is avoiding the position in
which the quadriceps works hardest, and that avoidance is the common response
to pain, to an effusion and to quadriceps weakness alike. 4.7.8 covers
extension lag.

Rising from a chair loads the extensor mechanism through a large arc, so an
abnormal rise points at the extensor mechanism or the patellofemoral joint
rather than at a ligament. 3.3.11 covers kinematics during squatting.

Turning loads internal and external rotation, which is why a protected turn is
the inspection equivalent of a rotational test. 3.3.14 covers kinematics during
cutting.

### How Good It Is

Observation from the door correlates with the patient's complaint better than
any single manual test and is almost never recorded, which is an odd
combination worth stating plainly.

It is sensitive and not specific. It shows that something is wrong and rarely
shows what, which makes it a screen rather than a diagnosis.

It is unreliable in a patient who is anxious, in pain from another joint, or
walking on an unfamiliar floor, and all three are common in a clinic.

It is the only part of the examination that cannot be repeated once the patient
is undressed and on the couch, which is why its place in the sequence is fixed.
4.3.2 covers gait observation in more detail.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Walking and rising are the knee's real loading conditions.
- A knee avoiding terminal extension is avoiding where the quadriceps works hardest.
- An abnormal rise from a chair points at the extensor mechanism rather than a ligament.
- A protected turn is the inspection equivalent of a rotational test.
- It is sensitive, not specific, and it cannot be repeated once the patient is on the couch.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this observation sets the question the rest of the
examination answers, which is a larger contribution than it is usually given
credit for.

A patient whose knee limits a specific task is a patient with a functional
problem, and functional problems are what operations are offered for. A grade
is not. 4.11.6 covers that argument in its sharpest form.

Where the observation and the couch examination disagree, the observation is
more often the one that matches the outcome, and that disagreement is worth
writing down rather than resolving in favour of the measurable finding.

The observation also identifies the patient whose complaint is not the knee. A
patient who rises badly and walks well is usually describing a hip, and a
patient who walks badly and rises well is usually describing the knee.
4.2.15 covers referred pain.

In a revision setting the observation is the most honest assessment available,
because every measurable finding has been altered by surgery. 4.10.12 covers
revision assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not doing it, which is a choice made by starting the
examination at the couch.

The second is recording a gait description that was never observed, which
appears in letters more often than it should.

The third is over reading a single observation. A patient carrying a bag or
wearing unfamiliar shoes walks abnormally for a reason that is not the knee.

The fourth is attributing every abnormality to the knee being examined, when the
hip, the ankle and the spine all produce the same patterns. 4.1.4 covers the
structured approach.

The fifth is letting the observation replace the examination rather than
frame it.

### Key Learning Points

- The observation sets the question the rest of the examination answers.
- A functional problem is what an operation is offered for; a grade is not.
- Where observation and the couch disagree, the observation more often matches the outcome.
- Rising badly and walking well usually means the hip.
- In revision practice it is the most honest assessment available.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the observation is frequently the only part of the
assessment that has not been done, and it is frequently where the referral's
answer is.

A patient who walks into the clinic unaided, having been referred with a
disabling knee, is describing a mismatch, and establishing whether it is
expectation, confidence or compensation is the work of the consultation.
4.22.6 covers the score built for that question.

A patient who is reluctant to be observed, or who performs differently once
asked, raises a question the examination cannot settle and that has to be
handled rather than recorded as inconsistency. 4.23.7 covers the medicolegal
setting.

In the multiply operated knee the observation separates stiffness from
instability more reliably than the couch does, because a stiff knee shows itself
in the transfer onto the couch and a lax one does not. 4.2.4 covers stiffness.

An athlete walking normally is uninformative, because the task is far below the
demand that fails, and the observation has to be moved to a task that
approaches it. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing.

The observation is also where a neurological cause declares itself first. A foot
that slaps or a toe that catches points at the nerve rather than the joint, and
in a knee with lateral ligament surgery that is a specific question. 4.13.4
covers the corner.

### Key Learning Points

- A patient walking in unaided with a disabling referral is describing a mismatch to be worked out.
- Performing differently once asked is a question to handle, not an inconsistency to record.
- In a multiply operated knee this separates stiffness from instability better than the couch.
- An athlete walking normally is uninformative; the task has to approach the demand that fails.
- A slapping foot or catching toe points at the nerve rather than the joint.
