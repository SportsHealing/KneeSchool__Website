# T2 Imaging

The sequence fluid is bright on, which is most of what makes magnetic resonance
imaging useful in a knee.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A T2 weighted image takes its contrast from how quickly the signal decays, and
free water decays slowest. 5.2.12 covers the basic principles.

So fluid is bright, which makes an effusion, a cyst, a tear filled with fluid
and oedema all conspicuous. 5.6.3 covers horizontal meniscal tears.

It matters because almost every acute knee finding involves fluid or oedema, so
this weighting carries most of the diagnostic load. 5.7.4 covers the acute
cruciate injury.

### How It Is Acquired

It is acquired with a long repetition time and a long echo time, which weights
the image towards the decay difference. 5.2.12 covers the basic principles.

Fluid is bright, muscle intermediate, and fat remains fairly bright on a
conventional sequence. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Fat being bright is the problem, because a bright lesion against bright fat is
hard to see, which is why fat suppression exists. 5.2.16 covers fat suppression.

Tendon, ligament and normal meniscus are dark, so fluid signal within them is
the abnormality. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

It is acquired in all three planes, because a linear abnormality has to be
confirmed on more than one. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

### Key Learning Points

- Contrast comes from how quickly the signal decays.
- Fluid is bright, which makes most acute findings conspicuous.
- Fat stays fairly bright on a conventional sequence.
- Normal tendon, ligament and meniscus are dark.
- A linear abnormality is confirmed in more than one plane.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer links bright fluid to the findings it reveals.

The expected points are the long repetition and echo times, bright fluid, bright
fat as the limitation, dark fibrous structures, and the three planes.

Say that fat suppression is added because fat is bright here. 5.2.16 covers fat
suppression.

Say that fluid reaching a meniscal surface is the finding this sequence exists
to show. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Say that a finding on one plane only is treated with caution. 5.5.1 covers the
standard protocol.

### How Good It Is

T2 weighting is the most informative single weighting for the knee and is never
used alone.

Its best contribution is fluid, which means effusion, cyst, tear and oedema
together. 5.6.3 covers horizontal meniscal tears.

It is limited by fat being bright, so a marrow or a fatty tissue lesion needs
the suppressed version. 5.2.16 covers fat suppression.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no sequence
timing values appear anywhere in this chapter, because those are scanner
specific protocol quantities.

### Key Learning Points

- Fat suppression is added because fat is bright on T2.
- Fluid reaching a meniscal surface is this sequence's defining finding.
- A finding on one plane only is treated with caution.
- Most informative single weighting, and never used alone.
- Marrow lesions need the fat suppressed version.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this is the sequence most knee decisions are made from, and
knowing what it cannot show matters more than knowing what it can.

A fluid filled tear, a displaced fragment and an effusion are all read here with
confidence. 5.6.6 covers bucket handle tears.

Degenerate tissue holds water without being torn, so intermediate signal is the
ambiguity this sequence creates as well as resolves. 5.6.2 covers meniscal
signal grading.

A chronic lesion with no fluid can be inconspicuous, which is the systematic
blind spot. 5.7.5 covers chronic cruciate injury.

Marrow assessment needs the fat suppressed version in the same study. 5.2.16
covers fat suppression.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading intermediate signal in a degenerate structure as
a tear. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

A marrow lesion is missed against bright fat. 5.2.16 covers fat suppression.

A chronic abnormality with no fluid is read as normal. 5.7.5 covers chronic
cruciate injury.

Fluid in a normal recess is reported as pathology. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

A single plane finding is reported without confirmation. 5.5.1 covers the
standard protocol.

### Key Learning Points

- Intermediate signal in degenerate tissue is read as a tear.
- A marrow lesion is missed against bright fat.
- A chronic lesion without fluid reads as normal.
- Fluid in a normal recess is reported as pathology.
- A single plane finding is reported unconfirmed.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the T2 sequence's ambiguity in the middle of its range is
the source of a large share of this section's problems.

Bright is a tear and dark is normal, and the intermediate signal that degenerate
tissue produces has no agreed meaning, which is exactly where most older knees
sit. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

That ambiguity propagates: a graft, a repair and a degenerate meniscus all
produce intermediate signal, and this section's pages on each reach the same
unresolved point. 5.7.10 covers graft maturation.

Quantitative relaxation measurement puts a number on it and has not resolved
the clinical question, because the question is mechanical. 5.18.2 covers T2
mapping.

The sequences have improved faster than the interpretive conventions, so modern
studies show intermediate signal that older grading systems were not written
for. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

The useful habit is to report intermediate signal as intermediate rather than
resolving it towards a diagnosis the image does not support. 5.20.1 covers
structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Intermediate signal has no agreed meaning and is where older knees sit.
- The same ambiguity recurs for grafts, repairs and degenerate menisci.
- Quantitative relaxation measurement does not resolve a mechanical question.
- Sequences have improved faster than the grading conventions.
- Report intermediate signal as intermediate.
