# PLC Injuries

A pattern rather than a structure, so the report has to name which components
are involved and the examination owns the instability.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

A posterolateral corner injury involves several structures together: the
collateral ligament, the popliteus, the popliteofibular ligament and the
capsule. 2.10.4 covers the posterolateral corner.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows each of them, the smaller ones inconsistently.
5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

It matters because the injury is defined by which components are involved rather
than by any single finding. 3.10.6 covers the posterolateral corner function.

### How It Is Acquired

Use a protocol with thin coronal and axial images through the fibular head.
5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

Assess each named structure separately and report each as intact, injured or not
assessable. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Assess the fibular head and styloid for avulsion. 2.3.2 covers the attachments.

Assess the cruciates, since a combined injury changes everything. 3.10.9 covers
the combined PCL and PLC injury.

Assess the common peroneal nerve. 5.10.5 covers the common peroneal nerve.

### What Normal Looks Like

Each lateral structure identifiable and continuous. 2.10.4 covers the
posterolateral corner.

An intact fibular head and styloid. 2.3.2 covers the attachments.

No marrow change at the lateral attachments. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

A normal common peroneal nerve. 2.10.6 covers the common peroneal nerve.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Injury of two or more lateral structures, which is the pattern. 2.10.4 covers the
posterolateral corner.

A fibular styloid avulsion, which implies the deeper structures. 5.10.4 covers
the popliteofibular ligament.

Oedema through the lateral soft tissues with the structures hard to identify
individually. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

A cruciate injury alongside, usually the posterior one. 5.8.3 covers the complete
PCL tears.

Nerve change, which alters the consent. 5.10.5 covers the common peroneal nerve.

### Key Learning Points

- The injury is defined by which components are involved.
- Each structure is reported separately.
- A styloid avulsion implies the deeper structures.
- A combined cruciate injury is the usual pattern.
- The nerve belongs in the assessment.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer lists the components.

The expected points are the thin images through the fibular head, each structure
named, the fibular avulsion, the cruciates, and the nerve.

Say that the grading is clinical and the imaging is structural. 4.13.4 covers the
posterolateral corner assessment.

Say that the dial test and the recurvatum test report the instability. 4.13.5
covers the dial test.

Say that a missed corner injury is a recognised reason a cruciate reconstruction
fails. 3.10.8 covers the PLC reconstruction biomechanics.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that these structures act together to resist varus and
external rotation, so the functional deficit depends on the combination rather
than on any one component. 3.10.6 covers the posterolateral corner function.

That is why the report lists components and why the examination grades the
instability. 4.13.4 covers the posterolateral corner assessment.

Acute oedema obscures the individual structures, so an early scan can establish
the pattern without resolving the parts. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

A fibular styloid avulsion is the single most useful indirect sign, because it
implies attachments that cannot be seen directly. 5.10.4 covers the
popliteofibular ligament.

### How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging is the best available structural assessment of this
corner and does not grade the instability.

Its best contribution is the combination of components and the fibular avulsion.
2.10.4 covers the posterolateral corner.

It is limited because the small structures are inconsistently seen, because
acute oedema obscures them, and because the deficit is rotational and clinical.
4.13.5 covers the dial test.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no rotation
values or grading thresholds appear either: those are clinical grades with
published definitions and this environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The deficit depends on the combination of components.
- The report lists components and the examination grades.
- Acute oedema establishes the pattern and hides the parts.
- A styloid avulsion is the most useful indirect sign.
- The deficit is rotational and clinical.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the imaging decides whether a cruciate reconstruction needs
the corner addressed at the same time. 3.10.8 covers the PLC reconstruction
biomechanics.

A corner injury left untreated is a recognised cause of graft failure. 5.7.11
covers the graft failure.

A combined posterior cruciate and corner injury is a different operation again.
3.10.9 covers the combined PCL and PLC injury.

Alignment belongs in the chronic case, because varus loads the reconstruction.
5.17.4 covers the osteotomy.

The nerve changes the consent and the rehabilitation. 5.10.5 covers the common
peroneal nerve.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the lateral collateral ligament alone. 2.10.1
covers the LCL.

Absence of a structure from the report is read as that structure being intact.
5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

A chronic injury with no oedema is read as normal. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

The fibular styloid avulsion is reported as an isolated fracture. 2.3.2 covers
the attachments.

Rotational instability is inferred from the images. 4.13.5 covers the dial test.

### Key Learning Points

- Reporting the collateral ligament alone is the error.
- Absence from the report is not evidence of integrity.
- A chronic injury with no oedema reads as normal.
- A styloid avulsion is reported as an isolated fracture.
- Rotational instability cannot be inferred.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the corner injury was missed at the index operation and the
graft has failed. 5.7.11 covers the graft failure.

The original scan usually shows the injury that was not reported, which makes
obtaining it more useful than repeating it. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Chronic injury has no oedema and may have no identifiable structures, so the
diagnosis becomes clinical and the imaging supports the plan. 4.13.4 covers the
posterolateral corner assessment.

Varus alignment in a chronic lateral injury is part of the problem and part of
the operation. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

A rotational profile adds to the picture in a knee whose instability does not
fit its structures. 5.16.3 covers the rotational profiles.

The useful report lists each component with its state, the fibular head, the
cruciates, the nerve and the alignment. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- The original scan usually shows what was not reported.
- A chronic injury may have no identifiable structures.
- Varus alignment is part of the problem and the operation.
- A rotational profile adds to a mismatched picture.
- The report lists components with their state.
