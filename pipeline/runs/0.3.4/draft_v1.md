# What MRCS and FRCS Actually Are

Two sets of letters appear after the names of most surgeons in the United
Kingdom. This page explains what each examination tests and when in a career
it sits.

## For Young Learners

### What the Work Involves

MRCS has two parts. The first is written and covers anatomy, physiology,
pathology and the principles of surgery. The second is practical, and the
candidate moves between stations examining actors, handling instruments,
interpreting images and explaining a diagnosis out loud.

FRCS also has two parts. The written part covers the whole of trauma and
orthopaedics. The oral part is a set of structured conversations with
consultant examiners, built around real cases, imaging and clinical scenarios.
Candidates describe it as the hardest thing they have done.

Neither examination is pass or fail on knowledge alone. Both test whether a
candidate can explain a decision and justify it under pressure, because that
is what the job actually consists of.

### The People Who Do It

MRCS stands for Member of the Royal College of Surgeons. FRCS stands for
Fellow of the Royal College of Surgeons. Both are examinations rather than
degrees, and both are taken while working as a doctor rather than while
studying full time.

They are not two attempts at the same thing. They test different stages of a
career and they look completely different when you sit them.

MRCS is taken by doctors in their first surgical posts, usually a few years
after qualifying. Candidates come from every surgical specialty and sit the
same examination, because at that stage they are being tested as surgeons
rather than as knee surgeons.

FRCS is taken near the end of specialty training, by which point a candidate
has chosen trauma and orthopaedic surgery and has a logbook of their own
cases. The examination is specific to that specialty.

### What It Takes

Anatomy is the subject that carries across both. The structures named in
Sections 1 and 2 of this site are the ones an MRCS candidate is expected to
know cold, and the ones an FRCS candidate is expected to be able to operate
around.

That is why learning the names of things at school is not wasted effort. It is
the same material, revisited at greater depth, which is what the spiral
curriculum on this site is built around.

### Where to Find Out More

The Royal College of Surgeons publishes the current syllabus, structure,
eligibility rules and fees for both examinations. Those details change between
sittings, so the college is the only reliable place to read them.

The British Orthopaedic Association publishes guidance on what specialty
training in trauma and orthopaedics involves alongside the examination.

### Key Learning Points

- MRCS and FRCS are examinations taken while working, not university degrees.
- MRCS sits early in surgical training and is the same for every surgical specialty.
- FRCS sits near the end of training and is specific to trauma and orthopaedics.
- Both examinations test whether a candidate can justify a decision out loud, not just recall facts.
- Anatomy is the subject that carries through both.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.
