# Proprioception

The meniscus reports back. Nerve endings in the horns tell the nervous system
what the joint is doing, which is a mechanical function in a different sense.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Controlling a joint needs information about where it is and what is happening
to it. That information comes from sensors in the tissues.

Proprioception is that sense. It is what lets a limb be positioned accurately
with the eyes closed.

Sensors in a structure that is being stretched or compressed can report the
load as well as the position.

### At the Knee

Mechanoreceptors are present in the meniscus, concentrated in the anterior and
posterior horns rather than in the body.

The horns are where the meniscus is deformed most at the extremes of range, so
sensors there report the end of movement rather than the middle of it.

Several receptor types are described, including those that respond to
sustained position and those that respond to rapid change.

The information contributes to reflex muscle control, which is part of how the
knee protects itself. 3.2.10 covers stability.

### What Changes It

Whether the horns are intact, since that is where the receptors are.

Injury, which damages receptors as well as tissue.

Resection, particularly of a horn.

Age, which reduces proprioceptive acuity throughout the body.

### Clinical Relevance

Meniscal injury reduces proprioceptive input as well as mechanical function,
and both affect how the knee is controlled.

That is one reason rehabilitation includes balance and control work rather
than strength alone. Section 6 covers management.

Preserving a horn preserves sensory tissue as well as load bearing tissue.
3.6.9 covers root biomechanics.

This is the fifth of the five functions, and it is the one most easily
forgotten. 3.6.1 covers them.

### Key Learning Points

- Proprioception is the sense of joint position and movement.
- Mechanoreceptors are concentrated in the meniscal horns rather than the body.
- The horns deform most at the extremes of range, so they report the end of movement.
- The information contributes to reflex muscle control.
- Meniscal injury reduces sensory input as well as mechanical function.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The receptors are the same families found in other joint tissues: Ruffini
endings for sustained position, Pacinian corpuscles for rapid change, Golgi
type endings for tension, and free nerve endings for pain.

Their distribution is the mechanically informative part. Concentration in the
horns means the signal is largest where deformation is largest, at the limits
of motion.

Proprioceptive input feeds reflex muscle activation, so a sensory loss becomes
a mechanical loss through delayed or reduced muscle response. 3.4.7 covers
internal moments.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching describes mechanoreceptors in the outer third and in the
horns of both menisci, with the posterior horn commonly reported as the
densest.

That matches the posterior horn being the part most deformed in flexion, where
the load share is highest. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Proprioceptive deficits are measurable after meniscal injury and after
resection, and separating them from the effects of pain and effusion is
difficult.

The cruciate ligaments carry mechanoreceptors too, so a combined injury
affects the sensory system from more than one site. 3.7 covers them.

### What Changes It

Horn integrity, since the receptors are concentrated there.

Pain and effusion, which alter measured proprioception without a structural
cause.

Resection, in proportion and by location.

Age and training, both of which change proprioceptive acuity.

### How It Is Measured

Histology and immunohistochemistry demonstrate receptor presence and
distribution, in cadaveric and surgical specimens.

Joint position sense and threshold to detection of passive motion testing
measures proprioception in patients, with poor reproducibility.

Reflex latency measurement assesses the functional consequence and is a
research technique.

Separating sensory loss from pain, effusion and muscle inhibition is the
standing difficulty in this literature.

### Clinical Relevance

Expect proprioceptive deficit after meniscal injury and resection, and expect
it to be hard to attribute.

Include balance and neuromuscular control in rehabilitation on these grounds.

Treat combined meniscal and cruciate injury as a larger sensory loss than
either alone.

Read joint position sense measurements with their poor reproducibility in
mind.

### Key Learning Points

- Ruffini, Pacinian, Golgi type and free nerve endings are all described in the meniscus.
- Concentration in the horns means the signal is largest at the limits of motion.
- A sensory loss becomes a mechanical loss through delayed muscle response.
- The posterior horn is commonly reported as the densest, by conventional teaching.
- Separating sensory loss from pain, effusion and inhibition is the standing difficulty.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the sensory function as an argument for preserving horns specifically,
which coincides with the load bearing argument for the same tissue.

Where the two arguments agree, the conclusion is secure. The sensory case does
not need to carry weight on its own, and it should not be asked to.

A transplant restores tissue and not innervation initially. Conventional
teaching holds that reinnervation of allograft is partial, which is one reason
proprioceptive recovery after transplantation is incomplete. 3.6.14 covers
transplantation.

### What Changes It

Horn preservation or restoration, which is where the receptors are.

Reinnervation of a graft, which is partial and slow.

Neuromuscular rehabilitation, which is the modifiable element.

Concurrent cruciate injury, which compounds the sensory loss.

### How It Is Measured

Histological study of retrieved allograft demonstrates the extent of
reinnervation.

Clinical proprioceptive testing measures the consequence with poor
reproducibility.

Functional performance testing measures the outcome that proprioception
contributes to, without isolating it.

No measurement attributes a functional deficit specifically to lost meniscal
proprioception.

### Clinical Relevance

Preserve horns, where the load bearing and the sensory arguments agree.

Expect proprioceptive recovery after transplantation to be incomplete.

Prescribe neuromuscular rehabilitation as the modifiable element.

Avoid resting a treatment argument on proprioception alone.

### Key Learning Points

- The sensory argument coincides with the load bearing one for preserving horns.
- A transplant restores tissue, not innervation; reinnervation is partial.
- Proprioceptive recovery after transplantation is incomplete.
- No measurement attributes a deficit specifically to lost meniscal proprioception.
- Do not rest a treatment argument on proprioception alone.
