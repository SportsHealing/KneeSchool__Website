# Valgus Alignment

The axis passes outside the knee centre and the lateral compartment carries
more. The mirror of varus geometrically, and not its mirror clinically.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

The same lever argument applies on the other side. A load passing outside a
joint loads the outer part of it.

But two sides of a joint are rarely built the same, so the same geometry does
not produce the same result.

A smaller surface carrying a larger share wears faster than a larger surface
carrying the same share.

### At the Knee

Valgus means the knee points inwards and the leg looks knock kneed. The
mechanical axis passes outside the knee centre. 3.13.1 covers the axis.

The lateral compartment then carries a larger share than usual. 3.5.3 covers
load distribution.

The lateral side of the knee has a smaller, more convex surface and a more
mobile meniscus than the medial side. 2.2.2 covers the plateau.

The medial soft tissue is stretched in a valgus knee, which is the opposite
pattern to varus. 3.9.1 covers valgus stability.

### What Changes It

Bone shape, which is the usual reason.

Lateral cartilage loss, which lets that side collapse further.

Previous lateral meniscus surgery, which removes a load spreader from the
smaller compartment. 3.5.5 covers meniscal loss.

A fracture healed in valgus, particularly of the lateral tibial plateau.

### Clinical Relevance

Lateral compartment wear is less common than medial, but valgus knees are
harder to manage when it happens. 6.74 covers the condition.

The medial soft tissue stretches over time in a valgus knee, which adds
instability to the wear.

Correction is less predictable than on the medial side, partly for that
reason. 3.13.9 covers osteotomy.

4.5.3 covers how it is assessed clinically.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus puts the mechanical axis outside the knee centre.
- The lateral compartment then carries a larger share than usual.
- The lateral surface is smaller and more convex than the medial.
- Medial soft tissue is stretched in a valgus knee.
- Lateral wear is less common than medial and harder to manage.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Geometrically valgus is varus reflected. Clinically it is not, because the
compartments differ in area, in congruence, in meniscal mobility and in soft
tissue.

Load share is only half the story. The same share on a smaller contact area is
a higher pressure. 3.5.1 covers contact areas.

So a symmetrical geometric statement produces an asymmetrical clinical one.

### At the Knee

Valgus reduces the external adduction moment and raises the abduction moment,
which loads the lateral compartment. 3.4.6 covers external moments.

The lateral plateau is smaller and convex while the medial is larger and
concave, so lateral contact pressure rises faster for a given share. 3.5.1
covers it.

The lateral meniscus is more mobile and contributes more of its compartment's
load spreading, which makes its loss more consequential there. 3.5.5 covers
that.

Chronic valgus stretches the medial collateral complex, producing a combined
deformity and laxity problem. 3.9.1 covers the restraints.

Valgus deformity is more often femoral in origin than tibial, which changes
where a correction belongs. 3.13.6 covers localisation.

### What Changes It

Distal femoral morphology, which is the commonest site of the deformity.

Lateral cartilage and bone loss, which is the intra-articular component.

Previous lateral meniscectomy, which raises lateral pressure independently.
3.5.9 covers it.

Medial soft tissue competence, which decides how much of the measured
deformity is correctable.

Inflammatory arthropathy, which produces valgus more often than mechanical
wear does. 6.79 covers it.

### How It Is Measured

Hip knee ankle angle on a standing long leg film, as for varus. 5.3.2 covers
the technique.

Joint orientation angles, which identify the femoral predominance. 3.13.6
covers them.

Stress radiography, which separates the medial laxity component from the bony
deformity.

Gait analysis for the abduction moment, which is less studied than its
adduction counterpart. 3.16.7 covers motion capture.

The lateral compartment has less published pressure data than the medial, so
figures there are weaker.

### Clinical Relevance

Localise a valgus deformity before correcting it, since it is more often
femoral. 3.13.6 covers it.

Assess medial soft tissue competence, because chronic valgus produces laxity
as well as deformity.

Expect lateral compartment pressure to rise faster than the load share does,
because the surface is smaller. 3.5.1 covers it.

Consider inflammatory causes in a valgus knee, which is a different pathway
from mechanical wear. 6.79 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Valgus raises the abduction moment and loads the lateral compartment.
- The lateral plateau is smaller and convex, so pressure rises faster for a given share.
- The lateral meniscus does more of its compartment's load spreading.
- Chronic valgus stretches the medial collateral complex.
- Valgus deformity is more often femoral than tibial in origin.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The asymmetry between varus and valgus is the useful teaching point, and it
has four sources: compartment area, congruence, meniscal contribution and soft
tissue behaviour.

Because the lateral compartment is smaller and convex, a correction that
equalises load share does not equalise pressure, which is one reason lateral
results are less predictable. 3.5.1 covers the areas.

The medial soft tissue laxity that accompanies chronic valgus means a bony
correction can leave a knee that is aligned and unstable. 3.9.1 covers the
restraints.

Distal femoral osteotomy is the usual correction site, which is a different
operation with a different complication profile from a proximal tibial one.
7.70 covers it.

Conventional teaching treats valgus correction as having a narrower indication
than varus correction, and the evidence base is correspondingly thinner. 6.154
covers the controversies.

### What Changes It

The site of the deformity, femoral or tibial, which decides the operation.
3.13.6 covers it.

Medial laxity, which is both a consequence of the deformity and a limit on
correcting it.

Lateral meniscal status, which changes the compartment's tolerance
independently of alignment. 3.5.9 covers it.

The underlying disease process, since inflammatory and mechanical valgus
behave differently. 6.79 covers it.

Patellofemoral consequences, because changing coronal alignment moves the
extensor vector too. 3.12.11 covers that.

### How It Is Measured

Standing long leg radiography and joint orientation angles, which together
localise the deformity.

Stress views, which quantify the medial laxity that will not be corrected by
bone.

Magnetic resonance imaging for lateral meniscal and cartilage status, which
modifies the prognosis. 5.11 covers cartilage imaging.

Gait analysis, which has far less valgus data than varus data in the
literature.

The thinner evidence base is itself a finding: most of what is quoted about
alignment and load comes from varus knees.

### Clinical Relevance

Localise the deformity and correct at its site, which in valgus is usually the
femur. 7.70 covers the operation.

Quantify medial laxity before correction, because an aligned unstable knee is
a poor result.

Expect lateral pressure to fall less than load share suggests, and counsel
accordingly.

State that most alignment and load data comes from varus knees when applying
it here.

Weigh the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, since the indication
here is narrower and the evidence thinner than for varus.

### Key Learning Points

- Varus and valgus differ in compartment area, congruence, meniscal contribution and soft tissue.
- Equalising load share does not equalise pressure, because the lateral surface is smaller.
- Chronic valgus can leave a knee that is aligned and unstable.
- Distal femoral osteotomy is the usual correction site.
- Most alignment and load data comes from varus knees.
