# Patellar Dislocation Event

The one injury patients usually describe accurately, and the one whose first
episode decides what happens for the next twenty years.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A patellar dislocation is the kneecap moving laterally out of the trochlear
groove, usually during a twist on a flexed, loaded knee.

Asking about it tests whether the patellofemoral joint rather than the
tibiofemoral joint is the problem, and patients usually describe it well
because they can see and feel it.

The event matters more than most, because the first dislocation establishes
the risk of recurrence and the opportunity to assess why it happened.

### How It Is Performed

Ask whether the kneecap came out of place, and whether they or anybody else
saw it to the side.

Ask how it went back: spontaneously on straightening the leg, by the patient
pushing it, or by somebody else.

Ask whether it has happened before, and whether the other knee has ever done
it.

Ask about immediate swelling, which is usual after a first dislocation.

Ask about family history and about general flexibility, since both bear on why
it happened. 4.23.3 covers hypermobile patients.

### What a Positive Means

A clear lateral dislocation that reduced on straightening the knee is a
patellar dislocation, and the differential is short.

A first dislocation with a haemarthrosis raises an osteochondral fragment from
the medial patellar facet or the lateral condyle, which is a reason to image
acutely. 4.17.3 covers osteochondral lesions.

Recurrent dislocation points towards an underlying structural cause: trochlear
dysplasia, patellar height, tubercle position or generalised laxity. 4.15.8
covers dysplasia.

A dislocation with no trauma at all, or in both knees, points more strongly to
structural predisposition than to injury.

Many patients report the kneecap going out when the knee has actually given
way at the tibiofemoral joint, so the account needs testing. 4.2.6 covers
giving way.

### Key Learning Points

- A patellar dislocation is the kneecap moving laterally, usually on a twisting flexed knee.
- Patients describe it accurately because they can see and feel it.
- A first dislocation with a haemarthrosis raises an osteochondral fragment.
- Recurrent or atraumatic dislocation points to a structural predisposition.
- Some patients report the kneecap going out when the knee gave way instead.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that a dislocation requires both a displacing force
and a knee that permits displacement, and the history addresses both.

A high energy event in a normal knee is an injury. A trivial event in a knee
with dysplasia is a structural problem that happened to be revealed.

The medial patellofemoral ligament is the primary restraint in early flexion
and is torn in almost all first dislocations. 4.15.7 covers its assessment.

Trochlear dysplasia is the strongest structural predictor of recurrence, which
is why the imaging after a first dislocation is about the shape rather than
the soft tissue. 4.15.8 covers it.

A patient who has dislocated both patellae, or who has a family history, has a
predisposition and the counselling differs.

### How Good It Is

The patellar dislocation history is among the most reliable in the knee,
because the event is visible and memorable and the reduction is distinctive.

It performs well for the diagnosis and poorly for the cause, which is why the
imaging and examination that follow are about predisposition rather than
confirmation.

The number of previous episodes is the most useful single feature for
predicting further ones, and it is reliably recalled.

Where it performs badly is in distinguishing a subluxation from a dislocation,
since the patient's account cannot separate them and the distinction matters
for management.

### Key Learning Points

- A dislocation needs a displacing force and a knee that permits displacement.
- A trivial event with dysplasia is a structural problem revealed, not an injury.
- The medial patellofemoral ligament is torn in almost all first dislocations.
- Trochlear dysplasia is the strongest structural predictor of recurrence.
- The number of previous episodes is the most useful predictor of further ones.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the history after a first dislocation determines both the
imaging and the counselling, and it is the assessment that most changes a
young patient's next twenty years.

A first dislocation with a high energy mechanism, no structural predisposition
and no fragment is usually managed non-operatively with a good prognosis.

A first dislocation with a trivial mechanism, dysplasia and a family history
has a high recurrence risk, and saying so at the first consultation is better
than discovering it at the fourth episode.

An osteochondral fragment changes the plan to an acute operation, and it is
the one finding that makes a first dislocation surgical. 4.17.3 covers the
lesion.

Recurrent dislocation needs the predisposing anatomy characterised before any
stabilising procedure, because the operation has to address the reason. 4.15.8
and 4.15.4 cover the assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating a first dislocation as an isolated injury
without assessing predisposition, which guarantees surprise at the second
episode.

The second is accepting a report of the patella dislocating when the knee gave
way, which sends the assessment to the wrong joint.

The third is attributing recurrent anterior pain after a dislocation to
instability when it is chondral damage from the event, which is common and has
a different prognosis. 4.15.10 covers patellofemoral change.

And in a skeletally immature patient a dislocation event raises a physeal or
sleeve injury and the imaging differs. 4.19.4 covers paediatric patellar
instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The history after a first dislocation determines both the imaging and the counselling.
- A trivial mechanism with dysplasia and a family history carries a high recurrence risk.
- An osteochondral fragment is the one finding that makes a first dislocation surgical.
- Recurrent dislocation needs the predisposing anatomy characterised before stabilisation.
- Anterior pain after a dislocation may be chondral damage rather than instability.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the failed stabilisation is the presentation, and the
history establishes which of several failures occurred.

Continued dislocation after a soft tissue stabilisation usually means the bony
predisposition was not addressed, and the history of the original episodes
will often reveal that they were atraumatic.

Pain without instability after stabilisation raises over-constraint, a
chondral injury from the original events, or a medial patellofemoral
reconstruction that is too tight, and these are separated by when the pain
began relative to the operation. 4.20.6 covers the assessment.

A patient who dislocates medially after a lateral release or a tibial tubercle
transfer has iatrogenic instability, which is rare, recognisable and almost
entirely historical in its diagnosis.

In a hypermobile patient a stabilisation has a different prognosis and the
history of other joints is what identifies them. 4.23.3 covers the assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Continued dislocation after a soft tissue procedure usually means the bony cause was not addressed.
- Pain without instability after stabilisation raises over-constraint or chondral injury.
- When the pain began relative to the operation separates those causes.
- Medial instability after a lateral release is iatrogenic and diagnosed almost entirely from the history.
