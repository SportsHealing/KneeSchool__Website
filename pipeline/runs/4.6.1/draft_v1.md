# Systematic Palpation

A route around the knee that does not depend on what you expect to find, and
the reason the order matters more than the hands.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

Systematic palpation tests the structures around the knee one at a time, in a
fixed order, so that what is found does not depend on what was suspected.

The knee is superficial. Most of its important structures can be felt, which
makes palpation more informative here than at the hip or the shoulder.
2.1.1 covers the gross anatomy.

The value is in the system. An examiner who palpates where the patient points
finds what the patient already knows; an examiner who follows a route finds what
nobody has mentioned. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

### How It Is Performed

Start with temperature, with the back of the hand, comparing both knees and the
thigh and calf of each. 4.6.2 covers it.

Assess for an effusion before anything that hurts, because a tender knee stops
cooperating. 4.6.3 covers that assessment.

With the knee flexed to ninety degrees, palpate the joint line from the patellar
tendon round medially to the posteromedial corner, then from the tendon round
laterally. 4.6.4 covers joint line tenderness.

Palpate the patella: its borders, its facets with the knee relaxed, and the
tendon above and below it. 4.6.5 covers patellar tenderness.

Palpate the collateral ligaments along their courses, the tibial tubercle, the
fibular head and the pes insertion. 4.6.7 covers the tubercle.

Finish with the back of the knee, with the knee extended and the patient
standing if a cyst is suspected. 4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa.

### What a Positive Means

Tenderness localises a problem to a structure more precisely than any other part
of the examination, which is its whole contribution.

Joint line tenderness means the meniscus, the deep ligament or the joint surface,
and the three are separated by what provokes the pain rather than by its
location. 4.12.7 covers that separation.

Tenderness over a ligament's course with no increased laxity is a sprain and is
treated as pain. 4.12.3 covers the grade it is distinguished from.

Tenderness over the fibular head raises an avulsion or a corner injury and
prompts a nerve examination. 4.6.8 covers it.

Warmth with tenderness and an effusion raises infection or inflammation and
changes the pathway. 4.3.6 covers skin changes.

### Key Learning Points

- A fixed order means the findings do not depend on what was suspected.
- The knee is superficial, which makes palpation more informative than at other joints.
- Palpate temperature first and the tender areas last.
- Tenderness localises better than any other part of the examination.
- Tenderness over a ligament with no increased laxity is a sprain.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva palpation is where a candidate either has a route or does not, and
having one is visible within seconds.

The expected points are temperature first, the effusion, the joint line with the
knee flexed, the patella, the ligaments, the bony points, and the back of the
knee last.

Say why the knee is flexed for the joint line, because flexion opens the joint
line and makes the anterior horns accessible. 4.6.4 covers it.

Say that you would palpate the asymptomatic side first, which establishes the
normal and gains the patient's confidence. 4.1.2 covers the principle.

Say that you would record where the tenderness is rather than which structure
you think it is, since the location survives and the attribution may not.
4.1.8 covers the record.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the knee's structures lie in predictable
relationships to palpable landmarks, so a tender point can be assigned to a
structure by its position. 2.1.7 covers surgical anatomy.

The joint line is the landmark that matters most, because the meniscus, the deep
medial ligament and the articular surfaces all meet there and nothing else does.
2.5.1 covers the medial meniscus.

Flexing the knee rotates the tibia relative to the femur and opens the anterior
joint line, which is why the position changes what can be reached. 3.3.2 covers
roll and glide.

Tenderness is a symptom rather than a sign in the strict sense, which is why it
localises well and grades badly, and why the provocation tests exist.
4.9.3 covers one of them.

### How Good It Is

Palpation is the most specific localising part of the knee examination and the
least able to grade anything.

Joint line tenderness is the most useful single palpation finding and is also
non specific between the structures that meet there. 4.6.4 covers it.

It depends on patient cooperation and on the examiner's care rather than on
force, which makes it more reproducible than the ligament tests in an acute
knee.

It is unreliable in an obese patient, where the landmarks are hard to find, and
in a patient who is tender everywhere. 4.23.4 covers those patients.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- The knee's structures lie in predictable relationships to palpable landmarks.
- The joint line matters most, because the meniscus, the deep ligament and the surfaces all meet there.
- Flexion opens the anterior joint line, which is why the position changes what can be reached.
- Tenderness localises well and grades badly, which is why the provocation tests exist.
- It depends on care rather than force, so it survives the acute knee better than the ligament tests.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level palpation narrows the differential and rarely decides an
operation, which is an honest description of a useful contribution.

Where it does decide something is in the acute knee, where a tender fibular head
or a tender joint line changes the imaging requested and occasionally the
urgency. 4.6.8 covers the fibular head.

It also decides whether a complaint has a localised source at all. A patient
with diffuse tenderness and no localising point has a different problem from one
with a single tender spot, and the distinction affects what is offered.
4.2.1 covers pain.

In the chronic knee palpation of the joint line and the compartments contributes
to the question of whether the surface is the problem, which changes the
category of treatment. 4.9.11 covers meniscal examination in osteoarthritis.

In the post-operative knee palpation over the hardware, the portals and the
graft harvest site localises pain that the patient cannot. 4.3.7 covers
surgical scars.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is palpating where the patient points, which finds nothing
new.

The second is palpating a tender knee before assessing the effusion and the
temperature, after which the patient will not cooperate.

The third is attributing joint line tenderness to the meniscus by default.
4.12.7 covers the alternatives.

The fourth is palpating with the knee extended, where the joint line is closed
and the anterior horns cannot be reached.

The fifth is omitting the back of the knee, which is where the findings that are
missed live. 4.6.9 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Palpation narrows the differential and rarely decides an operation.
- In the acute knee a tender fibular head or joint line can change the imaging and the urgency.
- Diffuse tenderness with no localising point is a different problem from a single tender spot.
- In the post-operative knee it localises pain the patient cannot.
- Palpating with the knee extended closes the joint line and hides the anterior horns.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice palpation is often the part of the examination that was
performed least carefully, and repeating it properly answers a surprising
number of referrals.

A patient referred with a ligament complaint whose only finding is tenderness
over the pes insertion or the medial collateral's course has a soft tissue
problem treated as a ligament one. 4.6.6 covers tendon tenderness.

A patient with a reconstruction and localised tenderness over a tunnel or a
fixation device has a hardware problem, and palpation is the only examination
that finds it. 4.3.7 covers scars.

A patient with posteromedial joint line tenderness sitting more posteriorly than
usual may have a root or meniscotibial problem, and the exact location is the
clue. 4.9.9 covers meniscal root tears.

A patient tender over several unrelated structures, with a normal examination
otherwise, has a pain presentation rather than a structural one, and recognising
that prevents a futile operation. 4.2.1 covers pain.

In medicolegal work a recorded tender point is weak evidence, since tenderness
is a reported symptom rather than an observed sign, and that limit should be
stated. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- Repeating palpation properly answers a surprising number of referrals.
- Tenderness over the pes insertion or the collateral's course is a soft tissue problem.
- Localised tenderness over a tunnel or a device is a hardware problem only palpation finds.
- Tenderness sitting more posteriorly on the medial joint line suggests a root or meniscotibial problem.
- Tenderness over several unrelated structures is a pain presentation, not a structural one.
