# Osteotomy

A correction that has to be re-measured by the method that planned it, standing,
because the achieved correction is not always the planned one.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Post-operative imaging of an osteotomy shows the correction achieved, the union
of the cut and the fixation. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

The correction is measured on a standing long leg radiograph, as the plan was.
5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

It matters because the achieved correction decides whether the operation did
what it was for. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

### How It Is Acquired

Obtain a standing long leg film including the hip and the ankle. 5.3.6 covers the
long leg alignment films.

Measure the alignment by the same method used in the plan. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

Assess union at the osteotomy, with any gap or graft. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Assess the fixation for migration or breakage. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

Assess the joint line and the patellar height, both of which a tibial correction
changes. 5.3.9 covers the joint line obliquity.

### What Normal Looks Like

Alignment at the planned target, measured by the planning method. 5.3.7 covers
the mechanical axis.

Progressive union across the osteotomy. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Fixation in position and intact. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

A joint line and patellar height consistent with the correction performed.
5.12.2 covers patella alta.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Alignment short of or beyond the planned target. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical
axis.

Delayed union or a persisting gap at the cut. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Loss of correction across serial films. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment
films.

Broken or migrated fixation. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

An intra-articular fracture through the hinge. 5.16.1 covers the fracture
assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- The correction is measured standing and by the planning method.
- Union, fixation and correction are three separate findings.
- A tibial correction changes the joint line and patellar height.
- Loss of correction appears across serial films.
- A hinge fracture is a recognised complication.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer re-measures rather than describes.

The expected points are the standing long leg film, the planning method, the
union, the fixation, and the joint line.

Say that a supine film cannot assess the correction. 5.3.6 covers the long leg
alignment films.

Say that the achieved correction is not assumed from the operation note. 5.20.8
covers common reporting errors.

Say that the examination carries the function. 4.20.4 covers the osteotomy
examination.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the operation's purpose was to move load, so the
only finding that reports success is the loaded alignment, which requires the
patient to stand. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Union and correction are independent: a united osteotomy can sit in the wrong
place, and a correct one can fail to unite. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

A correction alters every other measurement made on that limb, so the
pre-operative set is the only comparable one. 5.16.3 covers the rotational
profiles.

The receiving compartment now carries the load and becomes the compartment to
watch. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

### How Good It Is

Radiography is the best available assessment of an osteotomy and depends
entirely on being acquired the same way each time.

Its best contribution is the loaded alignment, the union and the fixation. 5.3.6
covers the long leg alignment films.

It is limited by positioning, by rotation changing the apparent correction, and
by saying nothing about whether the soft tissues tolerate it. 5.1.6 covers
imaging pitfalls.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
correction values, axis angles or union intervals appear either: those are
published quantities tied to particular techniques and this environment cannot
check them.

### Key Learning Points

- Only the loaded alignment reports success.
- Union and correction are independent findings.
- A correction invalidates every earlier measurement.
- The receiving compartment becomes the one to watch.
- Positioning and rotation change the apparent correction.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the imaging decides whether the correction needs revising and
whether the fixation can come out. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Under-correction with persisting symptoms is a different problem from a
non-union. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

The receiving compartment's state decides whether further correction helps.
5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Patellar height and the joint line change the patellofemoral joint and belong in
the assessment. 5.12.7 covers the patellofemoral arthritis.

A later arthroplasty is planned from these films, so the record matters beyond
this operation. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is measuring the correction on a film that does not include
both joints. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

Limb rotation on the film changes the apparent correction. 5.1.6 covers imaging
pitfalls.

Hardware prominence is reported as failure. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

A persisting lucency at the cut is read as non-union when a graft is
incorporating. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

The patellofemoral consequence is omitted. 5.3.3 covers the skyline view.

### Key Learning Points

- The film has to include both joints.
- Rotation changes the apparent correction.
- Prominent hardware is not failed hardware.
- An incorporating graft looks lucent.
- The patellofemoral consequence is omitted.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the limb has been corrected and the symptoms have not
resolved. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Whether the correction was wrong, lost or insufficient needs the plan, the
immediate film and the current film together, and the first two are often
missing. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

A corrected limb is a changed limb, so every later measurement, the arthroplasty
references included, describes the operation. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty
planning.

Serial films from different departments in different positions compare
radiography as much as alignment. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

Converting a corrected knee to a replacement is harder, and the imaging should
say what the surgeon will meet. 5.17.8 covers the total knee replacement.

The useful report gives the measured alignment with its method, the union, the
fixation, the joint line, the patellar height and the receiving compartment.
5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Three studies are needed and two are often missing.
- A corrected limb changes every later reference.
- Serial films across departments compare radiography.
- A corrected knee is harder to replace.
- The report names the method of measurement.
