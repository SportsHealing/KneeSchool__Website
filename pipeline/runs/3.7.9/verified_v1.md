# Pivot Shift Mechanics

A subluxation and a reduction, in one movement. It is the mechanical event
patients experience as giving way, and the thing a test tries to reproduce.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A joint that has lost a restraint can sit out of place in one position and
snap back into place in another.

The snap back happens when a structure that was slack becomes tight enough to
pull the joint home.

The event has two halves: a subluxation and a reduction. Both are necessary
for it to be felt.

### At the Knee

Near extension, a cruciate deficient knee allows the lateral part of the tibia
to sit forward. That is the subluxation. 3.2.7 covers anterior translation.

As the knee flexes, the iliotibial band passes behind the flexion axis and its
tension pulls the tibia back into place. That is the reduction. 2.11.1 covers
the band.

The reduction is sudden, which is why it is felt as a shift rather than as a
gradual movement.

The whole event is a coupled motion, involving rotation as well as
translation. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

### What Changes It

Whether the iliotibial band is intact, since it produces the reduction.

The secondary restraints, which determine how far the knee subluxates. 3.7.3
covers them.

Muscle activity, which prevents the subluxation and is why the event is hard
to reproduce in an awake patient.

Bony shape, since a convex lateral plateau and a steep slope both allow more
subluxation.

### Clinical Relevance

Patients describe this event rather than a forward slide, which is why their
account is about turning rather than about laxity.

The test that reproduces it is the most informative one for function, and the
hardest to perform in a guarding patient. Section 4 covers the tests.

Each episode loads the secondary restraints and the cartilage, which is part
of how a deficient knee degenerates. 3.5.8 covers contact mechanics.

A reconstruction that abolishes this event has restored what the patient
complained of.

### Key Learning Points

- The event is a subluxation followed by a sudden reduction.
- Near extension the lateral tibia sits forward; flexion pulls it back.
- The iliotibial band's tension produces the reduction.
- It is a coupled motion involving rotation as well as translation.
- Patients describe this event rather than a forward slide.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

The mechanics are a changing moment arm. The iliotibial band lies anterior to
the flexion axis in extension and posterior to it in flexion, so its line of
action reverses as the knee bends. 3.3.7 covers the instant centre.

Before the reversal it permits or contributes to the subluxation; after it, it
reduces the joint. The reduction angle is where the reversal occurs.

Conventional teaching places the reduction at roughly 20 to 30 degrees of
flexion, which is where the line of action crosses.

### At the Knee

The subluxation is chiefly of the lateral compartment, because the lateral
side translates further. 3.3.2 covers the asymmetry.

Grading is by the examiner's perception: a glide, a clunk, or a locked knee
that will not reduce, which conventional teaching numbers one to three.

A high grade shift suggests involvement of the anterolateral structures or the
lateral meniscus in addition to the cruciate. 3.7.3 covers secondary
restraints.

The test's sensitivity is low in an awake patient and high under anaesthetic,
which is a statement about muscle rather than about the knee.

### What Changes It

Anterolateral complex and lateral meniscal integrity, which set the grade.

Slope and lateral plateau convexity, which permit more subluxation.

Muscle guarding, which suppresses the event.

Examiner technique and experience, which is a recognised source of variation.

### How It Is Measured

Graded clinical examination is the standard and has modest inter observer
reliability.

Instrumented measurement uses skin mounted accelerometers, image analysis or
electromagnetic tracking to quantify the reduction, with no agreed threshold.

Robotic simulation applies a combined load and measures the coupled motion in
cadavers.

Examination under anaesthetic is where the grade is most reliably obtained.

### Clinical Relevance

Grade under anaesthetic where the grade will change the operation.

Read a high grade as suggesting injury beyond the cruciate alone.

Treat instrumented measures as research tools without a clinical threshold.

Account for examiner variation when comparing grades between assessments.

### Key Learning Points

- The iliotibial band's line of action reverses as the knee flexes.
- Conventional teaching places the reduction at roughly 20 to 30 degrees.
- The subluxation is chiefly of the lateral compartment.
- A high grade shift suggests injury beyond the cruciate.
- The test's sensitivity depends on muscle, not on the knee.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the pivot shift as the clinical sign that corresponds to what the
patient reports, and treat its measurement as the weakest link in the whole
chapter.

That weakness has consequences for the evidence. Construct comparisons that
rest on rotational control rest on a sign with modest reliability and no
threshold, which is why reinjury rates carry the argument instead. 3.7.7
covers rotation.

Conventional teaching holds that a residual pivot shift after reconstruction
is associated with poorer outcome and with graft failure, which is the reason
it remains the sign to abolish.

### What Changes It

Femoral tunnel position, which determines the rotational control restored.
3.7.6 covers it.

Addition of a lateral extra-articular procedure. 3.11 covers the constructs.

Slope and plateau morphology, and whether they were addressed.

Lateral meniscal posterior horn repair.

### How It Is Measured

Examination under anaesthetic before and after reconstruction is the practical
intraoperative measurement.

Instrumented quantification is used in research cohorts and has no clinical
threshold.

Robotic testing compares constructs under a simulated pivot shift load.

Reinjury rates are the endpoint the sign is a proxy for.

### Clinical Relevance

Check the pivot shift under anaesthetic after fixation, before leaving
theatre.

Add a lateral procedure where a residual shift is anticipated or demonstrated.

Address slope and lateral meniscal pathology as part of abolishing the sign.

State the sign's reliability limits when it is used to compare treatments.

### Key Learning Points

- The pivot shift corresponds to what the patient reports and is measured worst.
- Construct comparisons rest on reinjury rates because the sign has no threshold.
- A residual pivot shift is associated with poorer outcome and graft failure.
- Check it under anaesthetic after fixation.
- Slope and lateral meniscal pathology are part of abolishing it.
