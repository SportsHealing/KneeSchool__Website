# Bone Bruising

The imprint the injury left in the bone, which makes it evidence about the
mechanism rather than a lesion to treat.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

When the knee twists badly, two bones can strike each other inside the joint.
That leaves a bruise inside the bone itself. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of
injury.

Nothing is broken. The bone is bruised in the way a bruise forms anywhere else,
except that it is inside and only a scan can show it. 6.2.4 covers combined
injuries.

### Investigations and Imaging

The pattern of bruising tells the clinician how the injury happened, which is
useful even though the bruise itself heals on its own. 6.2.1 covers non-contact
injury.

### Key Learning Points

- Two bones can strike each other during a bad twist.
- That leaves a bruise inside the bone.
- Nothing is broken, and only a scan shows it.
- The pattern says how the injury happened.
- The bruise itself usually heals on its own.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

Bone bruising, sometimes called a bone marrow lesion, is injury within the bone
beneath the joint surface, without a break. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

It happens when the two bones make contact during the moment the ligament
fails. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

### Investigations and Imaging

It appears on magnetic resonance imaging and not on an x-ray, which is one
reason a scan report lists things an x-ray did not. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL
injury.

Its position records the direction the bones moved, so it corroborates the
account of how the injury happened. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

### Management Overview

The bruising itself usually settles over months and is not treated directly.
6.1.4 covers the natural history.

It can be painful while it settles, and it is one reason a knee aches during
the early weeks even once the swelling has gone. 6.2.5 covers acute
haemarthrosis.

What matters more is the cartilage surface above a severe bruise, which may have
been injured at the same moment and does not recover in the same way. 6.4.6
covers cartilage injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- Bone bruising is injury within the bone without a break.
- It happens as the two bones make contact.
- It shows on a scan and not on an x-ray.
- It usually settles over months and is not treated directly.
- The cartilage above a severe bruise may not recover in the same way.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Is bone bruising serious?
Usually not in itself. It resolves. It matters because of what it indicates:
the force that caused it, and the possibility that the cartilage surface above
it was damaged at the same time.

**Q.** Does it need treatment?
No specific treatment. It settles with time. Pain from it is managed as part of
the overall recovery rather than separately.

**Q.** How long does it take to go?
Months rather than weeks, and it usually outlasts the swelling. It is a common
reason a knee still aches when everything else has improved.

**Q.** Why is it on my report if nothing is done about it?
Because it is informative. It confirms the mechanism, it points to what else may
have been injured, and in a severe case it flags cartilage that needs watching.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

Bone bruising represents trabecular microfracture, haemorrhage and oedema within
the subchondral bone, without a cortical break. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow
oedema.

It is present in the great majority of acute cruciate ruptures, which makes its
absence in a convincing history worth a second look. 6.3.5 covers complete
tears.

### Investigations and Imaging

It is seen on fluid sensitive sequences and is invisible on radiographs. 5.7.4
covers acute ACL injury.

The classical pivot pattern involves the lateral femoral condyle and the
posterolateral tibial plateau, recording anterior tibial translation with
rotation. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

A different distribution suggests a different mechanism and prompts a different
search. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

A deep lateral femoral sulcus is a related finding and a secondary sign of
cruciate rupture. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

### Management Overview

The lesion itself is managed expectantly. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Its significance is as corroboration and as a marker of possible overlying
chondral injury. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- It is trabecular microfracture, haemorrhage and oedema without a cortical break.
- It is present in most acute cruciate ruptures.
- The pivot pattern is lateral femoral condyle and posterolateral tibial plateau.
- A different distribution suggests a different mechanism.
- It is managed expectantly and read as corroboration.

## MRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

In a viva bone bruising should be used rather than merely named.

The expected points are the pathology, the near universality in acute rupture,
the pivot distribution, the alternative distributions, and the relationship to
chondral injury. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

Say that the pattern corroborates the history, which makes it evidence rather
than an incidental finding. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Say that an unexpected pattern should change the search rather than be
reported and ignored. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

### Management Overview

No direct treatment, and no delay to surgery on account of the bruise alone.
6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The overlying cartilage is the thing worth following, because that is where an
irreversible consequence would be. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

### Complications and Prognosis

Persistent pain attributable to bruising is common in the early months and is
frequently misattributed to the reconstruction. 6.2.5 covers acute
haemarthrosis.

Severe bruising with overlying chondral damage is the combination with long term
consequence, and it is the reason the finding is not dismissed. 6.5.3 covers
cartilage degeneration.

### Key Learning Points

- Use bone bruising rather than merely naming it.
- The pattern corroborates the history and is evidence.
- An unexpected pattern should change the search.
- There is no direct treatment and no delay on its account.
- Severe bruising with overlying chondral damage is the consequential combination.

## FRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

At examination level the candidate is expected to read the bruise as a map of
the injury rather than as an item on a list.

The distribution identifies the mechanism, the mechanism predicts the associated
injuries, and the associated injuries decide the operation. 6.2.4 covers
combined injuries.

An anteromedial tibial bruise alongside the lateral pair suggests a
hyperextension component and raises the posterior structures. 6.2.3 covers
hyperextension injury.

Medial compartment bruising in a valgus mechanism raises the medial structures
rather than the cruciate alone. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

### Complications and Prognosis

The prognostic question is whether bruise severity predicts later cartilage
change, and the finding is suggestive rather than established. 6.5.3 covers
cartilage degeneration.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether bruise volume or severity predicts
post-traumatic osteoarthritis independently of meniscal status. 6.5.4 covers
osteoarthritis.

The second is whether the overlying cartilage injury is caused at impact or
develops afterwards, which bears on whether anything could intervene. 6.4.6
covers cartilage injuries.

The third is whether persistent bruising should delay return to sport. 6.1.4
covers the natural history.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

### Key Learning Points

- Read the bruise as a map of the injury.
- Distribution gives mechanism, mechanism gives company, company gives operation.
- An anteromedial bruise raises the posterior structures.
- Whether severity predicts later cartilage change is suggestive, not established.
- Whether the chondral injury happens at impact or later is unresolved.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level bone bruising is the clearest example in the group of a
finding that is reported on every scan and used on very few.

It is free information about the mechanism, available months after the event
when the history has become vague, and most reports note it without anybody
acting on it. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Its practical use in a chronic knee is to date and characterise an injury the
patient describes imprecisely, because the pattern persists longer than the
patient's account stays reliable. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Its practical use in an acute knee is to prompt a deliberate look at the
cartilage above it, which is the only part of this finding that is
irreversible. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

A fellow who treats it as noise loses both, and the second loss is permanent.
6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

### Key Learning Points

- It is reported on every scan and used on very few.
- It is free information about the mechanism.
- In a chronic knee it characterises an injury the patient describes vaguely.
- In an acute knee it prompts a deliberate look at the cartilage above.
- Treating it as noise loses both, and the second loss is permanent.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant use of this finding is in explaining a recovery that is slower
than the patient expected without implying anything has gone wrong.

A patient whose knee aches months after a technically successful operation is
commonly carrying resolving bone bruising, and the alternative explanations they
reach for are worse. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Naming it early, before it is needed, is more convincing than producing it later
as an explanation for disappointment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Where it becomes a genuine decision is the severely bruised knee with overlying
cartilage damage, and the five things decide what to say. The patient's age and
physiology, which set how many decades that cartilage must serve. Their medical
comorbidities, particularly body mass, which changes the load it carries. The
demands they put on the knee, which decide whether the surface will be tested.
Their psychological state, because a cartilage conversation frightens people
more than a ligament one. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations,
including what they have already read about arthritis after this injury.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

### Key Learning Points

- It explains a slow recovery without implying something went wrong.
- Naming it early is more convincing than producing it later.
- The genuine decision is the bruised knee with overlying cartilage damage.
- A cartilage conversation frightens people more than a ligament one.
- Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the
  patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide what to say.
