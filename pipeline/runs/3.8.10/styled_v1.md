# Inlay Techniques

Attaching the graft to the back of the tibia directly, so it never turns a
corner. It solves one problem and asks for a different exposure.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

If a rope wears where it turns a corner, one answer is to remove the corner
rather than to protect it.

Attaching the end of the rope to the surface it needs to reach, instead of
threading it through a hole, removes the turn entirely.

The cost is reaching that surface, which may be harder to get to.

### At the Knee

A transtibial posterior cruciate graft turns sharply at the back of the tibial
tunnel, which is where it is abraded. 3.8.8 covers the construct.

An inlay technique fixes a bone block from the graft directly into a trough on
the back of the tibia, so the graft runs straight from there to the femur.
2.8.6 covers the surgical anatomy.

Removing the turn removes the abrasion and the tension lost to friction, which
is the mechanical case for it.

The cost is the exposure. Reaching the back of the tibia means a posterior
approach or a different patient position, which is why the technique is not
universal.

### What Changes It

The fixation achieved in the tibial trough, which is bone to bone.

The graft chosen, since a bone block is needed at that end.

Patient positioning and the approach used.

Corner integrity, which is unchanged by the choice of technique. 2.10.4 covers
the corner.

### Clinical Relevance

The mechanical advantage is real and conventional teaching has not shown a
consistent clinical advantage, which is the same pattern as the bundle
question. 3.8.9 covers that.

The approach's demands are the reason it remains a selected rather than a
default technique. Section 7 covers technique.

It becomes more attractive where a tibial tunnel would converge with another
tunnel. 3.8.8 covers tunnel planning.

Comparing the two techniques means comparing a construct advantage against an
operative cost.

### Key Learning Points

- One answer to a rope wearing at a corner is to remove the corner.
- An inlay fixes a bone block into a trough on the back of the tibia.
- The graft then runs straight, with no abrasion and no tension lost to friction.
- The cost is the exposure needed to reach the back of the tibia.
- The mechanical advantage is real and the clinical advantage is not consistent.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Two mechanical gains are claimed and they are separable: no graft angle at the
tibial aperture, and bone to bone fixation at the tibial end rather than soft
tissue in a tunnel. 3.7.17 covers the healing difference.

The second gain matters earlier and the first matters throughout. Bone to bone
healing is faster, and the absent turn removes a continuing abrasion.

Against them sits an operative cost that is not mechanical at all, which is
why the comparison has never been clean.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching reports less graft attenuation and less tension loss
with inlay fixation in cadaveric and animal comparisons.

Clinical comparative studies report similar laxity and outcome between inlay
and transtibial techniques, in small numbers.

The posterior approach carries its own risks given the popliteal structures,
which is a surgical rather than a biomechanical consideration. 2.8.6 covers
the surgical anatomy.

An arthroscopic inlay or an all inside variant removes part of the exposure
cost, which changes the comparison as techniques develop.

### What Changes It

Fixation achieved in the trough and the bone quality there.

Graft type and whether a bone block is available.

Approach and position, and whether an arthroscopic variant is used.

Whether other tunnels are being drilled, which may favour avoiding one.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric construct testing compares graft tension loss and failure between
techniques.

Animal models compare graft attenuation over time.

Clinical comparative studies measure laxity and outcome, in small numbers.

Stress radiography is the reproducible clinical measure of the result.

### Clinical Relevance

Separate the two claimed gains, since they act at different times.

Expect less attenuation mechanically and similar outcomes clinically.

Account for the posterior approach's own risks in the decision.

Reconsider the comparison as arthroscopic variants develop.

### Key Learning Points

- Two separable gains: no graft angle, and bone to bone tibial fixation.
- Bone to bone healing matters earlier; the absent turn matters throughout.
- Conventional teaching reports less attenuation and tension loss with inlay.
- Clinical studies report similar laxity and outcome, in small numbers.
- The operative cost is not mechanical, which is why the comparison is not clean.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat the choice as a construct advantage traded against an operative cost,
and note that the trade has shifted as arthroscopic techniques have developed.

The mechanical argument is strongest in revision, where a previous tibial
tunnel may be wide or malpositioned and an inlay avoids using it at all. 3.8.8
covers the construct.

It is also strongest in the multiligament knee, where avoiding a tibial tunnel
removes a convergence risk with a corner reconstruction. 2.10.4 covers the
corner.

### What Changes It

Previous tunnels and their condition in revision.

Other tunnels planned in the same operation.

Graft availability with a bone block.

Surgeon familiarity with the approach.

### How It Is Measured

Computed tomography assesses previous tunnels and plans the approach.

Cadaveric work gives the construct comparison.

Clinical series compare techniques in small numbers and without revision
subgroups.

No study has compared the techniques specifically in revision, where the case
is strongest.

### Clinical Relevance

Consider inlay in revision where a previous tibial tunnel is wide or
malpositioned.

Consider it in a multiligament knee to avoid tunnel convergence.

Plan the approach and position from the imaging rather than from habit.

Note that the revision subgroup has no comparative evidence.

### Key Learning Points

- A construct advantage traded against an operative cost.
- The trade has shifted as arthroscopic techniques developed.
- The case is strongest in revision and in the multiligament knee.
- Avoiding a tibial tunnel removes a convergence risk.
- The revision subgroup has no comparative evidence.

## Fellowship Level

### At the Knee

At fellowship level the inlay is a tool for the compromised tibia: a wide
previous tunnel, a malpositioned one, or a tibia already carrying other
hardware.

Choosing it is a planning decision made from imaging before theatre, because
the approach and the position have to be set up for it. 3.8.8 covers tunnel
planning.

Conventional teaching accepts both techniques as reasonable, which puts the
choice with the surgeon's judgement about the particular tibia rather than
with a general rule.

### What Changes It

The state of the tibia, including tunnels and hardware.

Whether a posterior approach is already being used for another reconstruction.

Available graft with a bone block.

Patient positioning constraints in a multiligament case.

### How It Is Measured

Computed tomography maps the tibia, the tunnels and the hardware.

Intraoperative assessment confirms the trough and the fixation achieved.

Postoperative stress radiography measures the result.

No measurement predicts which technique suits a given compromised tibia.

### Clinical Relevance

Plan the technique from preoperative imaging of the tibia.

Set up the position and approach for the technique chosen rather than deciding
in theatre.

Use the inlay where the tibia is compromised and a tunnel is unattractive.

Accept that the choice rests on judgement about the particular tibia.

### Key Learning Points

- The inlay is a tool for the compromised tibia.
- It is a planning decision made from imaging before theatre.
- The approach and position have to be set up for it.
- Both techniques are accepted as reasonable, so judgement decides.
- No measurement predicts which technique suits a given compromised tibia.
