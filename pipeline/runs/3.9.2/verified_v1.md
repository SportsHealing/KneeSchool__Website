# Rotational Stability

The medial side resists the tibia turning outwards. That job belongs to the
deep layer and the corner rather than to the long superficial ligament.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A long ligament running down a joint resists the joint opening. A short one
attached close to the joint line resists the bones turning on each other.

Length is the reason. A long structure changes length little when the bones
rotate, so it is not loaded by rotation.

So a joint needing both coronal and rotational control needs structures of
both kinds.

### At the Knee

The deep medial collateral ligament is short and attaches to the meniscus and
the tibia close to the joint line. It resists rotation. 2.9.2 covers the
anatomy.

The superficial medial collateral ligament is long and spans from the femoral
epicondyle to the tibia below the joint line. It resists valgus. 3.9.3 covers
it.

The posterior oblique ligament and the posteromedial capsule add rotational
restraint near extension. 3.9.5 covers the posterior oblique ligament.

External rotation of the tibia is the motion the medial structures resist, and
internal rotation is resisted more laterally. 3.2.4 covers external rotation.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes which structure is taut.

Which layer is injured, since the layers do different jobs.

The anterior cruciate, which shares rotational restraint. 3.7.7 covers that.

Muscle activity, particularly the semimembranosus and the medial hamstrings.
2.9.5 covers the semimembranosus.

### Clinical Relevance

Rotational laxity on the medial side is the finding that matters, and it is
easier to miss than valgus laxity. Section 4 covers the tests.

Anteromedial rotatory instability is the pattern it produces, and it has its
own mechanics. 3.9.7 covers it.

A superficial ligament injury alone does not produce rotational laxity, which
is why the finding implicates the deeper structures.

Repairing or reconstructing the medial side has to address rotation as well as
valgus. 3.9.8 covers the construct.

### Key Learning Points

- A long ligament resists opening; a short one near the joint line resists rotation.
- The deep medial collateral ligament is short and resists rotation.
- The superficial ligament is long and resists valgus.
- The medial structures resist external rotation of the tibia.
- Rotational laxity implicates the deeper structures rather than the superficial one.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Rotational restraint depends on a structure's orientation relative to the axis
of rotation. A structure running parallel to the axis is not loaded by
rotation about it.

The medial structures lie at different distances and orientations, so their
rotational contributions differ even where their valgus contributions are
similar.

Rotatory laxity is a coupled motion, so it is harder to apportion than a pure
one. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives the deep medial collateral ligament and the
posteromedial capsule the main medial contribution to restraining external
rotation, with the superficial ligament contributing less.

The posterior oblique ligament's contribution is greatest near extension,
where it is taut and the capsule with it. 3.9.5 covers it.

The anterior cruciate contributes to rotational restraint, so a combined
injury produces a larger rotatory laxity than either alone. 3.9.10 covers
combined injury.

Anteromedial rotatory instability is the clinical pattern: anterior
translation of the medial tibial plateau with external rotation. 3.9.7 covers
it.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, through which structures are taut.

Which layer and which structure is injured.

Cruciate integrity, which shares the restraint.

Meniscal attachment integrity, since the deep layer attaches to the meniscus.
3.6.9 covers roots.

### How It Is Measured

Sectioning studies with an applied external rotation torque apportion the
restraint by structure and angle.

The anteromedial drawer and the dial test assess it clinically, with the
second also testing the lateral side. Section 4 covers the tests.

Rotatory laxity has no instrument with the reproducibility of a valgus stress
radiograph, which limits quantification.

Examination under anaesthetic is where the rotational findings are most
reliably obtained.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess external rotation as well as valgus on the medial side.

Expect a larger rotatory laxity where a cruciate is also injured.

Treat the absence of a reproducible instrument as a reason to examine under
anaesthetic.

Consider the meniscal attachment, since the deep layer attaches to it.

### Key Learning Points

- A structure parallel to the rotation axis is not loaded by rotation about it.
- The deep ligament and posteromedial capsule carry the main medial rotational restraint.
- The posterior oblique ligament's contribution is greatest near extension.
- Anteromedial rotatory instability is anterior translation of the medial plateau with external rotation.
- No instrument measures medial rotatory laxity as reproducibly as valgus stress radiography.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat rotational laxity as the finding that decides whether a medial injury is
operative, and valgus laxity as the finding that is easier to obtain.

Conventional teaching holds that a medial injury with rotatory laxity, or one
that fails to heal, is the operative subgroup, and that isolated valgus laxity
usually is not. Section 6 covers management.

In reconstruction, addressing rotation means reconstructing the posterior
oblique ligament or the deep layer in addition to the superficial, which is
why anatomic reconstruction is specified. 3.9.8 covers the construct.

### What Changes It

Which structures are injured and whether the corner is involved.

Concurrent cruciate reconstruction, which shares the rotational restraint.

Alignment, which loads the reconstruction. 3.13 covers alignment.

Chronicity, since a chronically rotationally lax knee has stretched its
remaining restraints.

### How It Is Measured

Examination under anaesthetic with the knee at several angles is the practical
assessment.

Valgus stress radiography quantifies the coronal component and not the
rotational one.

Robotic sectioning work apportions the rotational restraint and is where the
reconstruction targets come from.

No clinical measurement quantifies medial rotatory laxity reproducibly.

### Clinical Relevance

Decide the operative question on rotational rather than coronal laxity.

Reconstruct the posterior oblique ligament or deep layer where rotation is
lax.

Examine under anaesthetic, since no instrument substitutes.

Correct alignment where it would load the reconstruction.

### Key Learning Points

- Rotational laxity decides whether a medial injury is operative.
- Isolated valgus laxity usually is not operative.
- Addressing rotation means reconstructing the posterior oblique ligament or the deep layer.
- No clinical measurement quantifies medial rotatory laxity reproducibly.
- A chronically lax knee has stretched its remaining restraints.
