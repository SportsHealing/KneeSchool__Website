# Anterolateral Ligament

A structure argued about for a century: what can be found at the anterolateral
knee, where it attaches, and why its name is the contested part.

## For Medical Students

### Structure and Location

The anterolateral ligament is a thickening of the anterolateral capsule
running from the lateral femur to the anterolateral tibia.

Its femoral attachment is close to the lateral epicondyle, usually described
as just behind and above it, near the attachment of the lateral collateral
ligament.

Its tibial attachment is between Gerdy's tubercle and the head of the fibula,
about 5 to 10 mm below the joint line.

On the way it attaches to the lateral meniscus, which is the feature that
distinguishes it from the capsule around it.

It was described by Segond in 1879 as a pearly resistant band, named and
renamed many times since, and described again in 2013 in the paper that
brought it back into use.

### Relations

Superficial to it: the iliotibial band and its deep layers, which 2.11.1
covers.

Deep to it: the lateral meniscus and the joint.

Behind and below: the lateral collateral ligament, which is a separate
structure running to the fibula rather than to the tibia.

Its tibial attachment lies in the gap between Gerdy's tubercle in front and
the fibular head behind.

### Function

It restrains internal rotation of the tibia, particularly in flexion.

It contributes to the control of the pivot shift, which is the movement a torn
anterior cruciate ligament allows.

Its meniscal attachment means it also tethers the lateral meniscus anteriorly.

### Clinical Relevance

Its tibial attachment is the site of the Segond fracture, which 2.11.4 covers.

Whether it is a distinct ligament or a thickening of capsule is still argued.
What is not argued is that something at the anterolateral knee restrains
rotation.

Finding it depends on how it is looked for. A dissection from the surface
divides it with the capsule; a dissection from the deep surface finds it.

### Key Learning Points

- It runs from near the lateral epicondyle to the tibia between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head.
- Its tibial attachment is about 5 to 10 mm below the joint line.
- It attaches to the lateral meniscus on the way.
- It restrains internal rotation, particularly in flexion.
- Segond described it in 1879; it was described again in 2013.

## MRCS Level

### Structure and Location

Describe the femoral attachment carefully, because this is where descriptions
differ most. Reports place it anterior to, posterior to, or coincident with
the lateral collateral ligament's attachment, and the disagreement is real
rather than careless.

The tibial attachment is more consistent. Conventional teaching places its
centre about mid way between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head, about 7 mm
below the joint line.

Conventional teaching gives the ligament a length of the order of 35 mm and a
thickness of a few millimetres, which makes it thin relative to its length.

Identification rates in dissection studies range from about half to
essentially all, and the range tracks the dissection method rather than the
population.

### Relations

It sits in the anterolateral capsule, which is layer three anteriorly in the
scheme 2.10.4 uses.

The lateral inferior genicular artery runs close to its tibial attachment.

The capsulo-osseous layer of the iliotibial band lies superficial and slightly
posterior, and the two are close enough that a dissection can take them as
one. 2.11.1 covers the layer.

Its meniscal attachment is to the body of the lateral meniscus, in the region
the popliteal hiatus leaves otherwise untethered.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

Supply is from the lateral inferior genicular artery and from capsular
vessels, which is to say it shares the supply of the capsule it sits in.

Nerve supply is from the lateral articular branches, including the recurrent
articular branch of the common peroneal nerve.

Neither supply distinguishes it from the capsule, which is one of the
arguments used against calling it a separate ligament.

### Function

It restrains internal rotation, with its contribution rising between about 30
and 90 degrees of flexion.

Sectioning it in a cruciate deficient knee increases the pivot shift.
Sectioning it in an intact knee changes little, which tells you it is a
secondary restraint whose role appears when the primary one has gone. 3.11.3
carries the figures.

Its meniscal attachment makes it a tether for the lateral meniscus in the
segment where the hiatus removes the coronary attachment.

### Clinical Relevance

Read the Segond fragment as evidence of this attachment rather than as a
fracture in its own right, and 2.11.4 covers it.

Expect the pivot shift to be the clinical consequence. The ligament's
contribution appears when the cruciate has failed, and 3.11.4 covers the test.

Treat variable identification as a method problem rather than a population
problem. How a dissection approaches the capsule decides what it finds.

Avoid arguing about the name at the expense of the function. Something
restrains rotation there, and 2.11.5 is where that argument is held.

### Key Learning Points

- The femoral attachment is where descriptions genuinely differ; the tibial one is consistent.
- Its tibial centre is about mid way between Gerdy's tubercle and the fibular head, about 7 mm below the joint line.
- Conventional teaching gives a length of about 35 mm and a thickness of a few millimetres.
- Sectioning it matters in a cruciate deficient knee and changes little in an intact one.
- Identification rates track dissection method, not population.

## FRCS Level

### Relations

Its proximity to the capsulo-osseous layer of the iliotibial band is the
practical problem. Two structures within a few millimetres, both restraining
internal rotation, and a dissection plane that can include either or both.

Its tibial attachment is the only part that is reliably reproducible between
studies, which is why reconstructions are described from the tibia outwards.

The meniscal attachment means a graft routed along its path does not reproduce
it. No graft attaches to a meniscus.

### Function

Treat it as a secondary restraint that matters in a cruciate deficient knee.
That is the finding every sectioning study agrees on, whatever it calls the
structure.

Its contribution and the Kaplan fibres' contribution cannot be separated
clinically, which is why 2.11.5 treats the complex as the unit and 3.11.8
carries the combined reconstruction evidence.

The femoral attachment's variability means a reconstruction cannot be
anatomical in the sense a cruciate reconstruction can. The target is a
function rather than a footprint.

### Clinical Relevance

Consider an anterolateral procedure in a knee with a high grade pivot shift, a
revision cruciate reconstruction, or generalised laxity, and 3.11.8 carries
the indications.

Plan from the tibial attachment, which is consistent, rather than from the
femoral one, which is not.

Accept that the construct reproduces a function and not a structure, and say
so, because the language of anatomical reconstruction does not apply here.

Avoid over-tensioning in extension. Over-constraint of rotation is the
complication this anatomy makes available.

### Key Learning Points

- Two structures within a few millimetres both restrain internal rotation; the plane can take either.
- The tibial attachment is the reproducible one; the femoral attachment is not.
- No graft reproduces the meniscal attachment.
- It is a secondary restraint whose role appears when the cruciate has failed.
- The construct reproduces a function, not a footprint.

## Fellowship Level

### Clinical Relevance

The argument is about naming, not about function. Three structures at the
anterolateral knee restrain internal rotation: the Kaplan fibres, the
capsulo-osseous layer of the iliotibial band, and this. Studies that find a
discrete ligament and studies that find a capsular thickening are often
describing the same tissue approached differently, and a position on the
nomenclature changes no operation.

Reconstruction targets a function. Because the femoral attachment is not
reproducible between studies, a construct is positioned by what it should
restrain and at what flexion angle, rather than by a footprint. That is a
different kind of planning from a cruciate reconstruction and it is worth
saying out loud.

Over-constraint is the complication the anatomy makes available. A short, thin
structure replaced by a stiffer graft tensioned in extension produces a knee
that rotates less than normal, and the patient notices that in a way they do
not notice a slightly positive pivot shift.

Indication is the live question rather than technique. 3.11.8 and 3.11.9 carry
the evidence and the controversies; the anatomy here says only that there is
something to reconstruct and roughly where it runs.

The Segond fragment is the one piece of direct evidence a surgeon ever sees. A
patient with one has a demonstrated attachment at that point, which is more
than most knees offer, and 2.11.4 covers what the fragment contains.

### Key Learning Points

- The argument is about naming; three structures share the function and the dispute changes no operation.
- Reconstruction targets a function, because the femoral footprint is not reproducible.
- Over-constraint is noticed by patients in a way a slight pivot shift is not.
- Indication is the live question; the anatomy says only that there is something to reconstruct.
- A Segond fragment is the one piece of direct evidence of the attachment a surgeon sees.
