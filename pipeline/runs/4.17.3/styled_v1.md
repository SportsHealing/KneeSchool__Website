# Osteochondral Lesions

Cartilage with its bone attached, which makes the lesion repairable and the
timing matter.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

An osteochondral lesion is a defect involving both the cartilage and the bone
beneath it, usually from an acute impaction or a patellar dislocation.
4.2.22 covers the dislocation event.

Examining for it means establishing the mechanism, finding an effusion, and
looking for a mechanical symptom, because the fragment may be loose. 4.17.2
covers mechanical symptoms.

It matters because an osteochondral fragment with bone attached can be fixed
back, and that window closes, which makes this the most time sensitive
diagnosis in the chapter. 4.8.7 covers the presentation it shares.

### How It Is Performed

Establish the mechanism and the timing: an acute impaction, a dislocation, or a
twisting injury with a sudden tense effusion. 4.2.3 covers swelling as a
symptom.

Assess the effusion and its tension, since a tense effusion within hours is
blood and an osteochondral injury is on that short differential. 4.8.7 covers
it.

Palpate the compartments and the patellar facets, since the commonest sites are
a femoral condyle and the patella after a dislocation. 4.6.5 covers patellar
tenderness.

Measure the passive extension and record the end feel, since a displaced
fragment gives a block. 4.7.5 covers it.

Examine the patella's medial restraint if the mechanism was a dislocation, since
the two injuries travel together. 4.15.7 covers that assessment.

### What a Positive Means

An acute tense effusion after an impaction or a dislocation raises an
osteochondral injury, and that raises the urgency of the imaging rather than
waiting for the swelling to settle. 4.8.7 covers it.

A block to extension with a springy end feel indicates a displaced fragment and
is urgent. 4.7.5 covers the end feel.

Medial patellar tenderness with a haemarthrosis after a twisting injury
indicates a dislocation, and an osteochondral injury should be assumed until the
imaging says otherwise. 4.2.22 covers the event.

A normal examination weeks later in a patient with the right mechanism does not
exclude the lesion, and the repair window may already have closed. 4.17.1
covers the presentation.

Recurrent effusions and catching months after an injury suggest a fragment that
was missed. 4.17.2 covers the symptoms.

### Key Learning Points

- The lesion involves both cartilage and the bone beneath it.
- A fragment with bone attached can be fixed back, and the window closes.
- A tense effusion within hours of an impaction or a dislocation raises it.
- A springy block to extension means a displaced fragment and is urgent.
- A normal examination weeks later does not exclude it, and the window may have closed.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the time sensitive diagnosis in the acute knee, and the
expected answer links the haemarthrosis to the short differential.

The expected points are the mechanism, the tense effusion, the compartment and
patellar palpation, the extension with its end feel, and the patellar restraint
assessment.

Say that an acute haemarthrosis has a short differential and that this is on it,
which is why the imaging is early rather than delayed. 4.8.7 covers it.

Say that a fragment with bone can be fixed and that a purely cartilaginous one
usually cannot, which is why the distinction matters. 4.17.5 covers the
cartilage only defect.

Say that the repair window is measured in weeks, which is the reason for the
urgency rather than a general preference for speed.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that bone heals to bone, so a fragment carrying
subchondral bone can be fixed and will unite, where cartilage alone will not.
3.5.1 covers contact areas.

That is why the mechanism matters: an impaction or a dislocation shears bone with
the cartilage, where a degenerative process delaminates cartilage alone.
4.17.5 covers the second.

A patellar dislocation shears the medial patellar facet or the lateral femoral
condyle, which is why those are the sites to palpate after that mechanism.
2.4.5 covers the surgical relevance.

A displaced fragment obstructs the joint, which is the mechanism of the block
and the reason the end feel is firm or springy rather than leathery. 4.7.5
covers the reading.

### How Good It Is

The examination raises the possibility and does not make the diagnosis, which
imaging does, and in this condition the examination's value is the urgency it
creates. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

Its most useful findings are the tense acute effusion and the block, both of
which are reliable when present.

It is insensitive. A lesion without a displaced fragment produces an effusion and
little else, so the mechanism carries more weight than the findings.

Radiographs miss a purely cartilaginous fragment and show a bony one, which is
why the mechanism should drive the cross sectional imaging. 4.1.6 covers it.

and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Bone heals to bone, so a fragment carrying subchondral bone can be fixed.
- An impaction or a dislocation shears bone with the cartilage; a degenerative process does not.
- A dislocation shears the medial patellar facet or the lateral femoral condyle.
- The examination's value is the urgency it creates rather than the diagnosis.
- Radiographs show a bony fragment and miss a cartilaginous one.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this is the diagnosis in the chapter where delay changes the
operation, so the examination's job is to get the imaging done early.

An acute fragment with bone is fixed, and the result of fixation is better than
the result of any later reconstruction, which is why the window matters.

A displaced fragment with a block is an urgent arthroscopy regardless of the
rest of the assessment. 4.7.5 covers the finding.

A fragment after a patellar dislocation is the commonest version in a young
patient, and the patellar assessment and the osteochondral assessment are done
together. 4.15.7 covers the first.

A lesion found late, with the fragment absent or unfixable, becomes a cartilage
defect problem and the decision changes category. 4.17.5 covers it.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is waiting for an acute effusion to settle before imaging,
which spends the window.

The second is attributing a haemarthrosis to a cruciate rupture by default.
4.8.7 covers that error.

The third is not examining the patella after a twisting injury with a
haemarthrosis, which misses the dislocation and its fragment. 4.2.22 covers it.

The fourth is relying on radiographs in a young patient, where a cartilaginous
fragment is invisible.

The fifth is reading a normal examination at six weeks as excluding the lesion.

### Key Learning Points

- Delay changes the operation, so the examination's job is early imaging.
- Fixation of an acute fragment beats any later reconstruction.
- A displaced fragment with a block is an urgent arthroscopy regardless of everything else.
- A fragment after a patellar dislocation is the commonest version in a young patient.
- Waiting for the effusion to settle before imaging spends the window.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the osteochondral lesion arrives late, and the referral is
about what can still be done.

A patient with a fragment that has been loose for months has lost the fixation
option, and the decision becomes a cartilage reconstruction one. 4.17.5 covers
it.

A patient with a haemarthrosis attributed to a cruciate injury who has an
osteochondral fragment has had the wrong diagnosis made from the right finding.
4.8.7 covers the presentation.

A patient with recurrent effusions and catching after a dislocation treated non
operatively has a missed fragment, and the history is where that is caught.
4.17.2 covers the symptoms.

A patient with a fixed fragment and persisting pain needs the alignment and the
meniscal status assessed, since both determine whether the fixation survives.
4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

In medicolegal work a missed osteochondral fragment after a documented
dislocation is the most defensible form of criticism in this chapter, because
the mechanism and the window are both well described. 4.23.7 covers that
setting.

### Key Learning Points

- A fragment loose for months has lost the fixation option.
- A haemarthrosis attributed to a cruciate injury can be the wrong diagnosis from the right finding.
- Recurrent effusions and catching after a dislocation suggest a missed fragment.
- After fixation the alignment and the meniscal status determine survival.
- A missed fragment after a documented dislocation is the most defensible criticism here.
