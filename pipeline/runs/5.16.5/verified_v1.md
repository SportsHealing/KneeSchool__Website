# Osteotomy Planning

A correction planned on a loaded radiograph, with computed tomography supplying
the rotation and the joint surface the film cannot show.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

Osteotomy planning establishes where a limb's deformity sits and how much
correction is needed. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

The plan is built on a weight bearing long leg radiograph, with computed
tomography added for rotation and for the joint surface. 5.3.6 covers the long
leg alignment films.

It matters because an osteotomy moves load from one compartment to another, and
the amount moved is set by the geometry. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

### How It Is Acquired

Start from a standing long leg film, because the deformity that matters is the
loaded one. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

Establish whether the deformity is femoral, tibial or both. 5.3.8 covers the
anatomical axis.

Establish whether it is intra-articular, since a bone cut cannot correct a worn
surface. 5.3.9 covers the joint line obliquity.

Add computed tomography where rotation or the articular surface is part of the
deformity. 5.16.3 covers the rotational profiles.

Record the compartment that will receive the load after correction. 5.3.17
covers joint space narrowing.

### What Normal Looks Like

A standing film that includes the hip and the ankle. 5.3.6 covers the long leg
alignment films.

Both the femoral and the tibial contribution stated separately. 5.3.8 covers the
anatomical axis.

The joint line orientation measured and stated. 5.3.9 covers the joint line
obliquity.

The receiving compartment documented as preserved. 5.3.17 covers joint space
narrowing.

The patellofemoral joint assessed, because a tibial correction moves it too.
5.12.7 covers the patellofemoral arthritis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

A deformity attributed to the tibia when the femur carries part of it. 5.3.10
covers the deformity analysis.

An intra-articular deformity planned as though it were extra-articular. 5.3.9
covers the joint line obliquity.

Rotation unmeasured in a limb being corrected in one plane. 5.16.3 covers the
rotational profiles.

A receiving compartment already worn, which turns a correction into a transfer
of pain. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Planning done on a supine study, which loses the load. 5.1.6 covers imaging
pitfalls.

### Key Learning Points

- The deformity that matters is the loaded one.
- The femoral and tibial contributions are stated separately.
- A bone cut cannot correct a worn surface.
- Rotation belongs in the plan when it is part of the deformity.
- The receiving compartment has to be preserved.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer plans on the standing film and adds the scan for a
stated reason.

The expected points are the standing long leg film, the level of the deformity,
the intra-articular component, the rotation, and the receiving compartment.

Say that a supine study cannot carry the alignment question. 5.3.6 covers the
long leg alignment films.

Say that computed tomography contributes rotation and surface rather than
alignment. 5.16.3 covers the rotational profiles.

Say that the patellofemoral joint changes with a tibial correction. 3.12.12
covers the tibial tubercle position.

### What Normal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the correction is calculated from the mechanical
axis and the joint line, so the film has to contain both the hip and the ankle
to be usable at all. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Deformity analysis localises the apex, and correcting at the wrong level
restores the axis while leaving the joint line oblique. 5.3.9 covers the joint
line obliquity.

A rotational component corrected only in the coronal plane leaves the limb
malrotated. 5.16.3 covers the rotational profiles.

The soft tissue and the ligament state decide whether the corrected limb is
stable, and the scan does not carry that. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

### How Good It Is

Radiographic planning is the best available method for a knee osteotomy, and
computed tomography is the best available addition to it.

Its best contribution is the loaded mechanical axis with the deformity
localised. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

It is limited by positioning on the standing film, by supine scanning, and by
the soft tissues it cannot assess. 5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no
correction values, axis angles or planning thresholds appear either: those are
published quantities tied to particular techniques and this environment cannot
check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The film has to contain the hip and the ankle.
- Correcting at the wrong level leaves the joint line oblique.
- A coronal correction leaves rotation uncorrected.
- The ligament state decides stability after correction.
- Positioning on a standing film is a real limitation.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the imaging decides the level, the plane and the size of the
correction. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

It also decides against an osteotomy, when the receiving compartment or the
patellofemoral joint rules one out. 5.12.7 covers the patellofemoral arthritis.

A tubercle transfer is planned from the same geometry and interacts with any
tibial correction. 5.16.4 covers the TT-TG measurement.

A rotational correction is planned from the scan and changes the coronal plan.
5.16.3 covers the rotational profiles.

The post-operative imaging has to repeat the same measurements by the same
method to mean anything. 5.17.4 covers the osteotomy.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is planning from a film that does not include both joints.
5.3.6 covers the long leg alignment films.

Rotation of the limb on the standing film changes the apparent deformity. 5.1.6
covers imaging pitfalls.

Flexion on the standing film shortens the apparent femur and alters the axis.
5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

A supine scan is used to confirm an alignment measured standing, and the two
disagree legitimately. 5.18.5 covers weight-bearing MRI.

An intra-articular deformity is corrected extra-articularly, which moves the
load without levelling the surface. 5.3.9 covers the joint line obliquity.

### Key Learning Points

- A film without both joints cannot be planned from.
- Limb rotation changes the apparent deformity.
- Flexion on the film alters the measured axis.
- A supine scan and a standing film disagree legitimately.
- An extra-articular correction does not level an intra-articular deformity.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the limb has usually been corrected once already and the
question is what remains. 5.17.4 covers the osteotomy.

A previous correction moves the reference points, so the pre-operative films are
the only comparable ones. 5.12.8 covers the post-operative imaging.

Deformity in three planes is planned plane by plane, and the planes interact.
5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

A limb corrected into a position the ligaments cannot hold looks correct on the
film. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Planning software fixes the landmark rules, which removes variability and hides
the uncertainty in the landmarks themselves. 5.18.8 covers artificial
intelligence in imaging.

The useful report gives the standing film with both joints, the deformity
localised, the intra-articular component, the rotation, the receiving
compartment, and the patellofemoral joint. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- A previous correction moves the reference points.
- The planes interact and are planned separately.
- A film cannot show whether the ligaments hold the correction.
- Software removes variability and hides landmark uncertainty.
- The report should state the receiving compartment.
