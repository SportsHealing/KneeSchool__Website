# Mechanical Axis Assessment

The line that decides which part of the knee carries the load, and what the
hands can and cannot say about it.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

The mechanical axis of the limb runs from the centre of the hip to the centre of
the ankle, and where it passes through the knee determines how the load is
shared between the compartments. 3.13.1 covers the axis.

Assessing it clinically means estimating that line from the outside, which is a
screen rather than a measurement. The measurement is a long leg standing
radiograph. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

It belongs in an examination section because the alignment decides whether a
ligament reconstruction is protected or loaded, and that changes the operation.
4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

### How It Is Performed

Stand the patient up with the feet together and the knees exposed, and look from
the front at eye level. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Note whether the knees touch with the ankles apart, or the ankles touch with the
knees apart, and estimate the gap.

Look along the limb from hip to ankle and judge whether the knee lies medial or
lateral to that line.

Look from the side and note the sagittal alignment, since a flexion deformity or
hyperextension changes the loading as well. 4.5.4 covers flexion deformity.

Watch the patient walk, because the alignment under load is a different and more
consequential finding than the static one. 4.5.7 covers dynamic alignment.

### What a Positive Means

A knee lying medial to the hip to ankle line is in varus, and the medial
compartment carries more of the load. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

A knee lying lateral to that line is in valgus, and the lateral compartment
carries more. 4.5.3 covers valgus alignment.

Varus unloads the medial ligaments, which is why a medial reconstruction in a
varus limb has little holding it in tension. 4.12.8 covers that assessment.

Valgus loads the medial side continuously, which is why a medial reconstruction
in a valgus limb stretches. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

A symmetrical appearance in both limbs is usually constitutional and is not a
finding on its own. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

### Key Learning Points

- The axis runs from the centre of the hip to the centre of the ankle.
- Where it passes through the knee determines how load is shared between compartments.
- The clinical assessment is a screen; the long leg standing radiograph is the measurement.
- Varus unloads the medial ligaments and valgus loads them continuously.
- A symmetrical appearance in both limbs is usually constitutional.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected answer separates the clinical estimate from the
radiographic measurement, and says what each is for.

The expected points are the standing position, the front and side views, the
estimate of the line, the comparison of the two limbs, and the walk.

Say that the measurement requires a long leg standing radiograph and that the
clinical assessment decides whether to obtain one.

Say that you would distinguish the anatomical axis from the mechanical axis,
since the two differ and the distinction matters in planning. 3.13.2 covers the
anatomical axis.

Say that you would establish where in the limb the deformity sits, because a
femoral and a tibial deformity are corrected in different places. 4.3.10 covers
deformity.

Say that you would assess joint line obliquity, since two limbs with the same
mechanical axis can load differently. 3.13.6 covers it.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that load passes along the mechanical axis, so a line
passing medial to the knee's centre increases medial compartment load and
decreases lateral. 3.13.1 covers the axis.

That redistribution both accelerates wear in the loaded compartment and alters
the tension in the ligaments on each side, which is the mechanism linking
alignment to ligament surgery. 3.13.8 covers alignment and osteoarthritis.

Joint line obliquity modifies the distribution, which is why the assessment is a
measurement of the whole limb rather than of the knee. 3.13.6 covers it.

Alignment also changes under load as the collateral structures slacken, which
makes the dynamic finding more consequential than the static one. 3.13.7 covers
dynamic alignment.

### How Good It Is

The clinical estimate is a reliable screen for gross malalignment and a poor
measurement of any malalignment, which is the clean division this page rests
on.

It is unreliable for small deviations, in obese patients, and in anyone who
cannot stand with the feet together. 4.23.4 covers those patients.

It cannot localise the deformity between femur and tibia reliably, and the
correction is performed where the deformity is. 4.3.10 covers deformity.

Its value is deciding who needs the radiograph, which is a real contribution
made in two seconds. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Load passes along the mechanical axis, so a line medial to the knee's centre loads the medial compartment.
- The redistribution alters both compartment wear and ligament tension.
- Joint line obliquity means the assessment is of the whole limb rather than the knee.
- The clinical estimate cannot localise the deformity between femur and tibia.
- Its value is deciding who needs the radiograph.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the alignment decides whether a ligament operation will
survive, and that makes it the most consequential non ligamentous finding in
this section.

A varus limb with lateral ligament deficiency loads the lateral reconstruction
with every step, so the correction comes first or instead. 4.13.4 covers the
corner.

A valgus limb with medial deficiency is the mirror case, and a medial
reconstruction without correction stretches. 4.12.8 covers that assessment.

In the chronically cruciate deficient knee the alignment determines how fast the
compartment changes, which is part of the argument about reconstructing a
deficient knee that is not complaining. 4.11.6 covers it.

In revision the alignment is frequently the finding that explains the first
failure, and it is frequently absent from the first assessment. 4.10.12 covers
revision assessment.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not standing the patient up, which removes the finding
entirely.

The second is treating the clinical estimate as a measurement.

The third is assuming the deformity is at the knee.

The fourth is reading a constitutional varus as pathological, in a patient who
has walked on it for decades. 3.13.5 covers it.

The fifth is omitting the dynamic assessment, where a thrust turns a static
measurement into an urgent finding. 4.5.7 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- The alignment decides whether a ligament operation will survive.
- A varus limb with lateral deficiency needs the correction first or instead.
- In a chronically deficient knee the alignment determines how fast the compartment changes.
- In revision it frequently explains the first failure and is frequently missing from the first assessment.
- A thrust turns a static measurement into an urgent finding.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice the alignment is the commonest reason a soft tissue
question has a bony answer.

A patient with two competent reconstructions and a persisting complaint usually
has an uncorrected alignment or an abnormal tibial slope, and no further soft
tissue surgery addresses either. 4.14.5 covers that pattern.

A patient with a stretched lateral reconstruction in a varus limb had the
operations in the wrong order, and saying so plainly is part of the
consultation. 4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

In a knee with a previous osteotomy the alignment was deliberately changed, so
the question becomes whether the correction achieved the plan, and the
pre-operative films are required to answer it.

A patient with constitutional varus presents a judgement rather than a
measurement, because correcting a limb to a neutral axis it has never had is
not obviously the right target. 3.13.5 covers that argument.

In medicolegal work the pre-injury alignment is almost never recorded, which
makes attributing a later deformity to an injury difficult in both directions,
and the honest position is to say so. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

### Key Learning Points

- The alignment is the commonest reason a soft tissue question has a bony answer.
- Two competent reconstructions with a persisting complaint usually means alignment or slope.
- A stretched lateral reconstruction in a varus limb had the operations in the wrong order.
- After an osteotomy the question is whether the correction achieved the plan.
- Correcting a constitutional varus to a neutral axis is a judgement, not an obvious target.
