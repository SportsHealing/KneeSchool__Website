# Ramp Lesions

A tear at the back of the medial meniscus, in the part of the scan a reader
reaches last and the part a camera reaches only by looking behind.

## For Young Learners

### Definition

The meniscus is a cushion inside the knee. At its back edge it is attached to
the lining of the joint. 1.2.3 covers the menisci.

A ramp lesion is a tear where that attachment meets the meniscus, right at the
back of the knee. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

It often happens at the same moment as an ACL tear, and it is one of the easiest
things to miss. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Investigations and Imaging

It is hard to see on a scan and hard to see during an operation, because both
look at the knee from the front. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

### Key Learning Points

- The meniscus is attached to the joint lining at its back edge.
- A ramp lesion is a tear where that attachment meets the meniscus.
- It often happens at the same time as an ACL tear.
- It is one of the easiest injuries to miss.
- Both the scan and the camera look from the front.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent,
teacher, coach or doctor.

## For Patients

### Definition

A ramp lesion is a tear at the back of the medial meniscus, where it joins the
capsule lining the joint. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

It is common alongside ACL injuries and has been recognised more in recent years
because surgeons started looking for it deliberately. 6.2.4 covers combined
injuries.

### Investigations and Imaging

It can be difficult to see on a scan, because it sits at the back and the tear
can be subtle. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

During an operation it is seen only by passing the camera behind the cruciate to
look at the back of the joint, which is an extra step rather than part of the
routine view. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

### Management Overview

It can usually be repaired, and the repair is done at the same time as the
cruciate reconstruction. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Leaving it is thought to contribute to a knee that remains loose after an
otherwise good reconstruction. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Because it is repairable and because the meniscus does not grow back, finding it
matters. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Key Learning Points

- It is a tear at the back of the medial meniscus, at its capsular join.
- It is common alongside ACL injuries.
- It is difficult to see on a scan and needs a deliberate look at surgery.
- It can usually be repaired at the same time.
- Leaving it may contribute to a knee that stays loose.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q.** Why has nobody mentioned this before?
It has been recognised for decades and taken seriously more recently. Looking
for it specifically, by passing the camera behind the cruciate, is now a routine
part of many surgeons' practice and was not always.

**Q.** If it is hard to see on the scan, how is it found?
Often at the operation. A scan that does not show one does not exclude it, which
is why the look behind is done regardless of what the report said.

**Q.** Does repairing it make the knee more stable?
It is thought to contribute, because that part of the meniscus acts as a
secondary restraint to the shin sliding forward. How much it contributes is
still being studied.

**Q.** Does it change my recovery?
A meniscal repair usually adds restrictions in the early weeks, on how much
weight and how much bend. Your surgeon will say what applies to the repair you
had.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen
with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange
urgent assessment.

## For Medical Students

### Definition

A ramp lesion is a tear at the meniscocapsular junction of the posterior horn of
the medial meniscus. 2.5.1 covers the gross anatomy.

The posterior horn of the medial meniscus is a secondary restraint to anterior
tibial translation, which is why its loss matters mechanically in a cruciate
deficient knee. 3.7.3 covers the secondary restraints.

It is strongly associated with cruciate rupture and with the pivot mechanism.
6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

### Examination

There is no reliable clinical test for it, so it is not diagnosed at the
bedside. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

### Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging has limited sensitivity for it, and posteromedial
tibial bone marrow oedema is a useful indirect sign. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

A negative scan does not exclude it, which is the practical point. 6.4.1 covers
bone bruising.

Arthroscopic assessment requires a posteromedial view, obtained by passing the
arthroscope through the notch, and a standard anterior inspection misses it.
6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- It is a tear at the meniscocapsular junction of the posterior horn medially.
- That horn is a secondary restraint to anterior translation.
- It is strongly associated with cruciate rupture.
- There is no reliable clinical test.
- A negative scan does not exclude it, and a posteromedial view is needed.

## MRCS Level

### Investigations and Imaging

In a viva this is the lesion to raise unprompted when asked about an ACL
operation.

The expected points are the location, the mechanical role of the posterior horn,
the poor sensitivity of imaging, the need for a posteromedial view, and the
repairability. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Say that posteromedial tibial oedema on the scan should prompt the look even
where no tear is reported. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Say that the decision to inspect is taken before the operation rather than
during it, because it is a planned step. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

### Management Overview

Repair where found, at the same sitting as the reconstruction. 6.5.2 covers
meniscal damage.

A stable knee is a precondition for the repair to heal, which is the argument
for doing both together rather than either alone. 6.5.1 covers instability.

### Complications and Prognosis

An unrecognised ramp lesion is a recognised contributor to residual laxity after
reconstruction. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Repair carries the usual meniscal repair restrictions and the usual failure
rate, neither of which this page quantifies. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

### Key Learning Points

- Raise it unprompted when asked about an ACL operation.
- Posteromedial tibial oedema should prompt the look.
- The decision to inspect is taken before the operation.
- Repair at the same sitting, because the repair needs a stable knee.
- An unrecognised lesion contributes to residual laxity.

## FRCS Level

### Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to have a routine and to defend
it.

Inspecting the posteromedial compartment in every cruciate reconstruction is
defensible and adds time; inspecting selectively on imaging findings is also
defensible and misses the lesions imaging does not show. 5.6.10 covers ramp
lesions.

Repair technique and whether a stable lesion needs repair at all are the
technical questions that follow, and they follow the look rather than preceding
it. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Complications and Prognosis

Residual anterior laxity in a well placed graft should prompt a question about
the posteromedial corner of the meniscus before a question about the graft.
6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Meniscal repair in this location has a reputation for healing well, because the
tissue is peripheral and vascular. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

### Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether all ramp lesions need repair or whether
stable ones can be left. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

The second is how much residual laxity an unrepaired lesion actually
contributes. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The third is whether routine posteromedial inspection changes outcomes enough to
justify the added time across every case. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked
in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

### Key Learning Points

- Have a routine for the posteromedial look and defend it.
- Technical questions follow the look rather than precede it.
- Residual laxity in a well placed graft points here before the graft.
- The location heals well because the tissue is peripheral and vascular.
- Whether stable lesions need repair is unresolved.

## Fellowship Level

### Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the ramp lesion is the chapter's best illustration of how a
structure becomes visible: not through better imaging, but because people
started looking.

Its recognition rose when the posteromedial view became routine, which means the
historical literature describes a population in which it was present and
unrecorded. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

That has a consequence for how older outcome studies are read: knees reported as
isolated cruciate reconstructions included an unknown proportion with untreated
ramp lesions. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The practical discipline is to record that the posteromedial compartment was
inspected and what was found, because a note that omits it cannot distinguish
intact from unexamined. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

That is the same discipline this group keeps arriving at from different
directions. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

### Key Learning Points

- It became visible because people started looking, not because imaging improved.
- Historical series include it present and unrecorded.
- Older outcome studies describe knees with untreated ramp lesions in them.
- Record that the compartment was inspected and what was found.
- A note that omits it cannot distinguish intact from unexamined.

## Consultant Perspective

### Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question is whether to add a step to every case for a lesion
present in a minority of them.

Routine inspection costs operative time in every knee and finds the lesions that
imaging does not. Selective inspection saves that time and accepts a known miss
rate. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

A department can answer this for itself by recording what it finds when it
looks, which is more useful than the literature here and is rarely done. 6.1.3
covers the epidemiology.

For the individual patient the five things apply as everywhere else. Their age
and physiology, because a young meniscus is worth more years of preservation.
Their medical comorbidities, which bear on the tolerance of a repair protocol.
The demands they put on the knee. Their psychological state, because a repair
imposes restrictions that test compliance. And their own ideas, concerns and
expectations, including whether they were expecting one operation and one
recovery.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and say before the operation
that the look will be done and what it might lead to. 6.5.2 covers meniscal
damage.

### Key Learning Points

- The question is adding a step to every case for a minority lesion.
- Routine inspection finds what imaging does not and costs time in every knee.
- A department can answer this by recording what it finds when it looks.
- A repair imposes restrictions that test compliance.
- Say before the operation that the look will be done and where it may lead.
