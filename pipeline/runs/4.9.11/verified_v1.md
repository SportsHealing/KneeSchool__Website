# Meniscal Examination in OA

Where the tests stop working, why, and what the examination is actually for in
a degenerate knee.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

In a degenerate knee the meniscal tests are being asked a question they cannot
answer, and knowing that is more useful than performing them better.

Degenerate meniscal tears are common in knees with no symptoms attributable to
them, so finding one does not make it the cause.

The examination's job in this knee is therefore to establish whether there is
a mechanical problem on top of the degeneration, which is a narrower and more
answerable question.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history for a mechanical symptom specifically, and distinguish a true
block from a catch and from pain. 4.2.5 covers locking.

Examine for a mechanical block as the priority, because that is the finding
that would change management. 4.9.8 covers it.

Assess alignment and range, which describe the degenerative knee and are both
recordable and comparable. 4.5.1 and 4.7.1 cover them.

Palpate the joint lines, expecting tenderness and recording whether it is
localised or diffuse.

Perform the provocative tests if you wish, understanding that a pain-based
positive in this knee carries little information.

### What a Positive Means

A confirmed mechanical block in a degenerate knee is still a mechanical
problem and is still an indication, and this is the main exception the page
exists to state.

Localised tenderness at one point with a matching mechanical symptom is more
informative than diffuse tenderness with activity-related pain.

Diffuse tenderness, an effusion after use, stiffness after rest and a reduced
range describe degenerative disease rather than a symptomatic tear. 4.18.2
covers established disease.

A positive provocative test in this setting is weak, because forced rotation
provokes pain in a degenerate joint regardless of whether a tear is present.

### Key Learning Points

- Degenerate tears are common in knees with no symptoms attributable to them.
- The examination's job is to find a mechanical problem on top of the degeneration.
- A confirmed block in a degenerate knee is still an indication.
- Diffuse tenderness with activity-related pain describes degeneration rather than a tear.
- A pain-based positive carries little information in this knee.

## MRCS Level

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is about base rates and about what the trials actually
tested.

Degenerate meniscal change is common in asymptomatic middle aged knees, so the
scan finding has limited weight on its own. 4.1.6 covers the point.

Randomised trials comparing arthroscopic partial meniscectomy with
physiotherapy or with a sham procedure in degenerate knees with mechanical
symptoms found no advantage for surgery. 3.17.3 covers them.

Those trials recruited mechanical symptoms rather than mechanical blocks,
which is the most important thing to know before quoting them in either
direction.

So the honest position is that they settle the degenerate tear with pain and
catching, and do not settle the degenerate tear with a true block.

### How Good It Is

Every test in this chapter performs worse in a degenerate knee, and the
reasons are consistent.

Joint line tenderness is near universal, so its specificity collapses.

The provocative tests rely on pain, and a degenerate joint hurts on forced
rotation whatever its meniscus is doing.

The weight bearing tests are frequently impossible, because the patient cannot
squat.

The one finding that holds is a mechanical block, and that is the whole reason
the examination is still worth doing in this population. 4.9.8 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Degenerate change is common in asymptomatic knees, limiting the weight of a scan finding.
- The trials recruited mechanical symptoms, not mechanical blocks.
- They settle the degenerate tear with pain and catching; they do not settle a true block.
- Joint line tenderness is near universal here, so its specificity collapses.
- The one finding that holds is a mechanical block.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this page carries the chapter's most consequential
conclusion: in a degenerate knee, the examination exists to find the exception
rather than to confirm the rule.

The rule is that a degenerate tear with activity-related pain is managed
non-operatively, and the evidence for that is as good as anything in knee
surgery. 3.17.3 covers it.

The exception is a true mechanical block, which remains surgical.

The second exception is a symptomatic tear in a knee with otherwise preserved
cartilage and well localised findings, where the tear may genuinely be the
problem, and the examination is what identifies that patient.

Everything else in this knee is a load and function problem, and the
assessment is alignment, range, function and comorbidity. 4.18.6 covers
functional limitation.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error in the whole of knee practice is arthroscoping a
degenerate knee for pain on the strength of a tear on a scan, and 4.1.1 names
it as such.

The second is the mirror image: refusing an operation to a patient with a
genuine block because the knee is degenerate and the trials exist.

The third is quoting those trials as a verdict on arthroscopy generally, when
they are a verdict on one procedure in one population. 3.17.3 covers the
distinction.

And attributing all of a degenerate knee's symptoms to the one finding that
can be operated on, which is a bias of the available intervention rather than
of the evidence.

### Key Learning Points

- In a degenerate knee the examination exists to find the exception rather than confirm the rule.
- The rule is non-operative management; the exception is a true mechanical block.
- A second exception is a localised symptomatic tear in a knee with preserved cartilage.
- Refusing an operation for a genuine block because the knee is degenerate is the mirror error.
- Attributing everything to the one operable finding is a bias of the available intervention.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

In referred practice this presentation is the one where saying no is the
service being provided, and the examination is what makes saying no
defensible.

A patient arriving with a scan, a tear and an expectation of arthroscopy needs
an examination that establishes the absence of a block, the presence of
degenerative findings, and the localisation of their symptoms, and then needs
that explained.

The conversation is easier when the examination has been done properly,
because a recorded absence of a mechanical block is a reason rather than an
opinion. 4.1.8 covers the record.

The patient who does need an operation in this group is identified by the same
examination, which is why performing it carefully matters as much here as
anywhere.

The chapter's closing point is that eleven pages of meniscal tests lead to one
finding that acts alone, a combination that is better than any single test,
and a population in which the whole group performs poorly. That is an honest
summary of a widely taught examination and it is not how it is usually taught.

No figure here supports it, under decision 023.

### Key Learning Points

- In this group saying no is the service, and the examination makes it defensible.
- A recorded absence of a mechanical block is a reason rather than an opinion.
- The patient who does need an operation is identified by the same examination.
- Eleven pages of tests lead to one finding that acts alone and a combination better than any single test.
- In the degenerate knee the whole group performs poorly, which is not how it is usually taught.
