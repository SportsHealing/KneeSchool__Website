# MPFL Function

The checkrein that holds the patella medially in the first part of flexion,
before the groove takes over. It is a restraint rather than a strong one.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A checkrein is a strap that stops something going too far in one direction and
does nothing in the other.

A checkrein only works at the point where it becomes tight. Before that it is
slack and contributes nothing.

A structure that works over a small range is easy to overlook and easy to
blame for everything, and both mistakes are common here.

### At the Knee

The medial patellofemoral ligament runs from the medial side of the femur to
the medial edge of the patella. 2.12.2 covers the anatomy.

It resists the patella being pushed outwards, and it does so mainly near full
extension. 3.12.1 covers engagement.

Once the knee has bent far enough for the patella to sit in the groove, the
ligament has very little left to do. 3.12.4 covers the groove.

It is the structure that tears when the patella dislocates, which almost
always happens near extension. 6.59 covers the injury.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, because the ligament is only tight through part of the arc.

Whether it has been torn before, since a healed ligament is usually longer
than the original.

The shape of the groove, which decides how much the ligament is left to do.
3.12.4 covers dysplasia.

Muscle around it, particularly the lower part of the inner quadriceps, which
blends with it. 2.12.5 covers the dynamic stabilisers.

### Clinical Relevance

A first dislocation tears this ligament, which is why the knee often feels
unstable afterwards. 6.53 covers the first dislocation.

It is the structure that is reconstructed when the patella keeps dislocating.
7.51 covers the operation.

A reconstruction restores a checkrein. It does not deepen the groove or move
the pull. 3.12.12 covers tubercle position.

Section 4 covers how the ligament is tested.

### Key Learning Points

- A checkrein works only at the point where it becomes tight.
- The medial patellofemoral ligament runs from the medial femur to the medial patella.
- It resists lateral displacement mainly near full extension.
- It is the structure that tears in a patellar dislocation.
- A reconstruction restores a checkrein and changes nothing else.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

A passive restraint is defined by the arc over which it is tight and by the
force it can take before it fails. Both numbers matter.

The medial patellofemoral ligament scores modestly on the second and narrowly
on the first, which is why it is necessary rather than sufficient.

A structure like that fails under a load that a stronger structure would hold,
and its failure is therefore common rather than exceptional.

### At the Knee

The ligament arises from the femur between the adductor tubercle and the
medial epicondyle and inserts on the proximal half of the medial patellar
border. 2.12.2 covers the footprint.

Sectioning studies attribute roughly half to sixty per cent of the restraint
to lateral patellar translation near extension to this ligament.

Its contribution falls away as flexion increases and the trochlea engages, and
it is negligible beyond about 30 degrees.

It blends with the medial quadriceps tendon femoral ligament and the medial
retinaculum, so it is a complex rather than an isolated band. 2.12.3 covers
the MQTFL.

Its failure load in cadaver testing is low compared with the cruciate
ligaments, which matches how readily it tears clinically.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle, which determines whether the ligament is tight at all.

Previous rupture and healing, which typically leaves it lengthened rather than
absent. 6.59 covers the injury.

Trochlear morphology, which sets how much restraint is required of it. 3.12.4
covers it.

Patellar height, because an alta patella keeps the ligament as the only
restraint for longer. 3.12.12 covers it.

Vastus medialis activity, which adds a dynamic component to a passive
structure. 2.12.5 covers it.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric sectioning with measured displacement gives the proportional
contribution at each angle. 3.16.1 covers the method.

Robotic testing gives force to displacement curves before and after
sectioning. 3.16.2 covers it.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows continuity and the site of rupture rather
than function. 5.12.5 covers the appearances.

Clinical glide and apprehension testing estimate restraint and are influenced
by guarding. 4.15.4 covers the tests.

There is no clinical measurement of the ligament's strength, only of the
laxity that follows its failure.

### Clinical Relevance

Expect this ligament to be torn after a true dislocation, and look for it
rather than for a strain. 6.53 covers the presentation.

Assess lateral translation near extension, because that is the arc in which
the ligament works. 4.15.4 covers the test.

Describe the structure as a complex rather than a single band, since the
surgical anatomy follows that. 2.12.6 covers it.

Do not attribute instability in mid flexion to this ligament, because it is
not tight there. 3.12.4 covers what is.

### Key Learning Points

- A passive restraint is defined by its tight arc and its failure load.
- The ligament provides roughly half to sixty per cent of restraint near extension.
- Its contribution is negligible beyond about 30 degrees.
- It is a complex with the MQTFL and retinaculum rather than a single band.
- Instability in mid flexion is not this ligament's failure.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

The mechanically important point is that this ligament and the trochlea cover
different parts of the arc, and a knee needs both.

A reconstruction therefore restores a specific function over a specific arc.
Expecting it to compensate for a dysplastic groove is expecting it to work
where it was never tight. 3.12.4 covers the groove.

Graft tension and the femoral attachment point govern the result. Tensioning
in flexion overconstrains the medial facet and raises medial pressure. 3.12.3
covers pressure.

The femoral origin is close to the physis in a child, which is why the
operation differs in a growing knee. 7.94 covers paediatric stabilisation.

Conventional teaching has settled on anatomical femoral placement and low
graft tension, and the tension figure remains without a measured optimum.

### What Changes It

The arc over which the graft is tight, set by the femoral attachment point.

Graft tension at fixation, which decides whether the construct restrains or
compresses.

Trochlear morphology, which sets the demand placed on the construct. 3.12.4
covers it.

Patellar height and tubercle position, which decide how long the construct
works alone. 3.12.12 covers them.

Rotational alignment, which changes the direction the patella is pushed.
3.12.11 covers it.

### How It Is Measured

Cadaveric and robotic studies give the arc of restraint and the effect of
malpositioning the femoral attachment. 3.16.2 covers the method.

Finite element work predicts the medial facet pressure that follows
overtensioning. 3.16.4 covers modelling.

Clinically the result is measured as redislocation, apprehension and patient
reported outcome rather than as restraint. 4.15.3 covers the sign.

No intraoperative measurement of graft tension is in routine use, so tension
is set by feel.

The gap between a measured optimum in a cadaver and a tactile judgement in
theatre is the main source of variation in outcome.

### Clinical Relevance

Match the procedure to the arc that fails, and add a bony procedure where the
failure is outside this ligament's range. 3.12.8 covers the assessment.

Avoid overtensioning, because a tight medial construct raises medial facet
pressure and produces pain rather than stability.

Confirm the femoral attachment radiographically, since malposition changes the
arc of restraint. 7.51 covers the operation.

State that no measured optimum for graft tension exists, rather than quoting a
figure as though it did.

Weigh the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological
state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations alongside the morphology.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament and the trochlea cover different parts of the arc, and a knee needs both.
- A reconstruction restores restraint only where the ligament was tight.
- Tensioning in flexion overconstrains the medial facet and raises medial pressure.
- The femoral origin is close to the physis in a growing knee.
- No measured optimum for graft tension exists, and it is set by feel.
