# Varus OA

The bow legged worn knee, where the deformity and the wear reinforce each other
and the examination has to separate what is correctable from what is not.

## For Medical Students

### What It Tests

A varus knee with medial compartment wear presents as medial joint line pain, a
bow legged appearance that is worse standing, and often a visible lateral
thrust on walking. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

The examination is deciding how large the deformity is, whether it corrects,
and whether the lateral side has stretched. 4.3.10 covers the deformity.

It is the commonest pattern of knee wear, and the examination is mostly
observation and alignment assessment rather than special tests.

### How It Is Performed

Take the history: the medial pain, the duration, the walking distance and
whether the leg shape has changed. 4.2.9 covers the onset.

Look at the patient standing from the front, with the feet together, because
the deformity is a loaded finding. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Watch the gait from the front, specifically for the knee moving laterally
during stance, which is the lateral thrust. 4.4.6 covers the arthritic gait.

Palpate the medial joint line in flexion for tenderness and osteophyte. 4.6.4
covers joint line tenderness.

Assess the correctability by applying a valgus force with the knee near
extension and recording how far the alignment improves. 4.12.1 covers valgus
stress testing.

Assess the lateral collateral ligament, since a long standing varus knee
stretches the lateral structures. 4.13.2 covers the lateral collateral
assessment.

Measure the range, recording the fixed flexion separately, since varus knees
commonly lose extension. 4.5.4 covers the flexion deformity.

### What a Positive Means

Medial joint line tenderness with a standing varus deformity and a lateral
thrust is the characteristic picture, and the thrust is the finding that
matters most.

A deformity that corrects to neutral means the bone loss is limited and the
lateral structures are not contracted, which widens the options. 4.3.10 covers
the deformity.

A deformity that does not correct means contracture, bone loss, or both, and
that narrows them.

Lateral laxity on top of a varus deformity means the lateral side has
lengthened and the deformity is structural. 4.13.1 covers the varus stress
test.

A thrust with a correctable deformity marks a knee that is dynamically unstable
in the coronal plane, which accelerates the wear. 4.4.6 covers the arthritic
gait.

### Key Learning Points

- Medial wear and varus alignment reinforce each other.
- The deformity is assessed standing, because it is a loaded finding.
- A lateral thrust on walking is the finding that matters most.
- Correctability separates limited from structural deformity.
- Lateral laxity means the lateral side has lengthened.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the varus knee station and the expected answer is observation
standing, gait, correctability and lateral stability in that order.

The expected points are the standing alignment, the gait with the thrust named,
the medial joint line, the correctability, the lateral ligament, the range and
the hip.

Say that you would assess the alignment standing rather than supine, since the
load is what produces the deformity. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Say that you would look for a thrust during gait specifically, because it is
easy to miss and it changes the urgency. 4.4.6 covers it.

Say that you would examine the hip and the ankle, since a varus knee changes
the loading of both and either can be the symptomatic joint. 4.2.15 covers
referred pain.

### What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that medial wear lowers the medial side, the limb
angulates into varus, the medial load rises further, and the cycle continues.
3.5.2 covers contact pressures.

A lateral thrust means the lateral soft tissues are no longer restraining the
opening, so each step adds load to the medial side. 4.4.6 covers it.

Lateral lengthening makes the deformity structural, so correcting the bone
alone leaves a lax lateral side. 4.13.1 covers the varus stress test.

A fixed flexion deficit in a varus knee usually comes from posterior osteophyte
and capsular contracture together, so both have to be addressed. 4.5.4 covers
it.

### How Good It Is

The examination is reliable for the presence and the direction of the deformity
and for the thrust, since all three are gross findings.

Its best contribution is the thrust and the correctability, neither of which
appears on a static radiograph. 4.4.6 covers the arthritic gait.

It is imprecise about the magnitude of the deformity, which a long leg film
measures and the examination estimates.

Long leg alignment films quantify the mechanical axis, so the examination
supplies the dynamic and soft tissue findings and the film supplies the
geometry. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023,
and the comparison is the claim being made.

### Key Learning Points

- Medial wear and varus alignment form a cycle that accelerates itself.
- A thrust means the lateral restraint is no longer holding.
- Lateral lengthening makes the deformity structural rather than positional.
- The examination supplies the dynamic findings a static film cannot.
- The magnitude of the deformity is measured on a film, not clinically.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level a varus knee is sorted by the magnitude of the deformity, its
correctability, the state of the lateral compartment and the presence of a
thrust, and the examination contributes to three of those four.

A young varus knee with medial pain, a correctable deformity, a full range and
a normal lateral compartment is the load redistribution group, and the
examination identifies it. 4.20.4 covers the osteotomy assessment.

A varus knee with a fixed deformity, lateral laxity or a lost range is not in
that group, and again the examination is the discriminator. 4.3.10 covers the
deformity.

A thrust changes the timing of the conversation rather than its content, since
a dynamically unstable varus knee deteriorates faster. 4.4.6 covers it.

Lateral joint line tenderness in a varus knee is examined deliberately, because
lateral involvement removes the compartment specific options. 4.6.4 covers the
technique.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is assessing the deformity supine, where a varus knee can
look nearly normal. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Correctability assessed with a strong valgus force overstates what is
correctable, because a contracture stretches under enough load. 4.3.10 covers
it.

A thrust can be missed if the patient is watched from the side rather than the
front, and the finding is directional. 4.4.6 covers it.

Lateral laxity measured in extension in a knee with a fixed flexion deficit is
unreliable, since the position alters what the ligament resists. 4.13.1 covers
the varus stress test.

Varus can originate at the tibia, at the femur or at both, and the clinical
examination does not localise it. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

### Key Learning Points

- A varus knee can look nearly normal supine, so the assessment is standing.
- Correctability under a strong force overstates what will correct.
- A thrust is seen from the front and missed from the side.
- Lateral laxity in a fixed flexion knee is unreliable.
- The examination does not localise the deformity to the tibia or the femur.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the varus knee's examination has to separate four things
that often coexist, and each can mask another.

Bone loss, capsular contracture, lateral lengthening and a dynamic thrust all
contribute to the same appearance, and only the thrust and the correctability
are directly observable. 4.4.6 covers the arthritic gait.

A knee that corrects fully and still thrusts is telling two different stories,
and the thrust is the one that predicts progression.

Lateral laxity in a varus knee can be absolute or relative, since a shortened
medial side makes a normal lateral side appear long, and the examination cannot
separate those. 4.13.1 covers the varus stress test.

A coexisting cruciate deficiency changes the thrust's character and is easy to
attribute to the deformity alone. 4.10.5 covers the anterior drawer test.

Torsional and rotational components of the deformity sit outside the coronal
description entirely and are not captured by it. 4.19.7 covers torsional
assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- Bone loss, contracture, lateral lengthening and thrust coexist and mask each other.
- A knee that corrects fully and still thrusts is giving two different signals.
- Lateral laxity may be relative rather than absolute, and the examination cannot tell.
- A coexisting cruciate deficiency alters the thrust and is attributed to the deformity.
- Rotational components are outside the coronal description.
