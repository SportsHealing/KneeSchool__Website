# Roll and Glide Mechanisms

A femoral condyle that only rolled would run off the back of the tibia.
Rolling and gliding together keep the contact inside the plateau through the
arc.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

Two curved surfaces in contact can move against each other in three ways. One
can roll on the other, it can glide across it, or it can spin on the spot.

Rolling moves the contact point on both surfaces. Gliding moves the contact
point on one surface and not the other.

A wheel on a road rolls. A tyre on ice glides. The knee does both at once, and
the proportion changes through the range.

### At the Knee

The femoral condyle is far larger in circumference than the tibial surface it
sits on. Conventional teaching gives the femoral articular arc as roughly
twice the length of the tibial one.

Pure rolling through flexion would therefore carry the femur off the back of
the tibia before the knee reached full flexion.

Pure gliding would drive the same point of the femur against the same point of
the tibia throughout, concentrating load and wear in one spot.

Combining them keeps contact within the plateau and spreads it across the
surface, which is the mechanical reason the knee is built this way.

### What Changes It

Flexion angle. Conventional teaching gives more rolling in early flexion and
more gliding as flexion deepens.

Which side of the knee. The lateral condyle rolls further than the medial,
which is the source of the rotation coupled to flexion. 3.2.9 covers coupling.

The menisci, which move with the condyles and keep a congruent contact as the
condyle travels.

Whether the limb is bearing weight, because compression changes how readily a
surface glides.

### Clinical Relevance

The knee has no single hinge point, which is why a brace with one fixed axis
cannot match the joint through a full arc.

Load is spread over a large area of each surface rather than concentrated,
which is part of why cartilage survives decades of use.

A knee that loses its posterior meniscus loses part of the guidance for this
motion, and the contact pattern changes. 3.6 covers meniscal biomechanics.

Restoring a plausible roll and glide relationship is one of the design
problems in knee replacement.

### Key Learning Points

- Rolling moves the contact point on both surfaces; gliding moves it on one.
- The femoral arc is roughly twice the tibial, so pure rolling would run off the back.
- Pure gliding would concentrate load on one spot of each surface.
- More rolling early in flexion, more gliding later, by conventional teaching.
- The lateral condyle rolls further than the medial, which produces coupled rotation.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Describe the relationship as a ratio rather than as a sequence. At any flexion
angle the motion is some rolling and some gliding, and the ratio shifts rather
than switching.

The quantity usually reported is contact point translation, because it is what
rolling and gliding differ in and what imaging can follow.

Posterior femoral rollback is the summary term for the net posterior migration
of the contact point with flexion, and it is the quantity arthroplasty design
tries to reproduce.

### At the Knee

Conventional teaching describes rolling as dominant through roughly the first
20 to 30 degrees of flexion, with gliding taking an increasing share beyond
that.

The two compartments differ. The medial contact point moves relatively little
through mid flexion while the lateral translates considerably, which places
the axis of rotation medially.

In extension the motion reverses: the condyles glide posteriorly and roll
anteriorly as the knee straightens, which is the same relationship read in the
other direction.

The cruciates are part of the mechanism rather than bystanders. Their geometry
constrains how far the condyle can translate at each angle, which 3.7 and 3.8
cover.

### What Changes It

Cruciate integrity, since a deficient anterior cruciate changes the contact
path and shifts it posteriorly in the loaded knee.

Posterior tibial slope, which alters the resting position the rollback starts
from.

Meniscal integrity, particularly the posterior horns, which guide the condyle
at the flexion angles where gliding dominates.

Open against closed chain, which changes which surface is fixed and therefore
how the same relationship is described.

### How It Is Measured

Fluoroscopic contact point tracking through a loaded task is the usual in vivo
method, and it measures the nearest point rather than the true contact area.

Magnetic resonance imaging in incremental flexion gives position without load,
which is a different condition rather than a static version of the same one.

Cadaveric rigs with pressure sensitive film measure area and position
together, in a specimen with no muscle.

Instrumented implants measure in vivo loading directly in a replaced knee,
which is a small and selected population.

### Clinical Relevance

Expect a cruciate deficient knee to sit and to travel differently rather than
only to translate further on testing.

Preserve the posterior horns where possible, on the mechanical ground that
they guide the contact path.

Read a rollback figure with its loading condition attached, since unloaded and
loaded measurements differ.

Account for tibial slope when a knee's resting contact position looks
abnormal.

### Key Learning Points

- The relationship is a shifting ratio, not a switch from rolling to gliding.
- Rolling dominates the first 20 to 30 degrees by conventional teaching.
- The medial contact point barely moves while the lateral translates, placing the axis medially.
- Posterior femoral rollback is the quantity arthroplasty design tries to reproduce.
- The cruciates constrain how far the condyle can translate at each angle.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Treat rollback as a design requirement rather than a description. A cruciate
retaining replacement relies on the posterior cruciate to produce it, a
posterior stabilised design produces it with a cam and post, and each choice
trades a different thing.

Paradoxical anterior sliding is the recognised failure of that aim: the
contact point moves anteriorly with flexion instead of posteriorly, and the
knee loses the lever arm that deep flexion needs.

The same quantity explains why a ligament reconstruction can restore a
measured limit and leave the contact path shifted, with the cartilage
consequences that follow. 3.5 covers contact mechanics.

### What Changes It

Implant geometry, particularly the sagittal radius and the insert conformity,
which together decide how much the path is imposed.

Posterior cruciate tension in a retaining design, which is a surgical variable
rather than an implant one.

Soft tissue balance and component rotation, both of which change which
compartment translates further.

Tibial slope, native or created, which shifts the whole path and is modifiable
by osteotomy. 3.13 covers alignment.

### How It Is Measured

Fluoroscopic kinematic studies of replaced knees are where paradoxical motion
was described, and they measure a selected group performing a prescribed task.

Cadaveric simulators compare designs under a repeatable load profile, without
the variability that makes the clinical question interesting.

Instrumented implant telemetry gives load rather than position, so the two
literatures answer adjacent questions.

No routine clinical measurement of contact path exists, which is why design
arguments rest on laboratory work and outcome data rather than on per patient
measurement.

### Clinical Relevance

Tension the posterior cruciate deliberately in a retaining design, since
rollback depends on it.

Consider paradoxical motion when a well aligned replacement gives poor deep
flexion without pain.

Address slope where it is shifting the contact path rather than treating it as
an incidental measurement.

Read a design comparison by the task its kinematics were measured in.

### Key Learning Points

- Rollback is a design requirement; retaining and stabilised designs produce it differently.
- Paradoxical anterior sliding loses the lever arm deep flexion needs.
- A reconstruction can restore the limit and leave the contact path shifted.
- Sagittal radius and insert conformity decide how much the path is imposed.
- No routine clinical measurement of contact path exists.
