# LCL

The lateral collateral ligament, a cord that is easy to find and easy to make
look abnormal.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

The lateral collateral ligament is a discrete cord running from the lateral
femoral epicondyle to the fibular head. 2.10.1 covers the LCL anatomy.

It is superficial and separate from the capsule, so ultrasound shows it along its
whole length. 5.4.3 covers the lateral knee.

It matters because it runs obliquely, so following it requires continuous
reorientation and an oblique beam darkens it. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

### How It Is Acquired

Start at the fibular head and follow the cord proximally to the femoral
epicondyle. 5.4.3 covers the lateral knee.

Reorient the probe continuously, because the ligament's direction changes along
its course. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

Distinguish it from the biceps femoris tendon, which converges on the same
landmark. 2.10.5 covers the biceps femoris.

Apply gentle varus while scanning where laxity is the question. 5.4.15 covers
dynamic instability.

Assess the common peroneal nerve at the same sitting, since it is adjacent.
2.10.6 covers the common peroneal nerve.

### What Normal Looks Like

A discrete bright cord from the femoral epicondyle to the fibular head. 2.10.1
covers the LCL anatomy.

A consistent thickness along its course.

Separation from the biceps femoris tendon at the fibular head. 2.10.5 covers the
biceps femoris.

No gapping on gentle varus. 5.4.15 covers dynamic instability.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

Thickening with loss of the fibrillar pattern. 5.10.1 covers lateral collateral
injuries.

Discontinuity, which indicates a complete tear. 5.10.1 covers lateral collateral
injuries.

A bony fragment at the fibular head attachment. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Gapping on varus with the cord not restraining. 5.4.15 covers dynamic
instability.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament is a discrete cord, separate from the capsule.
- It is superficial and visible along its whole length.
- It runs obliquely, so following it needs continuous reorientation.
- An oblique beam darkens it and imitates injury.
- It converges with the biceps femoris at the fibular head.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the ligament to name when asked which structure anisotropy
affects most on the lateral side.

The expected points are the fibular head start, the reorientation, the biceps
distinction, the dynamic varus, and the nerve.

Say that an isolated injury to this ligament is uncommon. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

Say that anisotropy along the oblique course is the main pitfall. 5.2.10 covers
anisotropy.

Say that the corner as a whole needs magnetic resonance imaging. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that this ligament is the primary varus restraint in
extension, so an isolated injury produces varus laxity without rotational
laxity. 3.10.3 covers the lateral collateral function.

Varus laxity with rotational laxity indicates the corner rather than the
ligament, which is the distinction that changes the operation. 3.10.6 covers the
posterolateral corner function.

An isolated injury is uncommon, so finding one should prompt a search for the
rest of the corner. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

The oblique course means the probe angle changes along the ligament, and a
constant angle produces a falsely dark segment. 5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

### How Good It Is

Ultrasound images this ligament completely and does not establish whether the
corner is intact.

Its best contribution is confirming or excluding a discrete injury to this
specific cord. 5.10.1 covers lateral collateral injuries.

It is limited because the clinically important question is usually about the
corner, whose deeper components are not all reachable. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no thickness
or gapping values appear either: those are published quantities and this
environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The ligament is the primary varus restraint in extension.
- Isolated injury gives varus laxity without rotational laxity.
- Varus plus rotational laxity indicates the corner.
- An isolated injury is uncommon and should prompt a wider search.
- A constant probe angle produces a falsely dark segment.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level this ligament is rarely the whole question.

A discrete isolated injury with varus laxity and no rotational laxity is the case
where this assessment is sufficient. 4.13.2 covers the LCL assessment.

Varus laxity with external rotation laxity means the corner, which needs a
magnetic resonance study and changes the operation. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral
corner injuries.

A fibular head avulsion is a direct finding with a surgical answer and is visible
here. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

The adjacent nerve should be assessed whenever a lateral injury is found. 2.10.6
covers the common peroneal nerve.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading anisotropy along the oblique course as injury.
5.2.10 covers anisotropy.

The biceps femoris tendon is mistaken for the ligament. 2.10.5 covers the biceps
femoris.

An isolated injury is reported without the corner being considered. 5.10.3 covers
posterolateral corner injuries.

The nerve is not assessed. 2.10.6 covers the common peroneal nerve.

A normal study is taken to exclude rotational instability. 3.10.6 covers the
posterolateral corner function.

### Key Learning Points

- Anisotropy along the oblique course is read as injury.
- The biceps femoris tendon is mistaken for the ligament.
- An isolated injury is reported without considering the corner.
- The adjacent nerve goes unassessed.
- A normal study is taken to exclude rotational instability.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this ligament demonstrates that imaging a structure
completely is not the same as answering the clinical question.

Ultrasound sees this cord end to end, better than it sees almost anything else on
the lateral side, and the decision rests on the corner's rotational function
rather than on this cord's continuity. 3.10.6 covers the posterolateral corner
function.

That makes a complete and confident assessment of the wrong variable, which is a
subtler failure than a missed finding. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner
injuries.

Dynamic varus assessment is available and unquantified, so the functional
observation that would bridge the gap is an impression. 5.4.15 covers dynamic
instability.

Magnetic resonance imaging assesses the corner's components directly and still
performs inconsistently on the deeper ones, so neither modality closes the
question. 5.10.3 covers posterolateral corner injuries.

The useful conclusion is to use this study for a named structure and to put the
functional question to the examination. 4.13.2 covers the LCL assessment.

### Key Learning Points

- The cord is imaged end to end better than anything else laterally.
- The decision rests on the corner's rotational function.
- A confident assessment of the wrong variable is a subtle failure.
- Dynamic varus assessment is available and unquantified.
- Use it for a named structure and put the function to the examination.
