# Trochlear Function

The groove is the constraint. Its depth and the height of its lateral wall
decide how much work the soft tissue has to do, and dysplasia removes that
help.

## For Medical Students

### The Principle

A ball in a deep groove stays put without anything holding it. A ball on a
flat surface needs holding.

Shape is therefore a form of stability, and it costs the body nothing to use.

A joint that relies on shape is stable without effort. A joint that has lost
its shape has to rely on soft tissue, which can tear.

### At the Knee

The lower end of the femur carries a groove between its two condyles, called
the trochlea. 2.12.1 covers the anatomy.

The patella sits in that groove once the knee has bent far enough to engage
it. 3.12.1 covers engagement.

The outer wall of the groove is normally taller than the inner wall, which is
what resists the patella sliding outwards.

In some knees the groove is shallow or flat, which is called trochlear
dysplasia. 6.55 covers the condition.

### What Changes It

The shape the knee developed with, which is fixed by adulthood. 2.4.2 covers
ossification.

The flexion angle, because the groove deepens as it runs down the femur.

Previous injury to the cartilage or bone of the groove.

Nothing about exercise or training, which cannot change the shape of bone.

### Clinical Relevance

A shallow groove makes dislocation more likely and makes it more likely to
happen again. 6.54 covers recurrent instability.

It is the reason some people dislocate the patella with almost no injury at
all.

No amount of strengthening replaces a groove, although muscle can help the
soft tissue that is doing the work.

The shape is assessed on imaging rather than on examination. 5.12.1 covers it.

### Key Learning Points

- Shape is a form of stability that costs the body nothing.
- The trochlea is the groove in the lower femur that holds the patella.
- Its outer wall is normally taller, resisting outward sliding.
- A shallow or flat groove is called trochlear dysplasia.
- Strengthening cannot replace a groove, because bone shape does not change with exercise.

## MRCS Level

### The Principle

Bony constraint and soft tissue restraint substitute for each other, and the
exchange rate is poor. Soft tissue is weaker, and it is the thing that fails.

A joint's stability is therefore best described as how much of the job the
shape is doing at a given angle.

In the patellofemoral joint that proportion swings from nearly nothing in
extension to nearly everything in flexion. 3.12.1 covers the handover.

### At the Knee

Trochlear depth increases from proximal to distal, so constraint increases as
the patella descends into the groove with flexion.

The lateral facet of the trochlea is normally higher and more prominent than
the medial, which is the principal restraint to lateral displacement.

The sulcus angle describes the groove's opening. A larger angle means a
shallower groove.

Dysplasia takes several forms: a shallow groove, a flat groove, a convex
floor, and a supratrochlear spur. 5.12.1 covers the imaging classification.

A convex floor is mechanically the worst, because the surface actively pushes
the patella out rather than failing to hold it in.

### What Changes It

Developmental morphology, which is the dominant factor and is not modifiable.
2.4.2 covers ossification.

The flexion angle at which the patella engages, which decides how much of the
groove is available. 3.12.12 covers patellar height.

Cartilage thickness over the trochlear floor, which contributes to the
effective depth.

Previous osteochondral injury to the lateral facet.

Femoral rotation, which changes where the groove points relative to the line
of pull. 3.12.11 covers it.

### How It Is Measured

The sulcus angle is measured on an axial view at a stated flexion angle, and
it changes with that angle. 5.12.1 covers the technique.

Trochlear depth and lateral facet inclination are measured on axial magnetic
resonance imaging or computed tomography.

The crossing sign and the supratrochlear spur are assessed on a true lateral
radiograph, which is why the lateral view still matters.

Cadaveric and robotic testing quantify the force needed to displace the
patella at each angle, which is the mechanical measurement. 3.16.2 covers
robotic testing.

No single number captures dysplasia, which is why it is classified rather than
measured.

### Clinical Relevance

Grade the groove before attributing instability to soft tissue failure alone.
3.12.8 covers the mechanics.

Expect a soft tissue reconstruction to work less well where the groove offers
less. 7.51 covers the operation.

Order a true lateral radiograph as well as an axial study, because the
crossing sign is not visible axially. 5.12.1 covers the views.

Describe dysplasia by its type rather than by a single angle, because the
types behave differently.

### Key Learning Points

- Bony constraint and soft tissue restraint substitute for each other at a poor exchange rate.
- Trochlear depth increases distally, so constraint increases with flexion.
- The higher lateral facet is the principal restraint to lateral displacement.
- A convex floor pushes the patella out rather than failing to hold it in.
- No single number captures dysplasia, which is why it is classified.

## FRCS Level

### At the Knee

Trochlear morphology is the strongest single predictor of recurrent
instability, and it is the one factor no soft tissue procedure addresses. 6.54
covers recurrence.

The mechanical reason is that a reconstruction restores a checkrein at low
flexion angles and the groove is what holds the patella above them. 3.12.5
covers the ligament.

Displacement force studies show the greatest loss of constraint in the first
30 degrees in dysplastic knees, which is exactly the arc the soft tissue was
already covering alone.

The supratrochlear spur deserves separate mention. It converts engagement from
a descent into a groove into a climb over a ridge.

Conventional teaching has moved from treating dysplasia as a radiographic
curiosity to treating it as the primary lesion in a subgroup. 6.153 covers the
controversies.

### What Changes It

The type and severity of dysplasia, which decide whether the groove
contributes at all.

Patellar height, which interacts with dysplasia by delaying engagement into
the shallower proximal part. 3.12.12 covers it.

Tubercle position, which sets the lateral vector the groove has to resist.
3.12.12 covers it.

Femoral anteversion, which can rotate an adequate groove out of line. 3.12.11
covers it.

Previous surgery, which cannot add depth unless it has reshaped the bone. 7.56
covers distal realignment.

### How It Is Measured

Classification systems split dysplasia into types from radiographic and axial
features, with moderate interobserver agreement at best. 5.12.1 covers them.

Lateral trochlear inclination on axial imaging is the measurement with the
most reproducible relationship to instability.

Trochlear depth measured a set distance below the joint line depends on where
that distance is taken from.

Robotic displacement testing is the only method that measures constraint
rather than shape. 3.16.2 covers it.

The gap between measuring shape and measuring constraint is the reason
morphology predicts better in groups than in individuals.

### Clinical Relevance

Assess trochlear morphology in every patient with recurrent instability,
because it changes the expected result of a soft tissue procedure. 6.54 covers
the condition.

Identify a supratrochlear spur specifically, since it changes engagement
qualitatively rather than quantitatively.

Weigh morphology, patellar height, tubercle position and alignment together
rather than ranking them. 3.12.8 covers the four factors.

Expect shape to predict better across a cohort than for the patient in clinic,
and say so.

Take the patient's age, physiology, demands, psychological state and their own
ideas, concerns and expectations into the decision, because morphology
describes a knee and not a person.

### Key Learning Points

- Trochlear morphology is the strongest single predictor of recurrent instability.
- It is the one factor no soft tissue procedure addresses.
- Constraint is lost most in the first 30 degrees, which soft tissue already covered alone.
- A supratrochlear spur turns engagement into a climb over a ridge.
- Shape predicts better across a cohort than for the individual, because shape is not constraint.
