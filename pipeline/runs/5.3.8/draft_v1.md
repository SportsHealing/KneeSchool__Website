# Anatomical Axis

The line down the shaft of a bone, which is what a short film can give and is not
the mechanical axis.

## For Medical Students

### What It Shows

An anatomical axis is the line down the middle of a bone's shaft, drawn
separately for femur and tibia. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

It differs from the mechanical axis because the femoral shaft is angled relative
to the line from hip centre to knee. 3.13.2 covers the anatomical axis.

It matters because it is measurable on a film that does not include the hip, so
it is the axis available when a long film is not. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment
films.

### How It Is Acquired

Draw the mid shaft line of the femur and of the tibia, each from two points along
the canal. 3.13.2 covers the anatomical axis.

Measure the angle between them at the knee, which is the tibiofemoral angle.
5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Note that the femoral anatomical axis and the femoral mechanical axis differ by a
consistent angle in a normal femur, which is how one is converted to the other.
3.13.1 covers the mechanical axis.

Use the tibial axis for slope and for component alignment, since the tibial
anatomical and mechanical axes nearly coincide. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

Record which axis was used, because the two give different numbers for the same
knee. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

### What Normal Looks Like

A femoral shaft line and a tibial shaft line meeting at a slight angle at the
knee.

Shaft lines drawn through identifiable canal centres rather than estimated from
the cortex.

A tibial axis nearly parallel to the limb's mechanical axis. 3.13.2 covers the
anatomical axis.

Agreement with the mechanical axis measurement when both are available. 5.3.7
covers the mechanical axis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

An increased or reversed tibiofemoral angle. 3.13.3 covers varus alignment.

A shaft that is itself bowed, so no single straight line represents it. 5.3.10
covers the deformity analysis.

Previous surgery or a healed fracture distorting the shaft. 5.16.5 covers
osteotomy planning.

A disagreement between the anatomical and mechanical measurements, which
indicates femoral geometry outside the usual relationship. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

### Key Learning Points

- An anatomical axis is the mid shaft line of one bone.
- It differs from the mechanical axis because the femoral shaft is angled.
- It is measurable without the hip in the field.
- The tibial anatomical and mechanical axes nearly coincide.
- Record which axis was used, because the numbers differ.

## MRCS Level

### How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the measurement to distinguish from the mechanical axis, and
the distinction is the answer.

The expected points are the two shaft lines, the tibiofemoral angle, the
femoral offset between the axes, the tibial coincidence, and recording which was
used.

Say that the two axes give different numbers for the same knee. 5.3.7 covers the
mechanical axis.

Say that the offset relationship assumes normal femoral geometry. 3.13.2 covers
the anatomical axis.

Say that a bowed shaft has no single anatomical axis. 5.3.10 covers the deformity
analysis.

### What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the femoral head sits medial to the shaft, so a
line down the shaft is not the line from the head, and the difference is the
neck and offset geometry. 3.13.2 covers the anatomical axis.

That makes the conversion between the axes dependent on a patient having usual
proximal femoral geometry, which a previous fracture or a dysplastic hip breaks.
5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

A bowed femur or tibia has curvature rather than a single angle, so representing
it with one straight line hides where the deformity is. 5.3.10 covers the
deformity analysis.

Component alignment in arthroplasty is referenced to these axes, which is why the
distinction is a surgical one rather than a semantic one. 5.16.6 covers
arthroplasty planning.

### How Good It Is

The anatomical axis is available on more films than the mechanical axis and
answers a narrower question.

Its best contribution is being measurable on a knee film, which makes it the
practical axis when a long film does not exist. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior
view.

It is limited by depending on normal proximal femoral geometry for any conversion,
and by being meaningless in a bowed bone. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no axis
angles or offsets appear either: those are published quantities and this
environment cannot check them.

### Key Learning Points

- The femoral head sits medial to the shaft, which is why the axes differ.
- The conversion assumes usual proximal femoral geometry.
- A bowed bone has curvature rather than a single angle.
- Component alignment is referenced to these axes.
- Available on more films, and answers a narrower question.

## FRCS Level

### How Good It Is

At decision level the anatomical axis is the reference for implant position and
the mechanical axis is the reference for limb alignment.

Component position in arthroplasty is set relative to bone axes, so these are the
lines the implant is aligned to. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

An osteotomy's correction is specified against the mechanical axis and executed
relative to the bone, so both are needed. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

A healed fracture or previous osteotomy breaks the usual relationship between the
axes, which makes the long film mandatory rather than preferable. 5.3.6 covers
long leg alignment films.

Reporting a tibiofemoral angle as though it were a mechanical axis deviation is a
specific and common confusion. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

### Where It Misleads

The commonest error is quoting an anatomical measurement against a mechanical
threshold. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

A bowed shaft is represented by one straight line. 5.3.10 covers the deformity
analysis.

A patient with previous proximal femoral surgery is converted between axes using
the usual relationship. 3.13.2 covers the anatomical axis.

The axis is drawn from the cortical outline rather than the canal. 5.2.2 covers
the image formation.

Which axis was used is not stated, so the number cannot be interpreted. 5.20.8
covers common reporting errors.

### Key Learning Points

- An anatomical measurement is quoted against a mechanical threshold.
- A bowed shaft is represented by one line.
- The axis conversion is applied where femoral geometry is abnormal.
- The axis is drawn from the cortex rather than the canal.
- Which axis was used goes unstated.

## Fellowship Level

### Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the two axis systems are a long standing source of
incompatible numbers in the same patient's notes.

Several angle conventions exist on each axis system and their normal ranges
differ, so a value is uninterpretable without its convention. 5.21.5 covers the
osteoarthritis imaging literature.

Alignment philosophies in arthroplasty reference different axes, so the same
radiograph supports different implant positions depending on the philosophy
rather than on the measurement. 3.13.10 covers the alignment correction
principles.

Robotic and navigated systems compute their own axes from a registered bone
model, which means the intra-operative number and the radiographic number are
not the same quantity. 5.16.7 covers robotic surgery planning.

Three dimensional planning makes the distinction largely obsolete by measuring
the geometry directly, which is where this is heading. 5.16.8 covers three
dimensional reconstructions.

Until then the discipline is to name the axis and the convention with every
angle, which is this chapter's repeated conclusion in another form. 5.20.1
covers structured reporting.

### Key Learning Points

- Several conventions exist on each axis system with different normal ranges.
- Alignment philosophies reference different axes.
- A navigated system's axes are not the radiographic ones.
- Three dimensional planning measures the geometry directly.
- Name the axis and the convention with every angle.
