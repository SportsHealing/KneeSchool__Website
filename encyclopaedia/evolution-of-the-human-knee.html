Evolution of the Human Knee

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Function

Humans are unusual. Almost every other mammal walks on four legs. We walk on two, and that changed our legs completely.

A chimpanzee walking upright has to keep its knees bent, which is tiring. Our knees can lock straight, so standing costs us almost no muscle effort. That is why you can stand in a queue for ten minutes without your legs aching.

Clinical Relevance

Standing upright brought a trade. Our thigh bones angle inwards from the hip to the knee, which keeps our feet under our body when we walk. It also means the kneecap is pulled slightly outwards every time the thigh muscle contracts.

This angle is larger in people with wider hips. It is one reason kneecap problems and some knee injuries are more common in girls and women than in boys and men doing the same sport. There is more to it than the angle alone, and researchers are still working on the rest.

Key Learning Points

  • Walking on two legs changed the human knee from the four legged pattern.
  • Our knees lock straight, so standing costs very little muscle effort.
  • The thigh bone angles inwards from hip to knee, keeping our feet under us.
  • That angle pulls the kneecap outwards and contributes to kneecap problems.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Function

The human knee is adapted for upright walking on two legs. Compared with our closest relatives, our knees are larger relative to body size, they straighten fully and lock, and they sit under the body rather than out to the side.

Locking is the adaptation people notice least and use most. In full extension the joint reaches a position where the ligaments tighten and the surfaces fit closely, so standing requires very little muscular effort.

Clinical Relevance

Upright walking brought compromises that still shape the problems people have. The thigh bone runs inwards from hip to knee, which keeps the feet beneath the body during walking and gives the knee a slight natural angle. That angle creates a sideways pull on the kneecap whenever the thigh muscle contracts.

Our knees also carry our full body weight through two joints rather than four. The load per joint is higher than in a four legged animal of similar size.

None of this makes knee trouble inevitable. It explains why certain patterns recur: kneecap pain, wear in the inner compartment, and injuries from twisting on a planted foot.

Key Learning Points

  • The human knee is adapted for upright walking on two legs.
  • Full extension locks the joint, so standing costs little muscular effort.
  • The thigh bone angles inwards, which creates a sideways pull on the kneecap.
  • Two legs rather than four means a higher load through each knee.
  • Evolutionary compromises explain recurring patterns rather than making trouble inevitable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does this mean human knees are badly designed? No. They are a compromise that works well for a very long time in most people. Any structure doing several conflicting jobs has characteristic weak points, and knowing them is useful.

Q. Why are some knee injuries more common in women? A wider pelvis gives a larger angle between thigh and shin, and hormonal, muscular and movement pattern differences also contribute. The relative weight of each factor is still being studied.

Q. Do animals get knee problems? Yes. Dogs commonly rupture the equivalent of the cruciate ligament, for example. The patterns differ because the mechanics differ.

Q. Did our knees evolve for running? Humans are unusually good endurance runners, and several features of the leg support that. The knee reflects both walking and running demands.

This page is background rather than advice. Speak to a clinician about any knee that is painful, swollen or unreliable.

For Medical Students

Structure and Location

Adaptation to habitual bipedalism distinguishes the human knee from that of other hominoids. The femoral condyles are enlarged relative to body mass, the lateral condyle is elongated anteriorly, and the distal femur carries a pronounced bicondylar angle so that the femoral shaft is oblique while the knee remains close to the midline.

The patella is larger and the trochlear groove deeper, consistent with the extensor demands of upright posture and of running.

Function

Full extension with the screw home mechanism permits prolonged standing with minimal quadriceps activity, an arrangement absent in facultative bipeds, which must maintain flexed knees and therefore continuous muscular work.

Comparative anatomy supports this reading. The chimpanzee knee is adapted to a flexed, abducted limb posture and lacks both the bicondylar angle and the degree of terminal locking seen in humans.

Clinical Relevance

The bicondylar angle produces a lateral vector on the patella during quadriceps contraction, which underlies the predisposition to patellofemoral maltracking and is larger where the pelvis is wider.

Bipedalism also concentrates body weight through two joints rather than four and shifts the resultant force medially in stance, which is consistent with the predominance of medial compartment change in the ageing knee.

These are explanatory rather than deterministic observations. They account for the distribution of common problems without predicting them in an individual.

Key Learning Points

  • The human knee shows specific adaptations to habitual bipedalism.
  • The bicondylar angle keeps the knee near the midline while the pelvis stays wide.
  • Terminal extension and locking permit standing with minimal quadriceps activity.
  • The bicondylar angle creates a lateral vector on the patella during extension.
  • Medial load concentration in stance is consistent with medial compartment predominance.
