Common Knee Problems

For Young Learners

Clinical Relevance

Knee problems fall into a few broad groups, and knowing the groups helps more than memorising names.

Some happen suddenly. A twist on a planted foot, an awkward landing, a tackle. Something tears and the knee swells quickly.

Some build up slowly. Running further than usual, a new sport, a growth spurt. The knee aches after activity and settles with rest, then comes back.

Some belong to growing bodies. While you are still growing, the soft areas near the ends of your bones are the weak point, so young athletes get different injuries from adults doing the same sport.

And some come with age, as the smooth surfaces inside the joint gradually thin.

Key Learning Points

  • Knee problems fall into a few families rather than being endlessly varied.
  • Sudden injuries usually involve a twist, a landing or a blow, and swell quickly.
  • Overuse problems build gradually and often follow a change in training.
  • Growing bodies have their own injury patterns, different from adults.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Function

The pattern of your symptoms is informative. Swelling within hours of an injury suggests bleeding inside the joint. Swelling over a day or two suggests irritation. Locking suggests something physically caught. Giving way suggests a stability problem.

None of these is diagnostic on its own, and each narrows the field considerably.

Clinical Relevance

Most knee trouble belongs to one of five families. Knowing which one you are in tells you roughly what to expect.

Sudden injuries to the soft tissues. A twist, a landing or a blow tears a ligament, a meniscus or a tendon. These usually swell within hours and often involve a memorable moment.

Overuse and load problems. Pain builds gradually, often after a change in activity, footwear or surface. The tissue has not failed; it has been asked for more than it could tolerate.

Kneecap problems. Pain at the front, worse on stairs, sitting for long periods and squatting. Common in teenagers and in active adults, and usually manageable without surgery.

Wear of the joint surface. Stiffness after rest, aching with activity, gradual loss of movement. More common with age, and strongly influenced by strength, weight and previous injury.

Inflammatory and infective causes. Less common, and important not to miss. A hot, swollen knee with fever needs assessment the same day.

Key Learning Points

  • Most knee trouble belongs to one of five families.
  • Rapid swelling after injury suggests bleeding inside the joint.
  • Pain that builds gradually usually follows a change in load rather than a tear.
  • Front of knee pain on stairs and sitting is a common and treatable pattern.
  • A hot swollen knee with fever needs assessment the same day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How quickly should swelling appear after an injury? Swelling within a few hours suggests bleeding into the joint, which is more likely with a significant tear. Swelling that develops over a day or two is usually irritation.

Q. What does locking actually mean? A true lock is the knee becoming stuck and refusing to straighten, which suggests something physically caught. Many people use the word for a catching sensation, which is different.

Q. Is grinding at the front of the knee serious? Usually not by itself. Grinding without pain is extremely common. Grinding with pain, swelling or giving way is worth assessing.

Q. Do I need a scan? Often not. The history and the examination settle most questions, and a scan is arranged when the answer would change what is done.

Seek urgent assessment for a hot swollen knee with a fever, a knee that cannot take your weight after an injury, or new numbness or coldness in the leg or foot.

For Medical Students

Clinical Relevance

Knee presentations group usefully by mechanism rather than by structure. Acute traumatic soft tissue injury, overuse and load related disorders, patellofemoral pain and instability, degenerative joint disease, and inflammatory or infective arthropathy together account for the large majority.

Chronology discriminates well. A haemarthrosis developing within hours of injury implies bleeding and raises the probability of cruciate rupture, osteochondral fracture or peripheral meniscal tear. An effusion developing over twenty four to forty eight hours suggests synovial irritation. Insidious onset without trauma points towards degenerative, inflammatory or load related causes.

Mechanical symptoms are similarly discriminating. True locking, with a fixed block to extension, suggests an interposed fragment. Giving way suggests ligamentous insufficiency or quadriceps inhibition, and distinguishing the two matters.

Age shifts the differential substantially. Physeal injury and apophysitis belong to the skeletally immature. Ligamentous and meniscal injury predominate in young active adults. Degenerative change and crystal arthropathy predominate later.

Septic arthritis is the diagnosis that must not be missed at any age. A hot swollen knee with systemic upset requires same day assessment and aspiration before the picture is obscured.

Key Learning Points

  • Knee presentations group by mechanism more usefully than by structure.
  • Haemarthrosis within hours implies bleeding and a significant structural injury.
  • True locking implies a mechanical block. Giving way implies instability or inhibition.
  • Age materially shifts the differential diagnosis.
  • Septic arthritis must be excluded in any hot swollen knee with systemic upset.
