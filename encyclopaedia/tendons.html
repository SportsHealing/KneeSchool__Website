Tendons

For Young Learners

Structure and Location

Tendons join muscle to bone. Put your fingers just above your kneecap and straighten your leg, and you will feel a thick cord tighten. That is the quadriceps tendon.

Now move your fingers just below the kneecap. There is another cord there, running down to a bump on your shin. That is the patellar tendon.

The kneecap sits between the two, like a bead threaded onto a string.

Function

Together these two tendons plus the kneecap make the extensor mechanism, which is the system that straightens your knee.

When the big thigh muscle pulls, the force travels down the quadriceps tendon, around the kneecap, down the patellar tendon and into the shin bone. The shin swings forward and your knee straightens. Every kick, every jump, every stand from a chair uses this chain.

If any link in the chain breaks, you cannot straighten the knee against gravity, no matter how strong the muscle still is.

Key Learning Points

  • Tendons join muscle to bone. Ligaments join bone to bone.
  • The quadriceps tendon is above the kneecap, the patellar tendon below it.
  • Together with the kneecap they form the extensor mechanism.
  • If any link in that chain breaks, the knee cannot be straightened against gravity.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Structure and Location

The quadriceps tendon is formed where the four heads of the thigh muscle converge above the kneecap. It is broad and layered, with fibres from different parts of the muscle arriving at slightly different depths.

The patellar tendon runs from the lower pole of the kneecap to the tibial tubercle, the bony bump you can feel on the front of the shin. Anatomically it joins two bones, so it is sometimes called the patellar ligament. Both names are in use.

Function

These two tendons and the kneecap work as a single unit called the extensor mechanism. Force generated by the quadriceps muscle is transmitted through this chain to the tibia, straightening the knee.

The kneecap redirects that force. By holding the tendon away from the joint, it improves the efficiency of the muscle, most noticeably in the last part of straightening.

Clinical Relevance

Because the mechanism works as a chain, a failure anywhere in it produces the same functional result: an inability to straighten the knee or hold it straight against gravity. Where the chain fails tends to vary with age, which is useful information clinically.

Tendons also adapt to load more slowly than muscle does. Muscle strength improves within weeks; tendon adapts over months. That mismatch is why pain at the front of the knee so often follows a rapid increase in training, and why recovery is measured in months.

Specific tendon conditions and their treatment are covered in the conditions section.

Key Learning Points

  • The quadriceps tendon lies above the kneecap, the patellar tendon below.
  • With the kneecap they form one continuous extensor mechanism.
  • A failure anywhere in the chain prevents straightening against gravity.
  • The patellar tendon joins two bones, so it is also called the patellar ligament.
  • Tendon adapts to load more slowly than muscle, over months rather than weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Is it a tendon or a ligament below the kneecap? Both terms are correct and both are used. It joins bone to bone, like a ligament, but it is the continuation of a tendon.

Q. Why does the front of my knee hurt after starting running? Tendon adapts more slowly than muscle, so a rapid increase in load is a common trigger. Building up gradually is the usual preventive measure.

Q. What is the bump below my kneecap? The tibial tubercle, where the patellar tendon attaches. It is prominent in many people and becomes tender in some growing athletes.

Q. How long does tendon pain take to settle? Usually months rather than weeks, because the tissue remodels slowly. Loading it appropriately under guidance is generally more effective than resting completely.

Seek urgent assessment if you cannot straighten your knee or lift your straight leg off the bed after an injury, particularly if there is a gap you can feel above or below the kneecap.

For Medical Students

Structure and Location

The quadriceps tendon is formed by the convergence of the four heads of quadriceps femoris and inserts on the base of the patella. The patellar tendon runs from the apex of the patella to the tibial tubercle, typically four to six centimetres long.

The medial and lateral retinacula run alongside, formed from the fascial expansions of the vasti.

The layered structure of the quadriceps tendon and the vascular supply of this region belong to the Anatomy Academy.

Function

The extensor mechanism comprises the quadriceps muscle, the quadriceps tendon, the patella, the patellar tendon and the retinacula, functioning as one continuous unit. The patella increases the moment arm of the extensor force about the flexion axis, with the effect greatest in terminal extension.

Tensile loads through the mechanism during jumping and landing substantially exceed body weight, transmitted through a relatively small cross sectional area.

Clinical Relevance

Disruption anywhere in the chain produces extensor lag or an inability to perform a straight leg raise. The site of failure varies with age, which assists clinical reasoning.

Intact retinacula may permit some active extension despite a tendon rupture, so apparent preservation of extension does not exclude a significant injury.

Tendon adapts to mechanical load over a longer time course than muscle, which underlies load related anterior knee pain after rapid training progression. Specific tendinopathies and ruptures are covered in the conditions section.

Key Learning Points

  • The quadriceps tendon inserts on the patella; the patellar tendon runs to the tibial tubercle.
  • The retinacula can maintain limited active extension despite central tendon disruption.
  • The patella increases the extensor moment arm, most in terminal extension.
  • Failure anywhere in the chain produces extensor lag; the site varies with age.
  • Tendon adapts over months while muscle adapts over weeks, which explains overload pain.
