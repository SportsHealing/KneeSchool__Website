Skeletally Immature Patients

Page 6.7.1 · Paediatric ACL Injuries · condition page

The decision changes because the knee is still growing, and growth is the one variable that cannot be paused while everybody thinks about it.

Skeletally Immature Patients

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

A growing bone has a special layer near each end called a growth plate, and that is where the bone gets longer. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

The growth plate is softer than the rest of the bone, and damaging it can make a leg grow crookedly or stop growing as much as the other one. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Causes and Risk Factors

That matters here because the ACL runs right past both growth plates, so any operation on it has to get around them. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Waiting is not free either, because a knee that keeps giving way damages the cushions inside it. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • A growth plate is the layer where a bone gets longer.
  • It is softer than the rest of the bone.
  • Damaging it can make a leg grow crookedly or shorter.
  • The ACL runs past both growth plates.
  • Waiting is not free, because giving way damages the knee.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Skeletally immature means the growth plates are still open, so the bones have growing left to do. It is a measurement rather than an age. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

Two children of the same age can be at very different stages, which is why skeletal age rather than birthday drives the decision. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Who Gets It

These injuries are diagnosed more often than they used to be, and the reasons include year round youth sport, earlier specialisation and better recognition. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Better recognition matters as an explanation, because part of the apparent rise is a change in who gets scanned rather than a change in who gets injured. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Management Overview

The decision has two risks on opposite sides, and that is the whole difficulty. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Operating near an open growth plate carries a risk of disturbing growth. Waiting carries a risk of further episodes of giving way, each of which can damage a meniscus permanently. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Current practice generally favours stabilising the unstable knee with a technique chosen to respect the growth plates, rather than waiting for skeletal maturity. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Key Learning Points

  • Skeletally immature means the growth plates are still open.
  • It is a measurement rather than an age.
  • Part of the apparent rise is better recognition.
  • Operating risks growth and waiting risks the meniscus.
  • Current practice generally favours stabilising rather than waiting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Can we not just wait until she stops growing? That used to be the standard answer. The difficulty is that a knee that gives way in the meantime can lose meniscal tissue permanently, and that loss matters more over a long life than a small growth risk does.

Q. How much growth is at risk? The reported problems are limb length difference and angular deformity, and both are uncommon with techniques chosen for an immature knee. This page carries no rate, because none has been verified here.

Q. How do you know how much growing is left? From skeletal age rather than from a birthday: hand or elbow films, the stage of puberty, the growth plates on the knee films themselves, and the family's own growth pattern.

Q. Will a brace do instead? A brace does not restore the ligament's restraint. It is sometimes used while a decision is being made or where surgery is declined, and it is not an equivalent.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

Skeletal immaturity means open physes, and the relevant physes here are the distal femoral and proximal tibial ones, both of which the ACL passes between. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

The distal femoral physis contributes most of the growth remaining in the limb, which is why disturbance there matters more than at the tibia. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Who Gets It

The group is adolescent pivoting athletes, with a rising recorded incidence and a female predominance within it that mirrors the adult pattern. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

This page carries no incidence figure, because none has been verified in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Examination

The examination is the adult examination, with two differences. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

The first is that children guard more and tolerate the pivot shift less, so a negative examination is less informative than in an adult. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

The second is that physiological laxity is greater, so a comparison with the other knee carries more weight than an absolute impression. 6.3.4 covers partial tears.

Investigations and Imaging

Plain films are obtained in a child for a reason they are not in an adult: a tibial spine avulsion is the paediatric equivalent pattern and is visible on a radiograph. 6.3.3 covers tibial avulsion.

MRI confirms the ligament and shows the menisci, and it also shows the physes themselves. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Skeletal age assessment is part of the workup rather than an addition to it. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Key Learning Points

  • The ACL passes between the distal femoral and proximal tibial physes.
  • The distal femoral physis carries most of the remaining growth.
  • Children guard more, so a negative examination is less informative.
  • Greater physiological laxity makes the comparison with the other side matter.
  • A radiograph is obtained to look for a tibial spine avulsion.

MRCS Level

Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer adds skeletal age to the adult workup and says why.

Say radiographs for the avulsion pattern, MRI for the ligament and menisci, and a skeletal age assessment because it determines the surgical options. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Say that the physes are visible on the MRI already obtained, so the imaging question is about interpretation rather than about an extra scan. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Management Overview

State the two sided risk first: growth disturbance against meniscal loss. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

State that the modern position favours stabilisation with a physeal respecting technique in the genuinely unstable knee, and that the technique is chosen by skeletal age. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

State that non-operative management with activity modification remains an option in a stable knee and a compliant family, and that compliance in an adolescent athlete is the weak point. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Complications and Prognosis

The complications divide into the ones shared with adults and the ones specific to growth. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Growth specific complications are limb length discrepancy and angular deformity, and both are detected by surveillance rather than by symptoms. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Re-rupture rates in this group are reported as higher than in adults, which belongs in the first consultation rather than the last. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Add skeletal age to the adult workup and say why.
  • The physes are already visible on the MRI obtained.
  • State the two sided risk before stating a preference.
  • Compliance in an adolescent athlete is the weak point of waiting.
  • Growth complications are found by surveillance rather than by symptoms.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is given a twelve year old with an unstable knee and asked to decide.

The answer that holds up establishes skeletal age before choosing anything, then picks a technique matched to it, then states the surveillance plan. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Omitting the surveillance plan is the common failure, because a growth disturbance that nobody looks for is found late and corrected harder. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The meniscal state changes the urgency more than the ligament does, exactly as in the adult chapter. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Complications and Prognosis

Surveillance means clinical limb length and alignment assessment at intervals through remaining growth, with imaging where a difference is suspected. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The counselling point that gets missed is the second injury risk, which in young pivoting athletes is the highest in this chapter and is what the family will judge the outcome by. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is the true rate of growth disturbance with current techniques, which is reported from series too small to settle it. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

The second is whether delayed reconstruction in a selected stable knee still has a place. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The third is whether the higher re-rupture rate in this group reflects biology, graft choice or return to sport behaviour. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Establish skeletal age before choosing anything.
  • Match the technique to skeletal age, then state the surveillance plan.
  • Omitting surveillance is the common failure.
  • Meniscal state changes urgency more than the ligament does.
  • Second injury risk is what the family will judge the outcome by.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the referral is the adolescent who has already been reconstructed elsewhere, and the two facts needed are the two least often recorded: skeletal age at surgery and the technique actually used.

Transphyseal, partial transphyseal and physeal sparing are different operations with different later consequences, and an operation note saying ACL reconstruction distinguishes none of them. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

The habit worth keeping is to record skeletal age with its method, the physeal relationship of each tunnel, the graft, and the fixation position relative to the physis. Four items. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

The second habit is a baseline limb length and alignment measurement at the time of surgery, because a discrepancy found later cannot be interpreted without it. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Neither is in a standard template, which is why the next surgeon reconstructs both from inference. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Key Learning Points

  • Skeletal age at surgery and the technique used are the two facts needed.
  • ACL reconstruction as a note distinguishes three different operations.
  • Record skeletal age with its method and each tunnel's physeal relationship.
  • Record a baseline limb length and alignment measurement.
  • A later discrepancy cannot be interpreted without that baseline.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that the person consenting is not the person being operated on, and the two have different time horizons. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

A parent weighs a growth risk now against an arthritis risk decades away, and the adolescent weighs a season. Both are legitimate and they do not point the same way. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which here means skeletal age and remaining growth and is the variable the whole decision turns on. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which in an adolescent are set by a squad, a school and a parent's ambition as much as by the child. Their psychological state, including whether the child has been asked anything at all. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which arrive twice over, once from the parent and once from the child, and frequently differ.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and ask the child separately what they want. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • The person consenting is not the person being operated on.
  • A parent weighs decades and an adolescent weighs a season.
  • Skeletal age and remaining growth is the variable the decision turns on.
  • Demand is set by a squad, a school and a parent's ambition.
  • Ask the child separately what they want.
