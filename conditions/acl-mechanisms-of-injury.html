Mechanisms of Injury

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Causes and Risk Factors

Most ACL tears happen with nobody touching the player. A foot is planted, the body turns, and the knee does not follow. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

Landing from a jump slightly off balance does the same thing. So does stopping suddenly with the knee nearly straight. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

A smaller number happen from a blow to the outside of the knee, which pushes the joint inwards. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

How It Presents

A pop is often heard or felt, and the knee swells within hours rather than days. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Players usually cannot carry on. Someone who returns to the pitch and finishes the game has not ruled anything out, but it is less typical.

Key Learning Points

  • Most tears happen with no contact at all.
  • A planted foot and a turning body is the classic pattern.
  • Landing off balance and stopping suddenly do the same thing.
  • A blow to the outside of the knee is the contact version.
  • A pop and swelling within hours are the usual signs.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Causes and Risk Factors

The commonest mechanism is a sudden change of direction or a landing, with the foot fixed and the knee close to straight. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

A direct blow to the outside of the knee produces a different pattern, and tends to injure more than one structure. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

Forcing the knee backwards past straight is a third pattern, less common and often more serious. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

How It Presents

Swelling within a few hours means bleeding inside the joint, which points at a significant injury rather than a sprain. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

What you were doing at the moment it happened tells the clinician which structures to check, which is why the question is asked in detail. 4.10.1 covers the history of ACL injury.

An injury with contact is more likely to have damaged something alongside the ACL. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • Changing direction or landing with the foot fixed is commonest.
  • A blow to the outside of the knee is a different pattern.
  • Forcing the knee backwards is a third and often more serious one.
  • Swelling within hours means bleeding inside the joint.
  • The mechanism tells the clinician what else to check.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Why does the clinician ask so much about how it happened? Because the mechanism predicts which structures were loaded. A twisting non-contact injury and a blow from the side damage different things, so the history narrows the examination before anyone touches the knee.

Q. I did not hear a pop. Does that mean it is not torn? No. A pop is common but not universal, and noise in a match makes it easy to miss. Swelling within a few hours is the more reliable sign.

Q. It swelled up the next morning rather than straight away. Is that different? Usually, yes. Bleeding into the joint swells it within hours. Slower swelling over a day is more often fluid than blood, which points at a different kind of injury.

Q. Can this happen without sport? Yes. A fall, a trip on a kerb, a skiing twist or a road traffic injury all produce it. The mechanism matters more than the setting.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Causes and Risk Factors

The classical non-contact mechanism is deceleration or landing with the knee near extension, a valgus moment and internal rotation of the tibia. 3.7.14 covers the ACL injury mechanisms.

Quadriceps contraction with the knee near extension pulls the tibia forward and adds to the load, which is why the position matters as much as the force. 3.7.12 covers the ACL loading during landing.

A valgus contact mechanism loads the medial structures first and reaches the ACL second, which is why that pattern produces combined injury. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

Hyperextension loads the ligament differently again and raises the question of the posterior structures. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

How It Presents

Immediate haemarthrosis is the finding that should prompt a search for a structural injury rather than reassurance. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Inability to continue playing is usual, and the exceptions are common enough that continuing excludes nothing. 4.10.1 covers the history of ACL injury.

Examination

Examine for what the mechanism predicts, then for what it does not, because the second is where the missed injuries are. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

A valgus mechanism means the medial structures are examined before the conclusion is reached. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

A rotational mechanism means the posterolateral structures and the menisci are examined on the same grounds. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • Deceleration near extension with valgus and internal rotation is classical.
  • Quadriceps contraction near extension adds to the load.
  • A valgus contact mechanism reaches the ACL second.
  • Immediate haemarthrosis prompts a search rather than reassurance.
  • Examine what the mechanism predicts and then what it does not.

MRCS Level

Causes and Risk Factors

In a viva the mechanism is the opening of the answer, because it organises everything that follows.

The expected points are the non-contact deceleration pattern, the valgus contact pattern, hyperextension, the role of quadriceps contraction, and the predicted associated injuries. 3.7.13 covers the ACL loading during cutting.

Say that the mechanism predicts the company, so the answer moves from the ligament to the structures the force passed through. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Say that a contact mechanism raises the prior probability of multi ligament injury and changes the urgency. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

Examination

The examination is directed by the mechanism and then deliberately widened past it. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

A guarded acute knee limits the rotational tests most, so the examination is repeated rather than concluded. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Investigations and Imaging

The bone bruise pattern records the mechanism, which makes it corroboration of the history rather than an incidental finding. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

A lateral femoral condyle and posterolateral tibial bruise pair is the classical pivot pattern, and its absence in a convincing history is worth a second look. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL injury.

A different bruise pattern should prompt a different injury search. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

Key Learning Points

  • The mechanism opens the answer and organises what follows.
  • The mechanism predicts the company.
  • A contact mechanism raises the chance of multi ligament injury.
  • The bruise pattern corroborates the history.
  • A different bruise pattern means a different search.

FRCS Level

Causes and Risk Factors

At examination level the mechanism is used to justify a plan rather than to describe an event.

A low energy non-contact tear in an isolated pattern supports a staged approach. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

A high energy or contact mechanism changes the differential towards multi ligament injury, and that changes the urgency, the imaging and the operation. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

A hyperextension mechanism raises the posterior structures and, where the energy is high, the neurovascular question, which is a different order of urgency altogether. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is how reliably the reported mechanism matches what actually happened, since most accounts are reconstructed after the fact. 4.10.1 covers the history of ACL injury.

The second is whether video analysis of injuries in professional sport generalises to the recreational knee, which is where most injuries occur. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

The third is which component of the mechanism prevention programmes should target, because the programmes train several at once and their active ingredient is not isolated. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 3.7.14 covers the ACL injury mechanisms.

Key Learning Points

  • The mechanism justifies a plan rather than describing an event.
  • Contact or high energy changes urgency, imaging and operation.
  • Hyperextension raises the posterior and neurovascular questions.
  • Reported mechanisms are reconstructed after the fact.
  • Prevention programmes train several components and isolate none.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the mechanism matters because the injuries it predicts are the ones that change the operation, and they are the ones most often found late.

A ramp lesion sits at the posteromedial junction and is both predicted by the rotational mechanism and missed by a study read for the cruciate. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

A lateral meniscal root tear accompanies the pivot mechanism and alters load distribution across the compartment if it is left. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

An anterolateral injury accompanies the same mechanism and is the structure the rotational control argument turns on. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

The practical consequence is that the history should direct a second look at the imaging, and a fellow who reads the scan before the history has lost the advantage the history gives. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Key Learning Points

  • The predicted injuries are the ones that change the operation.
  • A ramp lesion is predicted by the mechanism and missed by a cruciate read.
  • A lateral root tear alters load distribution if left.
  • An anterolateral injury accompanies the same mechanism.
  • Reading the scan before the history loses what the history gives.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant use of mechanism is in prevention and in counselling, and the two pull in different directions.

Prevention needs the mechanism to be modifiable, and the training programmes built on it do reduce injury, while which element does the work is unresolved. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Counselling needs the mechanism to be explicable without becoming blame, because a patient told their landing technique caused the injury hears something the evidence does not support about an individual event. 6.6.3 covers the neuromuscular factors.

Holding both at once is the skill: the mechanism is a population level lever and a poor individual level explanation. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

What is said to a patient turns on the same five things. Their age and physiology, which bear on tissue quality and on what training can change. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which decide whether prevention is relevant to them at all. Their psychological state, because a patient who believes they caused the injury behaves differently afterwards. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, including what they already believe about why it happened.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Prevention needs the mechanism to be modifiable.
  • Which element of a programme does the work is unresolved.
  • Counselling must explain the mechanism without implying blame.
  • It is a population level lever and a poor individual explanation.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations shape what is said.
