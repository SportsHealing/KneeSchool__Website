Combined Injuries

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Causes and Risk Factors

An ACL tear often comes with other damage inside the knee. The same force that tore the ligament passed through other things first. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

The cushions inside the knee, the ligaments at the sides, and the surface of the bone can all be injured at the same moment. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

How It Presents

The knee swells and hurts as with any ACL tear. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

What tells a clinician that more than one thing is injured is usually the examination and the scan rather than how it felt at the time. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • An ACL tear often comes with other damage.
  • The force passed through other structures first.
  • Cushions, side ligaments and bone surfaces can all be injured together.
  • How it felt at the time does not say how much was damaged.
  • The examination and the scan are what answer that.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Causes and Risk Factors

Isolated ACL tears happen, and they are not the usual finding. Most knees have something else injured alongside. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

The meniscus, the cartilage surface, the ligaments at the sides and the structures towards the back of the knee are the usual company. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

What else is injured matters more for the long term than the ligament itself, because some of it does not repair. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

How It Presents

The knee swells within hours, as it would with an isolated tear. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Locking, or a knee that will not fully straighten, suggests a displaced piece of meniscus rather than the ligament. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Feeling loose in more than one direction suggests more than one ligament, and that changes the urgency. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

Management Overview

A combined injury is treated as a pattern rather than as a list of separate problems, and the order of treatment is part of the plan. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

A meniscus that can be repaired is a reason to act sooner, because the chance of repairing it does not improve with waiting. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Most ACL tears come with something else injured.
  • The meniscus, the cartilage and the side ligaments are the usual company.
  • What else is injured matters more for the long term.
  • Locking suggests a displaced meniscus rather than the ligament.
  • A repairable meniscus is a reason to act sooner.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Why does the scan list so many things? Because the force went through more than one structure. Not all of them need treating. The report describes what is injured; the plan decides what matters.

Q. Which of them matters most? Usually the meniscus, because it does not grow back and losing it changes how the joint is loaded for the rest of your life. The ligament can be replaced; the meniscus cannot.

Q. Does this mean a bigger operation? Sometimes. A meniscal repair done at the same time adds to the operation and to the rehabilitation, and it is usually worth doing when the meniscus is repairable.

Q. Why does bone bruising show on the scan if nothing is broken? Bruising inside the bone is the imprint of the impact. It records the mechanism and usually settles on its own, but it tells the clinician what happened.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Causes and Risk Factors

An isolated anterior cruciate rupture is the exception rather than the rule, and treating it as the default underestimates most knees. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

The mechanism predicts the company: a pivot mechanism raises the meniscus and the anterolateral structures, a valgus contact mechanism raises the medial side, and hyperextension raises the posterior structures. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

Bone bruising is present in most acute tears and is corroboration of the mechanism rather than a separate injury. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

How It Presents

A locked knee or a fixed loss of extension is a mechanical finding and points at a displaced meniscal fragment. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Multi directional laxity on examination moves the diagnosis to a multi ligament injury, which carries different urgency. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

Examination

Examine every structure rather than those the mechanism predicts, because the unpredicted ones are the missed ones. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

A guarded acute knee hides meniscal signs as readily as rotational ones, which is why the examination is repeated. 4.10.9 covers the rotational instability.

Investigations and Imaging

The study is read as a survey rather than as a cruciate question, and the posteromedial junction is looked at deliberately. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Meniscal root integrity is assessed separately from the body of the meniscus, because the two have different consequences. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Key Learning Points

  • The isolated rupture is the exception rather than the rule.
  • The mechanism predicts which structures to expect.
  • Bone bruising corroborates the mechanism.
  • A locked knee points at a displaced meniscal fragment.
  • The study is read as a survey rather than a cruciate question.

MRCS Level

Causes and Risk Factors

In a viva the expected answer treats the cruciate as the index injury and not as the whole one.

The expected points are the rarity of the isolated tear, the mechanism's predictions, the meniscus as the structure that matters most, the multi ligament possibility, and the effect on timing. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Say that a repairable meniscus is the commonest reason to bring surgery forward. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Say that an unrecognised ramp lesion or root tear is a recognised cause of a reconstruction that does not perform as expected. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Examination

Examination findings decide urgency more than imaging findings do, because a locked knee and a multi directionally lax knee are both clinical diagnoses. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Management Overview

Treat the pattern: the order of reconstruction, what is repaired at the same sitting, and what is left are decided together rather than structure by structure. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

A meniscal repair performed with the reconstruction uses one anaesthetic and one rehabilitation, which is an argument on its own. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Key Learning Points

  • The cruciate is the index injury and not the whole one.
  • A repairable meniscus is the commonest reason to operate sooner.
  • An unrecognised ramp or root tear underperforms a reconstruction.
  • Examination findings decide urgency more than imaging does.
  • Treat the pattern rather than the structures one at a time.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to sequence a combined injury and justify the sequence.

A repairable meniscal tear with a cruciate rupture argues for a single operation, because the repair's success depends on a stable knee and the stability depends on the reconstruction. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

A medial collateral injury argues for delay, letting the medial side heal before a cruciate reconstruction, and the delay is bounded by meniscal repairability. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

A posterolateral injury argues against delay, because those structures heal less predictably and a late reconstruction is a harder operation. 6.2.3 covers hyperextension injury.

Complications and Prognosis

Stiffness rises with the number of structures addressed and with early surgery on an inflamed knee, which is the cost on one side of the timing argument. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Meniscal loss rises with delay in an unstable knee, which is the cost on the other, and it is irreversible where stiffness is not. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether a ramp lesion found at reconstruction should be repaired or left. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

The second is when an anterolateral procedure should be added, and to whom. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

The third is the optimal interval between a collateral injury and cruciate reconstruction. 6.4.5 covers MCL injuries.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • A repairable meniscus argues for a single operation.
  • A medial collateral injury argues for delay, bounded by repairability.
  • A posterolateral injury argues against delay.
  • Stiffness rises with early surgery and with the number of structures.
  • Meniscal loss rises with delay and is irreversible where stiffness is not.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the combined injury is the chapter's real subject, and the isolated tear is the simplification that teaching uses.

The structures most often missed are the ones at the margins of the study and the examination: the posteromedial junction, the meniscal roots and the anterolateral complex. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

They are missed in the same way each time, by a reader and an examiner who have already found the cruciate and stopped. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

A reconstruction that underperforms in a technically sound knee should prompt a search for what was missed at the index operation before it prompts a question about the graft. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The habit worth building is to state what was assessed and found intact, because a report that lists only abnormalities cannot distinguish normal from unexamined. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The combined injury is the real subject and the isolated tear the teaching case.
  • The missed structures sit at the margins of study and examination.
  • They are missed by stopping once the cruciate is found.
  • An underperforming reconstruction prompts a search for what was missed.
  • State what was found intact, or normal cannot be told from unexamined.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that each additional structure addressed adds benefit and cost, and the balance is struck per patient rather than per protocol.

Repairing everything repairable maximises preserved tissue and raises stiffness, operative time and the rehabilitation burden. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Addressing only the cruciate shortens everything and accepts a meniscus that may not be repairable later. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The five things decide where the line falls. The patient's age and physiology, because a young meniscus is worth more years and heals better. Their medical comorbidities, which change both operative risk and the tolerance of a long rehabilitation. The demands they put on the knee, which decide how much preserved tissue is worth to them. Their psychological state, because a longer rehabilitation asks more of it. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which decide whether a bigger operation now for a benefit decades away is one they want.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Each structure addressed adds benefit and cost.
  • Repairing everything maximises tissue and raises stiffness and burden.
  • Addressing only the cruciate accepts a meniscus that may not keep.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide where the line falls.

  • A benefit decades away is a choice the patient makes, not the surgeon.
