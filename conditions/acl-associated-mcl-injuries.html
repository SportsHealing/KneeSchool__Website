MCL Injuries

For Young Learners

Definition

The medial collateral ligament runs down the inner side of the knee. It stops the knee bending inwards. 1.2.4 covers the ligaments.

When a blow comes from the outside, this ligament takes the force before the ACL does, so the two are often injured together. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

How It Presents

The inner side of the knee is tender, and the knee hurts when it is pushed inwards. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Unlike the ACL, this ligament usually heals by itself. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The medial collateral ligament runs down the inner side.
  • It stops the knee bending inwards.
  • A blow from outside reaches it before the ACL.
  • The two are often injured together.
  • Unlike the ACL, it usually heals by itself.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

The medial collateral ligament resists a force pushing the knee inwards, and it is the commonest ligament injured alongside the ACL. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

It differs from the ACL in one important way: it has a good blood supply and usually heals without an operation. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

How It Presents

Tenderness along the inner side of the knee, and pain or looseness when the knee is pushed inwards. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Those are separate findings from the swelling and instability of the cruciate injury, and both are present together. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Management Overview

The usual approach is to let the medial ligament heal first, often in a brace, and then reconstruct the cruciate if that is needed. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

That is a decision about order rather than about technique, and it is why a combined injury is often not operated on immediately. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Occasionally the medial injury is severe enough to need its own operation, and that changes the plan. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • It resists force pushing the knee inwards.
  • It is the commonest ligament injured alongside the ACL.
  • It has a good blood supply and usually heals.
  • The usual approach is to let it heal, then reconstruct the cruciate.
  • That is a decision about order rather than technique.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Why does one ligament heal and the other not? Blood supply and position. The medial ligament lies outside the joint with good blood supply and its torn ends stay close together. The cruciate sits inside the joint, is poorly supplied in its middle, and its ends pull apart.

Q. Why wait before the cruciate operation? Partly to let the medial side heal, and partly because operating on a swollen, stiff knee raises the risk of it staying stiff. The waiting is doing something rather than nothing.

Q. Will I need a brace? Often yes, for the medial side, and the type and duration depend on how severe that injury is. Your surgeon or physiotherapist will set it.

Q. What if the inner side stays loose? That is the situation where the medial side may need its own treatment. It is uncommon and it is specifically looked for before any cruciate operation, because a loose medial side makes a cruciate graft work harder.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The medial collateral ligament is the primary restraint to valgus force, with superficial and deep components and the posterior oblique ligament behind them. 1.2.4 covers the ligaments.

It is extra-articular with a good blood supply, which is the basis for its reliable healing. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Combined cruciate and medial collateral injury follows the valgus contact mechanism. 6.2.2 covers contact injury.

Examination

Valgus stress testing in extension and in slight flexion separates the structures involved, because the posteromedial corner contributes in extension. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Laxity in extension suggests a more extensive injury than laxity in flexion alone. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Management Overview

Most medial collateral injuries are managed non-operatively with bracing and progressive motion. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The cruciate reconstruction follows once the medial side is competent, which is the standard sequence. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Operative treatment of the medial side is reserved for persistent instability, a distal avulsion that cannot appose, or a severe posteromedial injury. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • It is the primary restraint to valgus force.
  • It is extra-articular with a good blood supply.
  • Valgus testing in extension and flexion separates the structures.
  • Most are managed non-operatively with bracing and motion.
  • The cruciate reconstruction follows once the medial side is competent.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer tests the medial side in two positions and says why.

The expected points are the two position examination, the posteromedial contribution in extension, the reliable healing, the sequencing decision, and the exceptions to non-operative management. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Say that laxity in full extension raises the posteromedial corner rather than the collateral alone. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Say that a medial side left lax makes a cruciate graft carry load it was not designed for. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Management Overview

Brace, restore motion, confirm medial competence, then reconstruct the cruciate. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The interval is bounded by meniscal repairability, which is the counterweight to waiting. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Complications and Prognosis

A medial side that heals in a lengthened position leaves residual valgus laxity and is found on examination rather than on a scan. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Stiffness is the other complication, and it follows prolonged immobilisation rather than the injury itself. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Key Learning Points

  • Test in two positions and say why.
  • Laxity in full extension raises the posteromedial corner.
  • A lax medial side makes the cruciate graft carry extra load.
  • Brace, restore motion, confirm competence, then reconstruct.
  • The interval is bounded by meniscal repairability.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to justify an interval rather than quote one.

Waiting lets the medial side heal and lets the knee regain motion, both of which improve the cruciate result. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Waiting risks a meniscal tear becoming unrepairable in an unstable knee, which is the irreversible cost. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The interval is therefore set by the knee: medial competence and full extension reached, meniscus not deteriorating. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

A distal superficial avulsion displaced over the pes cannot appose and will not heal, which is the pattern that changes the plan from waiting to operating. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Complications and Prognosis

Residual valgus laxity after a healed medial injury is the complication that compromises a subsequent cruciate reconstruction. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

It is found by examining before the reconstruction, which is why that examination is a decision point rather than a formality. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is the optimal interval between medial injury and cruciate reconstruction. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The second is which medial injuries need operative treatment rather than bracing. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The third is whether residual low grade valgus laxity measurably affects graft survival. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Justify an interval rather than quote one.
  • Waiting improves the cruciate result and risks the meniscus.
  • The knee sets the interval, not the calendar.
  • A distal avulsion over the pes cannot appose and changes the plan.
  • The pre-reconstruction examination is a decision point.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the medial collateral injury is where this group's timing argument is most concrete, because both costs are real and both are visible.

A knee reconstructed too early is stiff, and stiffness after a combined injury is harder to treat than after an isolated one because more tissue was injured. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

A knee reconstructed too late has lost a meniscus, and that loss is not recoverable by any subsequent operation. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The trap specific to this injury is the medial side that healed long: the knee feels stable to the patient, tests slightly lax to the examiner, and loads the cruciate graft for the rest of its life. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Examining the medial side deliberately before every cruciate reconstruction in a knee with this history is the habit that catches it, and a scan will not. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • Both costs of the timing decision are real and visible here.
  • Stiffness after a combined injury is harder to treat.
  • A lost meniscus is not recoverable by any later operation.
  • The trap is the medial side healed long.
  • Examine the medial side before every reconstruction in such a knee.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that waiting is the right answer and looks like delay to everybody involved.

A patient with two torn ligaments expects urgency, a referrer expects a date, and the correct plan is a brace and a review, which reads as inaction unless it is explained as treatment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Explaining it once rarely holds, because the wait is longer than the explanation lasts, so it is worth restating at each review with what has changed. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The five things set how long to hold. The patient's age and physiology, which set healing rate and therefore the realistic interval. Their medical comorbidities, which change both healing and stiffness risk. The demands they put on the knee, including a season or a job that creates pressure on the date. Their psychological state, because waiting with an unstable knee is harder than it sounds. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which usually include an operation sooner than the knee wants one.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and name the meniscus as the thing the clock is actually about. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • Waiting is the right answer and looks like delay.
  • A brace and a review reads as inaction unless explained as treatment.
  • The wait outlasts a single explanation, so restate it at each review.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations set how long to hold.

  • Name the meniscus as the thing the clock is about.
