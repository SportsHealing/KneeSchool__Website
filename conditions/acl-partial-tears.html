Partial Tears

Page 6.3.4 · ACL Tear Patterns · condition page

The least reproducible diagnosis in the chapter, because partial describes an appearance and function decides the treatment.

Partial Tears

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

A ligament can tear all the way through, or some of its fibres can tear while the rest hold. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

The second is called a partial tear. The ligament is still joined up, but it is not as strong as it was. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

Deciding how much has torn is difficult, which is why two people looking at the same scan can disagree. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

How It Presents

The knee swells and hurts like any other ACL injury. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Some partial tears settle and the knee works normally. Others behave like a full tear once sport starts again. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • Some fibres tear while the rest hold.
  • The ligament is still joined up but weaker.
  • Deciding how much has torn is difficult.
  • Two people can disagree about the same scan.
  • Some settle and some behave like a full tear.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

A partial tear means some of the ligament's fibres have failed and the ligament remains continuous. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

The term describes what the ligament looks like rather than what the knee can do, and those two things do not always agree. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

How It Presents

The acute symptoms are the same as a complete tear, with rapid swelling. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

The difference appears later. A knee with a functionally competent partial tear behaves normally; one that is not behaves like a complete tear. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Management Overview

Treatment is guided by how the knee behaves rather than by the words on the scan report. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

A stable knee with a partial tear is managed with rehabilitation and watched. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A knee that gives way is treated as functionally deficient whatever the report said about the fibres. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Some fibres have failed and the ligament remains continuous.
  • The term describes the appearance rather than the knee's function.
  • Acute symptoms are the same as a complete tear.
  • Treatment follows how the knee behaves.
  • A knee that gives way is treated as deficient whatever the report says.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. My scan says partial tear. Is that good news? It is better than a complete tear if the knee is stable, and it means little on its own. What matters is whether the knee holds when you turn on it, and that is established by examination and by time rather than by the scan.

Q. Can a partial tear become a complete one? Yes. A knee that gives way loads the remaining fibres, and further injury is possible. That is one reason instability is taken seriously rather than tolerated.

Q. Will it heal? Partially torn ligaments can recover function, and whether the torn fibres heal as such is less clear. The practical question is whether the knee is stable, which is answerable, rather than whether the fibres healed, which mostly is not.

Q. Why do different doctors say different things about my scan? Because this is genuinely the least agreed diagnosis in this area. Distinguishing a partial tear from a complete tear with intact-looking remnant, or from a healed one, is difficult, and reasonable readers differ.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

A partial tear disrupts some fibres with preserved continuity of the ligament. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

The bundles can be affected differentially, so a tear can involve one bundle with the other intact, which is the anatomical basis for the term. 2.7.8 covers the anteromedial bundle.

Continuity is the defining feature and does not establish competence, which is the distinction the whole page turns on. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Examination

A Lachman test with a firm endpoint and a negative pivot shift supports functional competence. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

A positive pivot shift makes the knee functionally deficient regardless of the imaging description. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

The examination therefore outranks the report here, which is not true of most diagnoses. 4.10.9 covers the rotational instability.

Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging reports fibre continuity with altered signal, and distinguishing that from a complete tear with an apposed remnant is the difficulty. 5.7.2 covers partial tears.

Secondary signs are usually absent or mild, which supports the diagnosis without confirming it. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Key Learning Points

  • Some fibres are disrupted and the ligament remains continuous.
  • Bundles can be affected differentially.
  • Continuity does not establish competence.
  • Imaging struggles to separate this from a complete tear with an apposed remnant.
  • A positive pivot shift makes the knee deficient whatever the report says.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer treats the term with suspicion and says why, and the examination is what it falls back on.

The pivot shift is the discriminating test, because it reports the function the remaining fibres are supposed to be providing. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

A guarded acute knee cannot answer it, which is why this diagnosis is often deferred rather than made acutely. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Say that an examination under anaesthesia resolves what the scan cannot, where the answer changes management. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Investigations and Imaging

The expected points are the preserved continuity, the differential bundle involvement, the imaging difficulty, the primacy of examination, and the behaviour over time. 5.7.2 covers partial tears.

Say that interobserver agreement on this diagnosis is poor, which is a statement about the test rather than about any reader. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Management Overview

Rehabilitate, reassess, and let the knee's behaviour decide. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Reconstruct if the knee is functionally deficient, treating it as a complete tear in practice. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Treat the term with suspicion and say why.
  • Interobserver agreement is poor, which is a fact about the test.
  • An examination under anaesthesia resolves what the scan cannot.
  • The pivot shift is the discriminating test.
  • Rehabilitate, reassess, and let behaviour decide.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to refuse to manage a scan report and to manage a knee.

A functionally competent partial tear is managed non-operatively and followed, and the follow up is the active part rather than a formality. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

A functionally deficient knee is reconstructed, and whether the remnant is preserved is a technical question rather than a diagnostic one. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Selective bundle augmentation is described, is technically demanding, and rests on a diagnosis this page has just argued is unreliable, which is the tension worth naming. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Complications and Prognosis

The characteristic error is accepting a partial tear label and discharging a knee that later gives way, damaging a meniscus in the interval. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The opposite error is reconstructing a competent knee on the strength of a scan, which costs an unnecessary graft. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether selective bundle reconstruction outperforms standard reconstruction in a genuinely partial tear. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

The second is whether remnant preservation improves proprioception or revascularisation meaningfully. 2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

The third is whether the term should survive at all, given the agreement problem. 5.7.2 covers partial tears.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Manage a knee rather than a scan report.
  • Follow up is the active part of non-operative management here.
  • Remnant preservation is a technical rather than a diagnostic question.
  • Discharging a labelled partial tear can cost a meniscus.
  • Reconstructing a competent knee costs an unnecessary graft.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the partial tear is the clearest example in this group of a diagnosis that exists because imaging can see something, rather than because the distinction changes what is done.

Every management decision available here is taken on function, and function is assessed clinically, so the imaging label contributes little except reassurance. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Its harm is that reassurance: a patient told they have a partial tear hears a lesser injury and frequently stops attending, and the knee that then gives way presents late with a meniscal tear. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The fellow's job is usually to reinterpret the label for the patient rather than to dispute it with the radiologist, since both are describing the same appearance. 5.7.2 covers partial tears.

The honest summary is that partial is a radiological observation and competent is a clinical conclusion, and only the second should drive anything. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • The diagnosis exists because imaging can see something.
  • Every available decision is taken on function.
  • Its harm is false reassurance and lost follow up.
  • The knee that gives way presents late with a meniscal tear.
  • Partial is radiological; competent is clinical, and only the second drives

anything.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question is how to use a term that the service generates, the patient believes, and the evidence does not support as a management category.

Correcting it in clinic costs time and risks appearing to contradict a colleague who described exactly what they saw. 5.7.2 covers partial tears.

Leaving it uncorrected lets a patient carry a false reassurance into the activity that will test it. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The workable approach is to rename the question in front of the patient: not how torn is the ligament, but does this knee hold. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

The five things decide how hard to press. The patient's age and physiology, which set how many years a wrong call costs. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which decide whether the partial tear will ever be tested. Their psychological state, because reassurance already given is hard to withdraw. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which by this point include a word from a report they have read.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The service generates a term the evidence does not support as a category.
  • Correcting it risks appearing to contradict a colleague who described what

they saw.

  • Leaving it carries false reassurance into the activity that tests it.
  • Rename the question: not how torn, but does this knee hold.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide how hard to press.
