Healing ACL Tears

Healing ACL Tears

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

For a long time the ACL was described as a ligament that cannot heal. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

Scans now sometimes show a torn ACL that has joined back up. That does not always mean it is doing its job again. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Being joined and being strong enough are two different things, and only the second matters to the knee. 6.5.1 covers instability.

How It Presents

A knee with a healed looking ligament can still give way on turning, and a knee with a healed ligament can also be perfectly steady. 6.3.4 covers partial tears.

Which of those it is gets decided by testing the knee rather than by looking at the scan again.

Key Learning Points

  • The ACL was long described as a ligament that cannot heal.
  • Scans sometimes show a torn one that has joined back up.
  • Joined and strong enough are different things.
  • A healed looking ligament can still allow giving way.
  • Testing the knee decides it, not the scan.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Some torn ACLs reconnect over the months after injury, and this is seen more often than it used to be, partly because more knees are scanned again. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

A reconnected ligament is not automatically a working one. It can be longer than the original, or weaker, and then the knee is still unstable. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

How It Presents

Some people with a healed ligament have a knee that is steady and need nothing further. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Others have a knee that looks healed and still gives way, and they are treated according to the knee rather than the picture. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Management Overview

Treatment follows the knee's behaviour. A steady knee is watched; an unstable one is treated as deficient whatever the scan shows. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Bracing protocols intended to encourage healing exist and are being studied. They are not established treatment, and anyone offering one should say which parts are known and which are not. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

Key Learning Points

  • Some torn ACLs reconnect over the months after injury.
  • A reconnected ligament is not automatically a working one.
  • It can be longer or weaker than the original.
  • Treatment follows the knee's behaviour rather than the picture.
  • Protocols intended to encourage healing are being studied, not established.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. I was told the ACL never heals. Was that wrong? It was the accepted position and it was based on real experience of repairs failing. What has changed is that healing is now observed in some knees. How reliable it is, and in whom, is the part still being worked out.

Q. My scan says the ligament looks continuous now. Am I fixed? Possibly. Continuity on a scan does not establish that the ligament is doing its job, so the answer comes from examining the knee and from how it behaves when you turn on it.

Q. Should I ask about a bracing protocol? You can ask, and you should expect a clear answer about what is known. These protocols are under study. They are not a standard alternative to the two established routes.

Q. If it healed, can it tear again? Yes, and a healed ligament that is longer than the original may be more vulnerable. That is one reason the knee's behaviour matters more than its appearance.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The ACL's poor intrinsic healing has been attributed to its intra-articular, extrasynovial position, its limited mid-substance blood supply, and the dispersal of any clot by synovial fluid. 2.7.4 covers the blood supply.

Healing is nonetheless observed, most often in proximal tears where the ends remain apposed. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

A healed ligament may be continuous and elongated, which is a different state from a normal ligament and from a ruptured one. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Examination

A healed ligament is assessed by function, so the Lachman test and the pivot shift carry the diagnosis. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

A continuous ligament with a positive pivot shift is functionally deficient and is managed as such. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging reports continuity and signal, and cannot report length or tension. 5.7.8 covers ACL healing.

That is the limit of the modality rather than a reporting fault, and it is why serial imaging adds less here than serial examination. 5.7.2 covers partial tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Poor healing was attributed to position, blood supply and clot dispersal.
  • Healing is observed, most often in apposed proximal tears.
  • A healed ligament may be continuous and elongated.
  • Function carries the diagnosis rather than the scan.
  • Imaging reports continuity and cannot report tension.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer distinguishes structural continuity from functional competence and uses the examination to do it.

The expected points are the mechanisms of poor healing, the proximal apposed tear as the healing pattern, elongation as the failure mode, and the primacy of the pivot shift. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Say that continuity is not competence, which is the sentence this page exists for. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Say that a healed ligament in a stable knee needs no treatment and is not a reason to intervene. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Investigations and Imaging

Imaging documents the healing and does not grade it, so repeat scanning answers a question nobody is asking. 5.7.8 covers ACL healing.

Management Overview

Manage the knee: watch a stable one, reconstruct an unstable one. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Do not offer an unproven protocol as though it were a third established route. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Distinguish structural continuity from functional competence.
  • The apposed proximal tear is the healing pattern.
  • Elongation is the failure mode.
  • Continuity is not competence.
  • Repeat scanning answers a question nobody is asking.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level this topic tests whether a candidate can hold an emerging area without overstating it.

The established routes remain rehabilitation and reconstruction, and a candidate should say so before discussing anything else. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Protocols aimed at promoting healing are under investigation, with selection based on tear location and apposition. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

The honest framing is that observation of healing is established and its clinical reliability is not, and those are different claims. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Complications and Prognosis

An elongated healed ligament gives a knee that is lax with a continuous ligament, which is the state most likely to be misreported as intact. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A patient reassured by a healed appearance and returning to pivoting sport is exposed in the way this chapter keeps describing, with the meniscus paying. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is which tears heal reliably enough to be managed expectantly. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

The second is whether bracing protocols change the healing rate or select for knees that would have healed. 6.3.4 covers partial tears.

The third is how a healed ligament performs over a decade of pivoting sport, which no series is yet long enough to answer. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Say the established routes first.
  • Selection rests on tear location and apposition.
  • Observation of healing is established; its reliability is not.
  • An elongated healed ligament is most likely to be misreported as intact.
  • No series is yet long enough to answer the decade question.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this is the area where a fellow will see the gap between what is being published and what is being practised, and will be asked about it by patients who have read more than the clinic has.

The teaching that this ligament cannot heal was a reasonable generalisation from repair failures, and it hardened into a fact, which is how generalisations behave. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

Revising it is correct and carries its own risk, because an area with enthusiasm, early series and patient demand is where overstatement happens. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

The defensible position is specific: healing is observed, it is commoner in apposed proximal tears, continuity does not establish competence, and the knee's behaviour decides treatment regardless. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

A fellow who can say all four sentences separately will counsel better than one who holds either the old position or the new one whole. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The old teaching was a reasonable generalisation that hardened into a fact.
  • Revising it is correct and carries its own risk.
  • Enthusiasm, early series and patient demand invite overstatement.
  • Healing is observed and commoner in apposed proximal tears.
  • The knee's behaviour decides treatment regardless.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant problem is a patient arriving with a printed protocol and asking for it by name.

Refusing without explanation reads as conservatism, and agreeing without qualification commits a patient to an unproven route in an injury with two established ones. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

The usable middle is to separate the claims: that healing occurs, that it occurs more in certain tears, that the protocol's contribution is unproven, and that the decision point remains how the knee behaves. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

The five things decide what to offer. The patient's age and physiology, which set healing capacity and the years at stake. Their medical comorbidities, which bear on healing directly here. The demands they put on the knee, because a pivoting athlete is where an unproven route costs most. Their psychological state, including their tolerance for an uncertain plan. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which in this topic are frequently well informed and occasionally better read than the clinic.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and record what was explained. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Patients arrive asking for a named protocol.
  • Refusing without explanation reads as conservatism.
  • Agreeing without qualification commits them to an unproven route.
  • Separate the claims rather than accepting or rejecting them together.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide what to offer.
