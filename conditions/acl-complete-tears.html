Complete Tears

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

A complete tear means the ligament has torn all the way across. The two ends are no longer joined. 6.3.4 covers partial tears.

Unlike a partial tear, there is usually no disagreement about this one. It is clear on a scan and clear on examination. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

How It Presents

A pop, swelling within hours, and later a knee that gives way when it turns. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Because the diagnosis is usually straightforward, the interesting question becomes what else was injured at the same time. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The ligament has torn all the way across.
  • The two ends are no longer joined.
  • There is usually no disagreement about this diagnosis.
  • It shows clearly on a scan and on examination.
  • The interesting question is what else was injured.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

A complete rupture means the ligament is no longer continuous between the thigh bone and the shin bone. 6.3.4 covers partial tears.

This is the diagnosis most people mean by a torn ACL. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

How It Presents

Rapid swelling, inability to continue, and later a sense that the knee cannot be trusted when changing direction. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Walking and running straight often recover first, which is why the injury can be underestimated early. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Management Overview

The choice is between rehabilitation alone and reconstruction followed by the same rehabilitation. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

A completely torn ligament does not reliably heal back to its original strength, so an operation replaces it rather than repairing it. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

What decides the choice is whether the knee is stable during what you need it to do, and what else was injured. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The ligament is no longer continuous.
  • This is what most people mean by a torn ACL.
  • Straight line function often recovers first.
  • The ligament does not reliably heal back to its original strength.
  • Instability and what else was injured decide the treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Is a complete tear always an operation? No. Some people are stable enough for what they do without one. The decision rests on instability and demand rather than on the fact of the tear.

Q. How certain is the diagnosis? Usually very. Unlike a partial tear, a complete rupture is consistently identified on examination and on imaging, and the two usually agree.

Q. If it is so clear, why does the scan report say so much else? Because the cruciate is rarely injured alone. The rest of the report is where the useful information usually is, and some of it, particularly the meniscus, matters more for the long term than the ligament.

Q. Can I wait before deciding? Often yes, provided the knee is not giving way. Episodes of giving way are what cause further damage, so they are the reason to stop waiting rather than the passage of time.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

A complete rupture disrupts the ligament's continuity between its femoral and tibial footprints. 2.7.1 covers the gross anatomy.

It may be mid-substance, proximal or distal, and the location is a treatment variable rather than a severity grading. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

Examination

A Lachman test with increased translation and a soft endpoint is the most reliable acute finding. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

A positive pivot shift confirms rotational deficiency and is the finding that correlates with the patient's complaint. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Both are degraded by guarding, so the acute examination is repeated rather than relied on. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging confirms the rupture and, more usefully, surveys the rest of the joint. 5.7.3 covers complete tears.

Secondary signs including the bone bruise pattern, anterior tibial translation and a deep lateral sulcus support the diagnosis. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

The meniscus, the posteromedial junction and the anterolateral structures are assessed deliberately rather than incidentally. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • Continuity between the footprints is disrupted.
  • The location is a treatment variable rather than a grading.
  • The Lachman test is the most reliable acute finding.
  • The pivot shift correlates with the patient's complaint.
  • The scan's real value is the survey rather than the cruciate.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva this is the diagnosis to make quickly so that the rest of the answer has room.

The expected points are the Lachman and pivot shift findings, the effect of guarding, the role of examination under anaesthesia, and the move to the associated injuries. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Say that a confident complete tear diagnosis should accelerate the answer to what else is injured rather than dwell on the cruciate. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Investigations and Imaging

The study is read as a survey, and the structures most often missed are named rather than assumed. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

The bruise pattern corroborates the mechanism, which in turn predicts the company. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Management Overview

Rehabilitation alone and reconstruction are both legitimate, and the choice follows instability and demand. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

A repairable meniscus moves the decision earlier, which is the commonest reason timing changes. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • Make the diagnosis quickly so the rest of the answer has room.
  • Move to what else is injured rather than dwelling on the cruciate.
  • Read the study as a survey and name what was assessed.
  • Both rehabilitation and reconstruction are legitimate.
  • A repairable meniscus moves the decision earlier.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the complete tear is the stem and the question is always about something else.

The decision is instability against demand, with the associated injuries as the modifier, and a candidate who recites graft options without establishing the indication has answered the wrong question. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Timing follows the knee rather than the diagnosis: settled swelling, full extension and quadriceps control before surgery. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

The exception is a locked knee, where the mechanical block overrides the sequence. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Complications and Prognosis

Re-rupture concentrates in young patients returning to pivoting sport, which is the group most often reconstructed. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Meniscal loss during a period of instability is the irreversible complication and the one the timing argument exists to prevent. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether reconstruction changes the long term joint outcome. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The second is who can be managed non-operatively, since copers cannot be identified reliably in advance. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

The third is when an anterolateral procedure should be added. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • The complete tear is the stem and the question is about something else.
  • Establish the indication before discussing grafts.
  • Timing follows the knee rather than the diagnosis.
  • A locked knee overrides the sequence.
  • Meniscal loss during instability is the irreversible complication.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the complete tear is where the chapter's organising claim pays off: the pattern is a treatment question and the completeness is not.

A complete proximal tear, a complete mid-substance tear and a complete tibial avulsion are the same diagnosis by completeness and three different operations by location. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

Reading a report for the word complete and stopping therefore discards the information that would change the plan. 6.3.3 covers the tibial avulsion.

The same applies to the survey: the ligament is confirmed in seconds and the meniscus, the roots and the anterolateral complex take the rest of the review. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

A fellow's discipline here is to spend the least time on the most certain finding, which is the opposite of how most reports are read. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • The pattern is a treatment question and completeness is not.
  • One diagnosis by completeness is three operations by location.
  • Reading for the word complete discards what changes the plan.
  • The ligament is confirmed in seconds and the survey takes the review.
  • Spend the least time on the most certain finding.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that the certainty of this diagnosis makes the consultation feel settled before the decision has been made.

A patient arriving with a confirmed complete tear usually believes the conversation is about which operation, and the conversation that is actually owed is whether to operate. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Reframing that without appearing to withhold treatment is the skill, and it is easier when the non-operative route is named early rather than offered as a consolation. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The five things decide which route fits. The patient's age and physiology, which set healing and the decades the joint must serve. Their medical comorbidities, which change graft healing and operative risk. The demands they put on the knee, which is the single most informative item and is obtained by asking what they intend to do. Their psychological state, because fear of reinjury shapes the result of either route. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which usually arrive already containing an operation.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and say what the scan does not decide. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • Certainty makes the consultation feel settled before the decision is made.
  • Patients arrive believing the question is which operation.
  • The question owed is whether to operate.
  • Name the non-operative route early rather than as a consolation.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide which route fits.
