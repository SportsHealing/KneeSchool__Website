Acute Haemarthrosis

Page 6.2.5 · Acute ACL Injury · condition page

Blood in the joint within hours is a structural injury until proven otherwise, which is the whole of this page's clinical use.

Acute Haemarthrosis

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

How It Presents

After some knee injuries the joint swells up within a couple of hours. That quick swelling is bleeding inside the knee. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Swelling that creeps up over the next day or two is usually fluid rather than blood, and it means something different. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

Fast swelling matters because it usually means something inside has torn rather than been strained. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Investigations and Imaging

A doctor examines the knee first. A scan is often arranged, because the things that bleed are the things that need identifying. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Swelling within a couple of hours is bleeding inside the joint.
  • Slower swelling over a day or two is usually not blood.
  • Fast swelling usually means something has torn.
  • The speed of the swelling is a useful clue by itself.
  • It is a reason to be examined rather than to wait and see.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

How It Presents

A haemarthrosis is blood inside the knee joint, and it fills the knee quickly, usually within a few hours of the injury. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

The knee feels tight and full, bending is limited by the pressure rather than by pain alone, and it is often warm. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Slower swelling, appearing the following day, is usually an effusion, which is joint fluid rather than blood, and it follows a different set of injuries. 6.3.4 covers partial tears.

Investigations and Imaging

Rapid swelling after a twisting injury is one of the clearest reasons to arrange a scan, because the structures that bleed are the ones worth identifying. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

An x-ray is often taken as well, to look for a fracture rather than for the ligament. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Draining the knee is sometimes done to relieve pressure, and it is for comfort rather than for diagnosis. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • A haemarthrosis is blood in the joint and fills the knee within hours.
  • The knee feels tight and full, and bending is limited by pressure.
  • Slower swelling the next day is usually joint fluid, not blood.
  • Rapid swelling is a clear reason to arrange a scan.
  • Draining is for comfort rather than for diagnosis.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does a haemarthrosis always mean a torn ACL? No. It means something that bleeds has been injured. The cruciate is the commonest cause, and a fracture, a peripheral meniscal tear or a dislocated patella can all produce it.

Q. Is draining the knee necessary? Usually not. It can relieve a very tight, painful knee, and it does not change the diagnosis or the treatment. Most are left to resorb on their own.

Q. How long does it take to settle? Usually a few weeks for the swelling itself. Getting the knee moving and the quadriceps working again takes longer, and that work starts before any decision about surgery.

Q. My knee swelled the next morning rather than straight away. Does that mean it is less serious? It points away from bleeding and towards joint fluid, which follows a different group of injuries. It is a useful distinction and not a guarantee, so it is still worth being examined.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

How It Presents

Haemarthrosis develops within hours; a simple effusion develops over a day or more, and the distinction is obtained from the history rather than the examination. 6.2.1 covers non-contact injury.

The knee is tense, warm and held in slight flexion, which is the position of greatest capsular volume. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Range is limited by pressure, so a knee that will not extend because it is tense is a different finding from one that will not extend because something is blocking it. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Examination

A tense knee limits every ligament test, which is why the examination is repeated once the swelling settles. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Investigations and Imaging

The differential for an acute traumatic haemarthrosis is led by cruciate rupture, then osteochondral fracture, peripheral meniscal tear and patellar dislocation. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

Fat globules in an aspirate indicate a fracture communicating with the joint, which changes the diagnosis. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Radiographs are taken for fracture and for avulsion rather than for the ligament. 6.3.3 covers tibial avulsion.

Key Learning Points

  • Haemarthrosis develops in hours and an effusion over a day or more.
  • The distinction comes from the history.
  • The knee is held in slight flexion, the position of greatest volume.
  • Pressure limiting extension differs from a mechanical block.
  • Fat in an aspirate indicates a fracture into the joint.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva acute traumatic haemarthrosis should be treated as a differential rather than as a diagnosis, and the examination is where that starts.

The tense knee is the reason the acute examination is the least reliable one in the episode, and the reason a repeat examination is planned rather than offered. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Where the diagnosis will change management immediately, an examination under anaesthesia removes the limitation. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Investigations and Imaging

The expected points are the time course, the leading causes in order, the fat globule sign, the role of radiographs, and the limitation the swelling places on examination. 6.3.3 covers tibial avulsion.

Say that cruciate rupture leads the differential and does not exhaust it. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

Say that aspiration is therapeutic rather than diagnostic in most cases, with the fat globule finding as the exception. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Management Overview

Settle the swelling, restore extension and get the quadriceps working, because all three precede any operative decision. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Operating on a tense, stiff, inflamed knee is the recognised route to arthrofibrosis, which is why inflammation is settled first. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • Treat acute haemarthrosis as a differential rather than a diagnosis.
  • Cruciate rupture leads it and does not exhaust it.
  • Aspiration is therapeutic except where fat globules appear.
  • The acute examination is the least reliable of the episode.
  • Settle swelling, restore extension and recover the quadriceps first.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level this is a question about what the swelling permits rather than about the swelling itself.

The sequence is settle the effusion, restore full extension, recover quadriceps control, then decide, and departing from it is what produces stiff knees. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Full passive extension before surgery is the single most defensible prerequisite in this chapter, because losing it after surgery is far harder to regain. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

A locked knee is the exception that overrides the sequence, because the block is mechanical and will not settle with time. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Complications and Prognosis

Arthrofibrosis is the complication this sequence exists to prevent, and it is substantially a timing complication rather than a technical one. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Persistent quadriceps inhibition after a haemarthrosis is common and underestimated, and it limits rehabilitation long after the swelling has gone. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether early aspiration improves recovery of quadriceps function or merely comfort. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

The second is how much of the stiffness attributed to early surgery is actually attributable to operating on a knee that had not regained extension. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

This page states both and settles neither, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The question is what the swelling permits, not the swelling itself.
  • Settle, extend, recover quadriceps, then decide.
  • Full passive extension before surgery is the defensible prerequisite.
  • A locked knee overrides the sequence.
  • Arthrofibrosis is largely a timing complication.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the haemarthrosis is interesting for what it does to the muscle rather than for what it does to the joint.

Joint distension inhibits the quadriceps reflexly, and that inhibition outlasts the swelling, which is why strength lags behind the knee's appearance. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

A patient whose extension and quadriceps control are not restored before surgery starts the post-operative programme already behind, and the deficit tends to persist rather than to be made up. 6.5.1 covers instability.

That makes the pre-operative phase part of the operation's result, which is the part most easily lost when a list date arrives before the knee is ready. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

The defensible position is that the knee's readiness sets the date rather than the other way round, and that this is a surgical decision rather than an administrative one. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Distension inhibits the quadriceps and the inhibition outlasts the swelling.
  • Strength lags behind the knee's appearance.
  • A patient operated on early starts the programme behind.
  • The pre-operative phase is part of the operation's result.
  • The knee's readiness should set the date.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant version of this page is about a conflict between the knee's readiness and everything else pressing on the date.

A patient with a season, a contract, a job or a waiting list target has reasons to be operated on sooner, and the knee has reasons not to be. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Conceding to the first reliably produces the stiffness this page exists to avoid, and the patient will attribute it to the operation rather than to the timing. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

The five things decide how firmly to hold the line. The patient's age and physiology, because a stiff knee in a young patient costs more years. Their medical comorbidities, which change how quickly the knee will settle. The demands they put on the knee, which is what creates the pressure in the first place. Their psychological state, because waiting is harder for some than others. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which usually include a date they have already planned around.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and say plainly that the knee's state, not the calendar, is what the decision rests on. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The conflict is between the knee's readiness and the pressure on the date.
  • Conceding to the date reliably produces stiffness.
  • The patient will attribute that stiffness to the operation.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide how firmly to hold.

  • Say plainly that the knee's state rather than the calendar decides.
