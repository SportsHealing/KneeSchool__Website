Neuromuscular Factors

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

Neuromuscular control is how the brain and muscles work together to hold a joint steady while the body is moving. 2.7.10 covers proprioception.

Landing, turning and stopping all need it, and it can be trained in the same way strength can. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Causes and Risk Factors

The patterns that load the ligament most are landing with a straight knee, letting the knee fall inwards, and landing on one leg with the body leaning the wrong way. 3.3.13 covers kinematics during landing.

All three are habits rather than facts about a body, which is the reason this page matters. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Key Learning Points

  • Neuromuscular control is the brain and muscles holding a joint steady.
  • Landing, turning and stopping all need it.
  • A straight knee and a knee falling inwards load the ligament.
  • Those are habits rather than fixed facts about a body.
  • Control can be trained like strength.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Neuromuscular control describes the timing and coordination of muscle activity that keeps the knee in a safe position during fast movements. 2.7.10 covers proprioception.

It is distinct from strength. A strong athlete with poor control is a described and common pattern. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Causes and Risk Factors

Four features recur in the descriptions of high risk movement: the knee falling inwards on landing, a stiff and extended knee at contact, the quadriceps dominating over the hamstrings, and poor trunk control over the planted leg. 3.3.13 covers kinematics during landing.

They tend to appear together rather than separately, which is why training programmes address movement as a whole rather than one joint. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Rehabilitation Outline

Training that changes these patterns exists, and it looks like coached jumping, landing, cutting and strength work rather than like physiotherapy. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

It needs doing regularly over months and it stops working when it stops being done, which is the honest limitation. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Control is the timing and coordination that keeps the knee safe.
  • It is distinct from strength.
  • Knee falling inwards, a stiff landing, quadriceps dominance, poor trunk control.
  • The features appear together rather than separately.
  • Training works while it is being done and stops working when it stops.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Can a coach tell by watching whether I land badly? Some features are visible, particularly the knee falling inwards. Formal scoring systems exist for this. Watching catches the obvious and misses the rest.

Q. If I am strong, is my control good? Not necessarily. Strength and control are separate qualities, and an athlete can be strong and still land in a position that loads the ligament.

Q. Does this mean the injury was my fault? No. Movement patterns are learned and largely unconscious, and nobody chooses their landing mechanics in the fraction of a second that matters.

Q. How long does it take to change a landing pattern? Weeks to months of regular work, and it is maintained rather than completed. The prevention page describes what the programmes involve.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The neuromuscular account proposes that sex differences in movement strategy, rather than in tissue, carry much of the difference in injury rate. 3.7.15 covers female ACL biomechanics.

Its appeal is that every component named is measurable and trainable, which the hormonal and anatomical accounts are not. 6.6.2 covers hormonal factors.

Causes and Risk Factors

Four described patterns carry most of the literature. 3.3.13 covers kinematics during landing.

Dynamic knee valgus, meaning knee abduction with hip internal rotation during landing, is the most studied and the most visible. 3.7.12 covers ACL loading during landing.

Ligament dominance, quadriceps dominance, leg dominance and trunk dominance are the four headings usually taught, and they describe strategy rather than anatomy. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Reduced knee flexion at initial contact raises anterior tibial shear, which is the mechanical link between the pattern and the ligament. 3.7.12 covers ACL loading during landing.

Examination

Observation of a single leg landing, with the knee watched in the frontal plane, is the clinic level assessment. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing assessment.

Scored systems exist and improve consistency between observers. 4.21.6 covers the landing error scoring system.

Laboratory motion analysis quantifies the knee abduction moment, and it is a research tool rather than a clinic one. 3.3.13 covers kinematics during landing.

Key Learning Points

  • The proposal is that movement strategy carries much of the difference.
  • Its components are measurable and trainable.
  • Dynamic knee valgus is the most studied pattern.
  • Ligament, quadriceps, leg and trunk dominance describe strategy.
  • Reduced knee flexion at contact raises anterior tibial shear.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer separates what a clinic can assess from what a laboratory can measure.

Say single leg landing observation in the frontal plane, with a scored system if one is used. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing assessment.

Say that the knee abduction moment is a laboratory quantity, and resist quoting a threshold, because the screening claim built on thresholds is contested. 4.21.6 covers the landing error scoring system.

Say that movement screening describes a group better than it predicts an individual. 4.4.10 covers functional movement screening.

Management Overview

The management implication is that the modifiable factor is movement, and that it is addressed by coached training rather than by an operation. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

After reconstruction the same patterns need retraining, because surgery restores the restraint and not the strategy. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Rehabilitation Outline

Programmes combine plyometrics, strength, balance and technique feedback, and the technique feedback is the part most often dropped. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Criteria based release rather than time based release is the practical expression of this page in a clinic. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • Separate what a clinic can assess from what a laboratory can measure.
  • Resist quoting a threshold for the knee abduction moment.
  • Screening describes a group better than it predicts an individual.
  • Surgery restores the restraint and not the strategy.
  • Criteria based release is this page's clinic expression.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is asked how they would use movement assessment in a return to sport decision.

The defensible answer uses it as one criterion among several, states the others, and does not claim prediction for an individual. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

The criteria usually named are quadriceps strength symmetry, hop test symmetry, movement quality on landing, sport specific exposure completed, and the athlete's own confidence. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Naming the confidence item matters, since fear of re-injury predicts failure to return independently of any mechanical measure. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Complications and Prognosis

The prognostic value of these measures is real at group level and weak for the individual, and a release decision has to be made for an individual. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

That gap is the honest difficulty, and it is managed by using several criteria rather than by pretending any one of them predicts. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether movement screening can identify the individual at risk rather than describe the group. 4.4.10 covers functional movement screening.

The second is whether the knee abduction moment is a cause of injury or a marker of something else that causes it. 3.7.12 covers ACL loading during landing.

The third is whether criteria based release actually reduces second injury, which is widely assumed and less often demonstrated. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Use movement assessment as one criterion among several.
  • Name strength, hop symmetry, movement quality, exposure and confidence.
  • Fear of re-injury predicts failure to return independently.
  • Group level value and individual level weakness is the honest gap.
  • Several criteria manage that gap; one criterion cannot.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the problem is that movement quality is the criterion everybody endorses and almost nobody records.

Strength and hop symmetry arrive as numbers in a letter. Movement quality arrives as the word good, written by somebody who did not state what they watched. 4.4.9 covers single leg landing assessment.

The habit worth keeping is to record the task, the view, and the specific feature: single leg drop landing, frontal plane, knee valgus present or absent, with the limb named. Four items. 4.21.6 covers the landing error scoring system.

The second habit is to record whether the athlete returned to the same sport at the same level, because return to sport and return to performance are different outcomes and the literature mixes them. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Video helps and does not replace the written feature, since a clip without a stated finding leaves the next reader to make the judgement again. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Key Learning Points

  • Movement quality is endorsed by everybody and recorded by almost nobody.
  • Good is not a finding.
  • Record the task, the view, the feature and the limb.
  • Record return to the same sport at the same level.
  • A clip without a stated finding transfers no judgement.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that this page's content is delivered by other people. The surgeon identifies the pattern and a coach, a physiotherapist or nobody at all changes it. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

That makes the honest consultation one about what is available to this athlete rather than what is indicated for her, and those two answers differ by squad, school and postcode. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which set how trainable the pattern is and how long it has been practised. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which decide how often the pattern will be tested. Their psychological state, because confidence and fear both change movement and because an athlete told she moves wrongly may stop moving freely. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which often include an assumption that the operation fixed the problem.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and ask who will coach the landing. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Key Learning Points

  • The content of this page is delivered by other people.
  • What is available differs from what is indicated.
  • Availability varies by squad, school and postcode.
  • An athlete told she moves wrongly may stop moving freely.
  • Ask who will coach the landing.
