What is the ACL?

Page 6.1.1 · ACL Fundamentals · condition page

A short ligament deep in the knee that restrains a direction rather than a movement, which is why its loss is felt in sport and not in walking.

What is the ACL?

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

The anterior cruciate ligament, or ACL, is a tough band inside the knee. It runs at an angle from the thigh bone to the shin bone, crossing another ligament on its way. 1.2.4 covers the ligaments.

It is short, about as long as a thumbnail is wide, and it is one of the strongest things in the joint for its size.

Its job is to stop the shin bone sliding forward under the thigh bone, and to help control the knee when the leg twists. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

How It Presents

If it tears, people often hear or feel a pop, and the knee swells within a few hours. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Walking in a straight line can feel almost normal afterwards, which is one reason a tear is sometimes missed at first.

Turning and changing direction is where the knee feels wrong, because turning is the thing the ligament was controlling. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • The ACL is a short tough band crossing inside the knee.
  • It stops the shin bone sliding forward under the thigh bone.
  • It helps control the knee when the leg twists.
  • A tear often comes with a pop and swelling within hours.
  • Walking can feel normal while turning does not.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

The anterior cruciate ligament is one of two ligaments that cross inside the knee joint. It runs from the back of the outer thigh bone forwards and inwards to the front of the shin bone. 2.7.1 covers the gross anatomy.

It is the main restraint holding the shin bone back against forward slide, and it is a secondary restraint against the knee rotating too far inwards. 3.7.2 covers the primary restraints.

How It Presents

Most tears happen in a twisting or landing movement rather than a collision. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

The knee swells quickly, because the torn ligament bleeds into the joint. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Later, the knee can feel unreliable when you change direction, even though it feels steady walking. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Management Overview

A torn ACL does not always need an operation. What the knee is asked to do matters as much as what the scan shows. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The two routes are a structured rehabilitation programme, or a reconstruction that replaces the ligament with a graft and then the same rehabilitation afterwards. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A completely torn ACL rarely heals back to its original strength, which is why reconstruction replaces it rather than stitching it. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Key Learning Points

  • The ACL crosses inside the knee and holds the shin bone back.
  • It also helps resist the knee rotating inwards.
  • Most tears are twisting injuries, not collisions.
  • A quick swelling after a twist is bleeding inside the joint.
  • Whether an operation is needed depends on the knee and the person, not the

scan alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Where exactly is the ACL? Deep inside the knee joint, in the notch between the two knuckles at the bottom of the thigh bone. It is not something you can feel from outside.

Q. Why is it called cruciate? Because it crosses the other cruciate ligament inside the joint. Cruciate means cross shaped, and the two together form an X when the knee is viewed from the side.

Q. If it is so strong, why does it tear? Strength is not the whole story. The forces in a sudden twist on a planted foot are large and arrive fast, and the ligament is short, so it has little length over which to stretch.

Q. Does everyone with a torn ACL limp? No. Many people walk normally within weeks. The problem shows itself when the knee is asked to turn, which is why a normal walk does not rule a tear out.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The ACL is an intracapsular, extrasynovial ligament running from the posteromedial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle to the anterior intercondylar area of the tibia. 2.7.6 covers the femoral footprint.

It carries two functional bundles, anteromedial and posterolateral, named for their tibial insertions rather than their femoral ones. 2.7.8 covers the anteromedial bundle.

It is the primary restraint to anterior tibial translation and a secondary restraint to internal rotation, which is the pairing the whole group turns on. 3.7.8 covers the anterior stability.

Its blood supply arrives from the middle genicular artery, and it carries mechanoreceptors as well as collagen. 2.7.4 covers the blood supply.

Causes and Risk Factors

Ask what the knee was doing rather than what hit it, because most tears are non contact. 3.7.14 covers the ACL injury mechanisms.

The classical position is deceleration or landing near extension, with valgus and internal rotation of the tibia. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Female sex, previous injury and neuromuscular control are recognised contributors, and so is bony anatomy including tibial slope. 6.6.3 covers the neuromuscular factors.

Key Learning Points

  • The ACL is intracapsular and extrasynovial.
  • Its bundles are named for their tibial insertions.
  • It is the primary restraint to anterior translation.
  • It is a secondary restraint to internal rotation.
  • It carries mechanoreceptors as well as collagen.

MRCS Level

Definition

In a viva the expected answer gives the attachments, the bundles and the restraint role before anything about injury.

The expected points are the footprints, the two bundles, the primary and secondary restraints, the blood supply, and the extrasynovial position. 2.7.7 covers the tibial footprint.

Say that extrasynovial position matters, because it bears on healing and on why a torn ligament sits in blood rather than in synovial fluid. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Say that the bundles tighten at different parts of the arc, which is why a single test at a single angle does not report the whole ligament. 3.7.4 covers the bundle function.

Examination

The examination asks about translation and about rotation separately, because the ligament restrains both and a test can find one while missing the other. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

Anterior translation is the direct question and the one most reliably answered. 4.10.5 covers the anterior drawer test.

Rotation is the question the patient actually complains about, and it is the harder of the two to elicit. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

An acutely painful, guarded knee limits every one of them, which is why the examination is repeated rather than accepted once. 4.10.2 covers acute ACL injury.

Key Learning Points

  • Give attachments, bundles and restraint role before injury.
  • Extrasynovial position bears on how the ligament heals.
  • The bundles tighten at different parts of the arc.
  • Translation is the direct question and rotation the patient's complaint.
  • A guarded knee limits every test, so the examination is repeated.

FRCS Level

Examination

At examination level the ligament's anatomy is asked in order to explain a decision rather than for itself.

The femoral footprint's position decides where a graft is placed, so a question about anatomy is usually a question about tunnel position in disguise. 2.7.6 covers the femoral footprint.

The two bundle description underlies the single against double bundle argument, and the candidate is expected to know that the argument exists rather than to settle it. 3.7.4 covers the bundle function.

The secondary restraints matter because they carry rotation when the ACL cannot, and because their state changes what a reconstruction has to achieve. 3.7.3 covers the secondary restraints.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is how much of the rotational control attributed to this ligament belongs to the anterolateral structures beside it. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

The second is whether a proximal tear can be repaired rather than reconstructed, which turns on the ligament's own blood supply and on tissue quality. 2.7.4 covers the blood supply.

The third is what the mechanoreceptors contribute, and whether their loss explains the gap between a mechanically sound reconstruction and a knee the patient does not trust. 2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

None of the three is settled, and this page states them rather than resolving them, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Anatomy is asked at this level to explain a decision.
  • The femoral footprint question is a tunnel position question.
  • The anterolateral structures contest part of the rotational role.
  • Repair turns on blood supply and tissue quality.
  • Mechanoreceptor loss may explain a knee the patient does not trust.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this page is background to a decision made about a person, and the ligament's anatomy enters only where it changes what is possible.

A footprint that has been drilled before constrains revision, so the anatomy of the first operation outlives it. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The ligament's poor intrinsic healing is the reason the group's whole treatment arm is reconstruction rather than repair, and it is a property of the tissue rather than of the surgery. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

What the patient reports after a technically sound reconstruction is often about confidence rather than laxity, which points at the sensory role rather than the mechanical one. 2.7.10 covers the proprioception.

The useful summary for a fellow is that this ligament is mechanically simple and biologically awkward, and that the second half is where the unsolved problems are. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Key Learning Points

  • The anatomy of the first operation constrains the second.
  • Poor intrinsic healing is a property of the tissue.
  • That property is why the treatment arm is reconstruction.
  • Reports after sound reconstruction are often about confidence.
  • The ligament is mechanically simple and biologically awkward.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant question this page sits under is not what the ligament is but what its absence costs a particular patient, and the honest answer is that it depends on who they are.

A knee without this ligament is stable in a straight line and unreliable under rotation, so the cost is paid only by a person who rotates on it. 6.5.1 covers instability.

That makes the decision turn on the five things a trial population cannot carry. The patient's age and physiology, which set tissue quality and healing. Their medical comorbidities, because smoking, diabetes and body mass change operative risk and graft healing without changing the mechanics. The demands they put on the knee, which is occupation and sport rather than age. Their psychological state, because fear of reinjury governs what the knee is actually used for. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which decide whether the operation that is statistically right is the one this patient should have.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression. A reconstruction that is right for a cohort can be wrong for the person in front of you on any one of them. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The department level question is narrower and more answerable: whether its own results match what it tells patients, and whether anyone is checking. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • The cost of losing this ligament is paid by a person who rotates on the knee.
  • A straight line knee can be stable and still be unreliable.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations move the decision.

  • A reconstruction right for a cohort can be wrong for this patient.
  • The answerable departmental question is whether its results match its counsel.
