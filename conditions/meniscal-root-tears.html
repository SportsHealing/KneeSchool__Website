Meniscal Root Tears

Page 6.4.3 · ACL Associated Injuries · condition page

Detaching a root is functionally the same as removing the meniscus, which is why this is the associated injury with the longest shadow.

Meniscal Root Tears

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

Each meniscus is anchored to the shin bone at both ends. Those anchors are called roots. 1.2.3 covers the menisci.

The meniscus works by spreading load around a ring. If a root comes off, the ring cannot hold, and the meniscus slides out of the joint instead of cushioning it. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

So a torn root stops the whole meniscus working, even though the meniscus itself looks intact. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Investigations and Imaging

The clue is that the meniscus has moved out of position rather than that it looks torn. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Key Learning Points

  • Each meniscus is anchored at both ends by roots.
  • The meniscus spreads load around a ring.
  • If a root comes off, the ring cannot hold.
  • The meniscus slides out instead of cushioning.
  • The clue is that it has moved, not that it looks torn.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

A root tear detaches the meniscus from its bony anchor at the front or back of the shin bone. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

The meniscus works by converting the downward load into a hoop of tension around its ring. A detached root releases that hoop, so the meniscus is no longer doing its job. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Functionally, that is close to not having the meniscus at all, which is why this injury is taken seriously even when the meniscus itself looks normal. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Investigations and Imaging

The sign on the scan is that the meniscus has slipped outwards beyond the edge of the joint. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

That displacement, rather than the appearance of the tear itself, is what reports the problem. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

Management Overview

Repairing a root means reattaching it to the bone, and it is done to restore the hoop rather than to fix a tear. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

It is more worth doing in a younger knee with good cartilage than in a knee that has already lost its surface. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

When it accompanies a cruciate injury it is usually dealt with at the same operation. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • A root tear detaches the meniscus from its bony anchor.
  • The meniscus works by hoop tension around its ring.
  • A detached root releases the hoop.
  • Functionally that is close to not having the meniscus.
  • Displacement rather than the tear's appearance is the sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. The meniscus looks fine on the scan. Why is this serious? Because the meniscus works by being anchored at both ends. Released at one end, it can look entirely normal and do none of its job. The sign to look for is that it has moved, not that it looks damaged.

Q. Is repair always possible? No. It depends on the tissue, the position of the tear and the state of the cartilage. A repair into a joint whose surface has already worn is less likely to change what happens next.

Q. What happens if it is left? The load that the meniscus spread is carried by the cartilage directly. Over years that is the route to wear in that compartment, which is why age and cartilage condition matter so much to the decision.

Q. Does repairing it change my recovery? Yes. Root repairs usually carry restrictions on weight and bending for several weeks, more than a cruciate reconstruction alone. Your surgeon will set what applies.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

A root tear is a radial tear or avulsion within the meniscal root attachment, disrupting circumferential hoop tension. 2.5.7 covers the root attachments.

Loss of hoop tension converts the meniscus from a load distributor into a passive structure, which is biomechanically similar to meniscectomy. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Lateral root tears are strongly associated with acute cruciate injury; medial root tears are more often degenerative and present differently. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Investigations and Imaging

Meniscal extrusion beyond the tibial margin is the cardinal finding. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

The ghost sign, where the meniscus is absent from its expected position on a sagittal image, supports it. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

The tear itself can be inconspicuous, so a report that describes only the meniscal body may miss it. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Management Overview

Repair restores continuity of the root and aims to restore hoop tension. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

It is favoured in younger knees with preserved cartilage and correctable alignment. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Key Learning Points

  • A root tear disrupts circumferential hoop tension.
  • Loss of hoop tension is biomechanically similar to meniscectomy.
  • Lateral root tears associate with acute cruciate injury.
  • Extrusion beyond the tibial margin is the cardinal finding.
  • The tear itself can be inconspicuous on the report.

MRCS Level

Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer leads with the biomechanics rather than the appearance.

The expected points are the hoop tension mechanism, the equivalence to meniscectomy, extrusion as the sign, the lateral association with cruciate injury, and the case for repair. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Say that an intact looking meniscus with extrusion is a root problem until shown otherwise. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Say that the finding changes the operation rather than adding to the report. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

Management Overview

Repair at the same sitting as the cruciate reconstruction where both are present, because the repair needs a stable knee. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Alignment is assessed before a medial root repair, since uncorrected varus loads the repair it is meant to protect. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Complications and Prognosis

A root repair that fails returns the compartment to its unprotected state, which is where it was before. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The compartment's cartilage at the time of repair predicts the result more than the repair technique does. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • Lead with the biomechanics rather than the appearance.
  • An intact looking meniscus with extrusion is a root problem until shown otherwise.
  • The finding changes the operation rather than adding to the report.
  • Repair needs a stable knee, so both are done together.
  • The cartilage at repair predicts the result more than the technique.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to select for root repair rather than to describe it.

A lateral root tear with an acute cruciate injury in a young knee is the clearest indication, because the compartment is otherwise healthy and the knee is being operated on anyway. 6.2.4 covers combined injuries.

A medial root tear in an older varus knee with established cartilage loss is the clearest non-indication for repair alone, and raises realignment instead. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Between those, cartilage status and alignment decide, and both are assessed before the decision rather than during the operation. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Complications and Prognosis

Persistent extrusion after a technically satisfactory repair is a recognised outcome and means the hoop was not restored. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

That is the reason a healed looking repair does not guarantee a protected compartment. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether root repair alters the development of compartmental arthritis. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The second is whether persistent extrusion after repair negates the benefit. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

The third is the upper age and cartilage threshold beyond which repair stops being worthwhile. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Select for repair rather than describe it.
  • A lateral root tear with acute cruciate injury in a young knee is the clearest

indication.

  • A medial root tear in an older varus knee raises realignment instead.
  • Cartilage status and alignment decide, assessed before the operation.
  • Persistent extrusion after repair means the hoop was not restored.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the root tear is where this group's long shadow is cast, because it is the associated injury whose consequence is measured in decades.

Everything else in this chapter either resolves, is repaired, or is replaced. The compartment that loses its meniscus does none of those. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

That makes the root the structure where the argument for acting early is strongest and the evidence that acting helps is weakest, which is an uncomfortable combination and should be stated as one. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

A fellow is likely to see both errors: a root tear left in a young knee because it was not the presenting problem, and a root repaired in a worn compartment where it could not have helped. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The discriminator is the cartilage, assessed deliberately rather than inferred from age, and it is the same discriminator the cartilage page reaches independently. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The root's consequence is measured in decades.
  • Everything else here resolves, is repaired or is replaced.
  • The argument for acting early is strongest where the evidence is weakest.
  • Both errors are common: left in a young knee, repaired in a worn one.
  • The discriminator is the cartilage, assessed rather than inferred from age.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is counselling about a benefit that arrives, if at all, long after the patient has stopped attending.

A root repair asks a patient for a harder rehabilitation now in exchange for a compartment that may last longer in twenty years, and that is a difficult trade to present honestly without either overselling or dismissing it. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Overselling it is the commoner error, because the biomechanical argument is compelling and the outcome evidence is not yet. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

The five things decide. The patient's age and physiology, which here directly set how many years the compartment must serve and therefore the size of the prize. Their medical comorbidities, particularly body mass, which changes the load the compartment carries whatever is repaired. The demands they put on the knee. Their psychological state, because a restricted rehabilitation for a distant benefit tests motivation. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, including how they weigh a probable inconvenience now against a possible one later.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and say which part of the argument is mechanical and which is evidenced. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The benefit arrives long after the patient stops attending.
  • A harder rehabilitation now against a compartment that may last longer later.
  • Overselling is the commoner error, because the mechanics are compelling.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide.

  • Say which part of the argument is mechanical and which is evidenced.
