Graft Failure

Page 6.8.1 · Revision ACL Problems · condition page

A failed reconstruction is a question rather than a diagnosis, and the question is why, because the answer decides everything that follows.

Graft Failure

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

A reconstruction replaces the torn ligament with a graft, which is a piece of tendon used to do the same job. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Sometimes the new ligament stops working. The knee then gives way again, the way it did before the operation. 6.5.1 covers instability.

How It Presents

It can happen because of a new injury, because the graft was placed in the wrong position, or because it never became strong. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Working out which of those happened is the first job, because the three need different answers. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Key Learning Points

  • A graft is a piece of tendon doing the ligament's job.
  • Sometimes the new ligament stops working.
  • The knee then gives way again.
  • A new injury, a wrong position or a graft that never got strong.
  • Working out which one happened comes first.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Graft failure means the reconstructed ligament is no longer doing its job, and the knee is unstable again. 6.5.1 covers instability.

That is different from a knee that is painful or stiff after a reconstruction, which is a separate problem with separate causes. 4.10.12 covers the revision ACL assessment.

It is also different from disappointment, where the graft is intact and working and the knee has not returned to what the patient hoped for. Both are real and they are not the same thing. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Causes and Risk Factors

Three groups of cause are described. A further injury, usually a pivot in sport. A technical reason, most often the position of the tunnels. And a biological reason, where the graft never properly became a ligament. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Timing gives a clue. Failure within the first months points towards biology or technique, and failure at eighteen months with a clear injury points towards trauma. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Management Overview

The first step is identifying which cause applies, because a revision that repeats the original error fails the same way. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

That assessment includes the original operation note, imaging of the tunnels, examination, and a conversation about what the knee was doing when it gave way. 5.7.14 covers revision ACL imaging.

Revision surgery is more complex than the first operation, sometimes needs two stages, and has less reliable results, all of which belongs in the first conversation. 4.20.9 covers revision knee surgery.

Key Learning Points

  • Graft failure means the knee is unstable again.
  • Pain, stiffness and disappointment are separate problems.
  • Trauma, technique and biology are the three groups of cause.
  • Timing gives a clue to which applies.
  • A revision that repeats the original error fails the same way.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does a failed graft mean the operation was done badly? No. Technique is one of three groups of cause, and the other two are a new injury and a biological failure to incorporate. Establishing which applies is the point of the assessment.

Q. Can the same thing be done again? Usually something can be done, and it is often not the same operation. Tunnel position, bone quality and graft availability decide, and sometimes the work is staged.

Q. Are the results as good? Reported results after revision are less good than after a first reconstruction. This page carries no figures, because none has been verified here.

Q. Why does the old operation note matter so much? Because it records the graft used, the tunnel positions and the fixation, and all three change what is possible now. A revision planned without it is planned with less information.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

Graft failure is recurrent symptomatic instability with a graft that is not performing its restraining function. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The useful distinction is between graft rupture, which is a structural finding, and clinical failure, which is a functional one. They overlap and they are not synonyms. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

A third category, failure to meet expectation with an intact graft, is common and is not graft failure. 4.23.5 covers revision surgery patients.

Causes and Risk Factors

The traditional classification is traumatic, technical and biological. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Technical causes include tunnel malposition, inadequate fixation, graft tensioning errors and unaddressed associated injury, with femoral tunnel position the most cited. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

Biological causes include failure of incorporation and remodelling, and they are largely a diagnosis of exclusion. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Traumatic causes are a new pivotal injury, and the risk is highest in young athletes returning to pivoting sport. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Unaddressed secondary restraints and uncorrected malalignment belong in the technical group even though they are not errors of drilling. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Examination

The examination establishes whether the knee is lax, in which direction, and whether the rotational component is high. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

A high grade pivot shift after reconstruction raises the question of anterolateral insufficiency or of a non-anatomical graft. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Posterior and varus examination matters here more than in a primary case, since an unrecognised secondary deficiency is a recognised reason for failure. 4.10.12 covers the revision ACL assessment.

Investigations and Imaging

Radiographs show tunnel position, hardware, bone quality and alignment. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

MRI shows the graft and the menisci, and it is less reliable for a graft than for a native ligament. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

Computed tomography is the test that quantifies tunnel position and widening and is what a revision plan is built from. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Key Learning Points

  • Graft rupture is structural and clinical failure is functional.
  • An intact graft that disappointed is not graft failure.
  • Traumatic, technical and biological is the classification.
  • Femoral tunnel position is the most cited technical cause.
  • Computed tomography quantifies the tunnel position a plan needs.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer examines for the cause rather than for the diagnosis.

Say Lachman and pivot shift, then say that you are also examining the posterior, medial, lateral and rotational restraints, because a missed secondary deficiency is why the first graft failed in some cases. 4.10.12 covers the revision ACL assessment.

Say that alignment is assessed standing, because varus in a deficient knee loads the graft. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Investigations and Imaging

Say radiographs for tunnels, hardware and alignment; long leg films if the standing assessment raises a question; computed tomography for tunnel position and width; MRI for the graft, menisci and surfaces. 5.7.14 covers revision ACL imaging.

Say that the operation note is an investigation, which is the answer examiners are listening for. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Management Overview

State the sequence: establish the cause, correct what caused it, then revise. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

State that a single stage revision needs usable tunnel positions and adequate bone, and that widened or malpositioned tunnels may require grafting and a staged approach. 4.20.9 covers revision knee surgery.

State that malalignment or an untreated secondary restraint is addressed in the plan rather than after it. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Key Learning Points

  • Examine for the cause rather than for the diagnosis.
  • A missed secondary deficiency is a recognised reason for failure.
  • The operation note is an investigation.
  • Establish the cause, correct it, then revise.
  • Widened or malpositioned tunnels may require grafting and staging.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is given a failed reconstruction and asked to plan, and the plan is marked on whether the cause was established first.

The defensible sequence is cause, correction, revision, with the decision between one and two stages made from tunnel position, tunnel width and bone quality rather than from preference. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Graft choice follows availability and what the previous operation used, and the contralateral limb is a legitimate source that has its own cost. 3.7.17 covers hamstring graft biomechanics.

The question that separates candidates is what they would do if the tunnels are correct, the restraints are intact, the alignment is normal and the graft still failed, since that is the biological case. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Complications and Prognosis

Revision results are reported as inferior to primary results on every outcome measure, and the meniscal and chondral state at revision explains much of the difference. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The honest prognosis statement separates stability, which revision usually restores, from function and from joint outcome, which it less reliably changes. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether staged bone grafting is required as often as it is performed. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

The second is whether adding a lateral extra-articular procedure at revision reduces further failure enough to justify it routinely. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

The third is how much of the reported revision failure rate is return to sport behaviour rather than surgical. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Key Learning Points

  • The plan is marked on whether the cause was established first.
  • One or two stages is decided by tunnel position, width and bone quality.
  • The biological case is the question that separates candidates.
  • Revision restores stability more reliably than function or joint outcome.
  • Meniscal and chondral state explains much of the difference.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level most of these knees arrive from elsewhere, and the recurring obstacle is that nobody can say what the first operation did.

A note recording ACL reconstruction with hamstring graft leaves tunnel position, fixation and tensioning unstated, and all three change the revision plan. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

The habit worth keeping is to write the primary note as though a revision surgeon will read it: graft and diameter, femoral tunnel position described anatomically, tibial tunnel position, fixation type and depth, and the state of the menisci and surfaces. Five items. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The second habit is to record the cause assigned at revision, because the literature's technical, biological and traumatic proportions are built from assignments that were mostly never written down. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

The third is to record what the patient returned to, since a failure in a knee that returned to elite pivoting is a different event from one in a knee that failed walking. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Key Learning Points

  • Most of these knees arrive with the first operation undocumented.
  • Hamstring graft leaves position, fixation and tensioning unstated.
  • Write the primary note for the revision surgeon who will read it.
  • Record the cause assigned at revision.
  • Record what the patient actually returned to.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that assigning a cause involves judging a colleague's operation, often on imaging alone, and the judgement then goes into a letter the patient reads. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

A tunnel described as malpositioned carries an implication that the patient hears whether or not it was intended, and the accurate statement is usually that the position differs from current practice rather than that an error occurred. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which set whether a second graft is the last one they will need. Their medical comorbidities, including smoking and metabolic factors that bear on incorporation. The demands they put on the knee, which caused the first failure in a proportion of cases and will be unchanged unless discussed. Their psychological state, because a second failure is received very differently from a first. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which commonly include an explanation already assigned to the first surgeon.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and say what the second operation can and cannot restore before choosing it. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Key Learning Points

  • Assigning a cause means judging a colleague's operation in a letter.
  • Differs from current practice is usually the accurate statement.
  • Demand caused the first failure in a proportion of cases.
  • A second failure is received very differently from a first.
  • Say what the second operation cannot restore before choosing it.
