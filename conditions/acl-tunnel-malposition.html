Tunnel Malposition

Page 6.8.2 · Revision ACL Problems · condition page

The commonest technical cause of failure, and the one that has to be corrected rather than worked around.

Tunnel Malposition

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

To hold a graft in place, the surgeon drills a tunnel in each bone and passes the graft through them. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Where those tunnels sit decides how the graft behaves. If a tunnel is in the wrong place, the graft is the wrong length at the wrong time. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

How It Presents

The knee can give way again, or it can feel tight and refuse to bend or straighten fully. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Both are signs that the graft is being asked to do its job from the wrong starting point. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Key Learning Points

  • The graft passes through a tunnel drilled in each bone.
  • Where the tunnels sit decides how the graft behaves.
  • A wrong position makes the graft the wrong length at the wrong time.
  • The knee can give way or feel tight.
  • Both point at the starting point rather than the graft.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

The graft runs between a tunnel in the thigh bone and a tunnel in the shin bone. Those two positions set how the graft tightens and slackens as the knee bends. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Malposition means a tunnel sits away from the position the original ligament occupied, and the consequence is predictable rather than random. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Causes and Risk Factors

The most described error places the thigh bone tunnel too far forward, which makes the graft tighten as the knee bends and either stretches it out or limits bending. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

A tunnel that is too vertical controls the forward slide but not the rotation, so the knee can test well on one examination and still give way on a turn. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

Management Overview

Identifying it needs imaging that measures position rather than one that shows the graft. 5.7.14 covers revision ACL imaging.

If a revision is planned, the old tunnel either can be avoided, can be used, or has to be filled with bone and left to heal before a new one is drilled. That last route is why some revisions are done in two stages. 4.20.9 covers revision knee surgery.

The reason the position matters more than anything else on this page is that it is the one cause of failure that is fully correctable. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Key Learning Points

  • Tunnel positions set how the graft tightens as the knee bends.
  • Malposition has a predictable consequence rather than a random one.
  • A tunnel too far forward tightens in flexion.
  • A vertical tunnel controls sliding but not rotation.
  • It is the one cause of failure that is fully correctable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How is tunnel position measured? With imaging that quantifies it rather than imaging that shows the graft, which in practice means computed tomography and specific radiographic views.

Q. Why would a revision need two stages? Because a tunnel that is both malpositioned and widened leaves no sound bone for a new one. Filling it with bone graft and allowing it to heal creates that bone, and that takes months.

Q. Does a vertical graft always fail? No. It describes a position that controls rotation less well, and whether that becomes failure depends on what the knee is asked to do.

Q. Was my first operation wrong? Position differing from current practice is not the same as an error. Accepted positions have changed over time, and the useful question now is what the current position allows.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

Tunnel position is described relative to the native femoral and tibial attachments, and malposition means a departure from them large enough to change graft behaviour. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

The femoral tunnel is the one that carries most of the consequence, because the femoral attachment is where the ligament's length pattern through flexion is set. 3.7.8 covers the anterior stability.

Causes and Risk Factors

An anterior femoral tunnel produces a graft that tightens in flexion, giving either loss of flexion or graft elongation. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

A vertical femoral tunnel, classically from a transtibial drilling constraint, controls anterior translation and under-controls rotation, which presents as a persistent pivot shift with an acceptable Lachman. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

A posterior tibial tunnel risks impingement and a graft that tightens in extension. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

Tunnel widening is a separate phenomenon that interacts with position, since a widened tunnel removes the bone a corrected tunnel would use. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Investigations and Imaging

Radiographs including a tunnel view show position in two planes and show hardware. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

Computed tomography quantifies position and width in three dimensions and is what a revision plan uses. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

MRI shows the graft and the impingement and is not the measurement tool. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • Malposition is a departure large enough to change graft behaviour.
  • The femoral tunnel carries most of the consequence.
  • An anterior femoral tunnel tightens the graft in flexion.
  • A vertical tunnel under-controls rotation with an acceptable Lachman.
  • Computed tomography is the measurement and MRI is not.

MRCS Level

Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer names the measurement test and the reason for it.

Say radiographs with a tunnel view for position and hardware, and computed tomography for three dimensional position and width. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

Say that the plan depends on whether the existing tunnel can be avoided, reused, or must be grafted, and that only a quantified assessment answers that. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Management Overview

State the three routes: a new independent tunnel where the old position allows it, reuse where the position is acceptable, and staged bone grafting where it is neither. 4.20.9 covers revision knee surgery.

State that hardware removal may be required and may itself dictate staging. 5.7.14 covers revision ACL imaging.

State that an unaddressed secondary restraint or malalignment is corrected in the same plan, because a correctly placed graft in a varus or rotationally deficient knee fails again. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Complications and Prognosis

A graft placed through a corrected tunnel in an otherwise addressed knee is the best situation in the revision chapter, which is worth saying because this is the correctable cause. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The cost is time: a staged revision means two operations and months between them, during which the knee is still unstable. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • Name the measurement test and the reason for it.
  • Avoid, reuse, or graft and stage are the three routes.
  • Hardware removal may dictate staging.
  • Correct the secondary restraint and the alignment in the same plan.
  • The cost of staging is months of continuing instability.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is shown tunnel imaging and asked for a plan, and the mark is in the staging decision and its justification.

The defensible position states the measured position, the width, the bone available, and only then the number of stages. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Where the knee is unstable and a stage is being added, the meniscal cost of the interval belongs in the discussion, because months of instability is not a neutral wait. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Where the old position is acceptable by current standards, reusing it is not a compromise and saying so avoids an unnecessary stage. 4.20.9 covers revision knee surgery.

Complications and Prognosis

The specific trap is treating malposition as the whole answer. A malpositioned tunnel in a knee that also has an unaddressed lateral deficiency, varus alignment or a destroyed meniscus will be corrected and still disappoint. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

The prognosis statement that holds up names which of those have been addressed and which have not. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is what magnitude of deviation from the native position actually matters clinically, since the measurement is far more precise than the evidence. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

The second is whether staged bone grafting is required as often as it is performed. 4.20.9 covers revision knee surgery.

The third is whether tunnel widening is a biological process, a mechanical one, or both, which bears directly on whether it recurs after grafting. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • State position, width and available bone before stating the number of stages.
  • Months of instability between stages is not a neutral wait.
  • An acceptable old position can be reused without compromise.
  • Malposition is not the whole answer in a knee with other deficiencies.
  • The measurement is more precise than the evidence behind it.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the measurement is easy and the attribution is not, because a tunnel measured today was drilled under a different standard.

Positions accepted in earlier practice are now described as non-anatomical, and calling such a tunnel malpositioned reports a comparison as a fault. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

The habit worth keeping is to record the measurement, the reference used, and the era of the primary operation, so that the next reader can see a comparison rather than a verdict. 5.7.14 covers revision ACL imaging.

The second habit is to state in the plan what the tunnel position explains and what it does not, since a knee with a vertical graft and a destroyed meniscus has two problems and only one of them is being corrected. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The third is to keep the intra-operative imaging from the revision, because the next surgeon after you will be in the same position you are in now. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • The measurement is easy and the attribution is not.
  • A tunnel drilled under a different standard is a comparison, not a fault.
  • Record the measurement, the reference and the era of the primary operation.
  • State what the position explains and what it does not.
  • Keep the revision's intra-operative imaging.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that this page's finding is attributable, nameable and written into a letter, which makes it the one part of the revision assessment with a professional consequence attached. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

The position that holds up describes position, states the standard used, and avoids the word error unless the position was outside accepted practice at the time it was drilled. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which bear on whether a staged revision is a reasonable use of a year of their life. Their medical comorbidities, which bear on graft incorporation and on two anaesthetics rather than one. The demands they put on the knee, which decide whether the rotational deficiency a vertical tunnel leaves actually matters to them. Their psychological state, because a patient told the first operation was misplaced reinterprets everything that followed it. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which in this chapter frequently arrive as a second opinion already obtained.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and separate the measurement from the attribution when you say it out loud. 6.8.3 covers biological failure.

Key Learning Points

  • This is the revision finding with a professional consequence attached.
  • Describe position, state the standard, and avoid the word error.
  • A staged revision costs a year of the patient's life.
  • Whether a rotational deficiency matters depends on demand.
  • Separate the measurement from the attribution when saying it aloud.
