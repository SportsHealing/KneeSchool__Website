Osteoarthritis

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

Osteoarthritis is what a joint looks like when the smooth surface has worn away and the bones underneath have changed shape in response. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

It takes a long time. A knee injured in a teenager's sport may not show it until that person is middle aged. 6.5.1 covers instability.

How It Presents

Pain with use, stiffness after sitting still, swelling after a long day, and a knee that gradually does less than it used to. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • Osteoarthritis is a worn surface and a changed bone underneath.
  • It develops over many years.
  • A teenage injury may show up in middle age.
  • Pain comes with use and stiffness comes after rest.
  • The knee gradually does less than it did.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Osteoarthritis after a knee injury is called post-traumatic osteoarthritis. It is the same end state as the osteoarthritis that comes with age, reached earlier and for a reason that can be named. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The reason, in this chapter, is the sequence the previous three pages describe. Instability tears meniscus, meniscal loss concentrates load, concentrated load wears the surface, and the worn surface is what a radiograph eventually shows. 6.5.1 covers instability.

How It Presents

Pain related to load rather than to rest, stiffness that eases with movement, swelling after use, and a slow narrowing of what the knee is willing to do. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Radiographic change and symptoms do not arrive together. People live with abnormal films and no complaint, and others complain with films that look better than they feel. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

Management Overview

Management is described here rather than advised, and the detail belongs with the clinicians who provide it.

The broad shape is load management, strength, weight where it is relevant, and procedures that realign or replace the joint when the symptoms justify them. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

What this page can say usefully is what does not work: waiting for a film to decide, and expecting a reconstruction performed decades earlier to have prevented it. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Key Learning Points

  • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is the same end state reached earlier.
  • The sequence runs instability, meniscal loss, load concentration, wear.
  • Films and symptoms do not arrive together.
  • Management is load, strength, weight, and surgery when symptoms justify it.
  • Waiting for a film to decide does not work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does an ACL tear mean I will get arthritis? It raises the risk and it does not decide the outcome. The things that carry most of that risk are how much meniscus you keep and how often the knee gives way, and both of those are partly within reach.

Q. Will a reconstruction prevent it? It reduces the episodes that damage the meniscus, which is the mechanism. Whether that shows up as less arthritis forty years later has not been demonstrated, and this page does not claim it.

Q. My film shows arthritis but my knee feels fine. What does that mean? That the two measure different things. The film records structure and your symptoms record function. Treatment follows the symptoms.

Q. How long does it take to develop? Typically decades rather than years, which is the practical difficulty with the whole subject. The outcome arrives long after the decisions that influenced it.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

Osteoarthritis is a whole joint condition involving cartilage loss, subchondral bone change, osteophyte formation and synovial involvement, rather than cartilage loss alone. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is the subset with an identifiable injury preceding it, and the knee after an ACL injury is its commonest example in young adults. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Who Gets It

The honest position is that this page carries no incidence or prevalence quantities, because none has been verified in this environment and an invented figure is worse than an absent one. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

What can be stated without a number is the shape: risk rises with meniscal resection, rises with repeated instability, rises with malalignment, and rises with time from injury. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

A reviewer with the literature can place the quantities into this section without changing anything else on the page. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Causes and Risk Factors

Meniscal status is the strongest modifiable factor in this chapter, which is why the meniscus page is its hinge. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Alignment decides which compartment carries the load and therefore where the change appears. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Chondral injury at the index event, repeated subluxation, body weight and time make up the rest. 6.4.6 covers cartilage injuries.

Investigations and Imaging

Weight bearing radiographs are the standard assessment, because the narrowing that defines it is a loaded finding. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

MRI shows the surface and the bone earlier than the radiograph, and it is not the test that defines the diagnosis. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

Long leg films are the ones that answer the alignment question, and the short film cannot. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Key Learning Points

  • Osteoarthritis is a whole joint condition rather than cartilage loss alone.
  • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis has an identifiable injury before it.
  • This page states the shape of the risk and carries no verified quantities.
  • Meniscal status is the strongest modifiable factor.
  • Weight bearing films define it and long leg films answer alignment.

MRCS Level

Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer states the loaded film first and resists reaching for an MRI.

Say that the diagnosis is clinical and radiographic, that the film must be weight bearing, and that a long leg film is added where alignment is in question. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Say that MRI adds information about the surface and the menisci and does not make the diagnosis. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

Management Overview

The order is symptoms, function, alignment, then procedure. A film does not appear in that list, which is the point being tested. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Osteotomy addresses a malaligned compartment in a younger patient and arthroplasty addresses the joint when the symptoms warrant it. Both belong to the surgical academy for technique. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Complications and Prognosis

This is where the chapter ends rather than where it becomes treatable, and the examination expects the candidate to connect it back. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The connection is that every decision on the three previous pages was made to delay this one, and none of them was made with evidence about this one. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • State the loaded film first and resist reaching for an MRI.
  • A long leg film is added where alignment is in question.
  • The order is symptoms, function, alignment, procedure.
  • A film does not appear in that order.
  • Earlier decisions were made to delay this page without evidence about it.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is given a young adult with an old ACL tear, a previous meniscectomy and early unicompartmental change, and asked what to offer.

The answer that holds up separates the symptom from the structure. If the knee gives way, that is an instability problem in an arthritic knee. If it aches on load, that is an arthritis problem in an unstable knee. The two lead different ways. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Alignment is the question that turns the case, because a malaligned unicompartmental knee in a young patient is the one situation on this page where an operation addresses the mechanism rather than the symptom. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Reconstruction alone in an aching, well aligned, stable knee treats nothing the patient came with. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Complications and Prognosis

The prognosis conversation is harder than the decision, because the honest version contains no date and the patient came for one. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Progression is variable, poorly predicted individually, and influenced by variables the patient controls more than by the ones the surgeon does. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether reconstruction reduces the incidence of post-traumatic osteoarthritis or only changes its timing and its symptoms. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The second is whether meniscal repair, pursued harder and in more marginal tears, would change joint outcome enough to justify the failure rate it carries. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

The third is whether early osteotomy in the young malaligned deficient knee alters the natural history or only moves the arthroplasty later. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Key Learning Points

  • Separate the symptom from the structure in the first sentence.
  • Giving way and aching on load lead different ways.
  • Alignment is the question that turns the case.
  • Reconstruction in an aching, stable, aligned knee treats nothing.
  • The honest prognosis contains no date and the patient came for one.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level this page is the one that exposes how little anybody records across a timescale that matters.

The decisions sit decades before the outcome, the patients move practice, and the knee arrives in somebody else's clinic with no account of what was taken out of it. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The practical consequence is that meniscal status at every operation is the most valuable thing a surgeon can write down for a colleague who does not exist yet. How much was resected, from where, and whether a repair held. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

The second consequence is that alignment should be measured and recorded once, properly, on a long leg film, rather than estimated repeatedly from short films. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

Neither habit helps the patient in front of you, which is exactly why both get skipped. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Key Learning Points

  • The decisions sit decades before the outcome.
  • Knees arrive with no account of what was taken out of them.
  • Record how much meniscus was resected, from where, and whether repair held.
  • Measure and record alignment once, properly, on a long leg film.
  • Neither habit helps the patient in front of you, which is why both get skipped.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is the timescale. The decision is made in a knee that has decades left, the outcome arrives after the surgeon has stopped practising, and nobody involved will ever learn whether the choice was right. 6.5.1 covers instability.

That argues for stating the mechanism and declining the prediction. The links in the chain are defensible one at a time. The forecast built out of them is not. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which decide how many decades of loading the joint has to survive and therefore how much this page matters to them. Their medical comorbidities, body weight among them, since it acts on the joint every step rather than once in theatre. The demands they put on the knee, which they will change several times over the interval in question. Their psychological state, because a long horizon invites both fatalism and false reassurance. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which usually contain a specific fear of a replacement and a vague idea of prevention.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and answer the fear they brought rather than the question they asked. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Key Learning Points

  • The outcome arrives after the surgeon has stopped practising.
  • State the mechanism and decline the prediction.
  • The links are defensible individually and the forecast is not.
  • Body weight acts on the joint every step rather than once in theatre.
  • Answer the fear they brought rather than the question they asked.
