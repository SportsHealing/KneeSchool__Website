Meniscal Damage

The hinge of the whole chapter: it is what instability costs, and it is what makes the loss permanent.

Meniscal Damage

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

The menisci are two rubbery cushions inside the knee that spread the load between the bones. 1.2.3 covers the menisci.

In a knee whose ACL does not work, the bones shift during turns. Those shifts can tear a meniscus. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A torn meniscus does not grow back, and a meniscus that is removed is gone for good. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

How It Presents

The knee can catch, lock, or refuse to straighten fully. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • The menisci are cushions that spread load between the bones.
  • Shifts during turns can tear them.
  • A torn meniscus does not grow back.
  • A removed meniscus is gone for good.
  • Catching or locking is the usual sign.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

The menisci distribute load across the joint surfaces. Losing meniscal tissue concentrates that load onto a smaller area of cartilage. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

In a knee without a working ACL, each episode of giving way can tear the meniscus, most often the back of the inner one. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

How It Presents

Catching, locking, or a knee that will not fully straighten are mechanical symptoms and point at the meniscus rather than the ligament. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Swelling after an episode is another clue that something inside was injured. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Management Overview

A meniscus that can be repaired should be, because preserving it protects the joint. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Repairability falls with repeated tearing and with time, which is the reason instability is treated rather than tolerated. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Where repair is not possible, the torn part is trimmed, and that is a loss rather than a fix. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Key Learning Points

  • The menisci distribute load across the joint surfaces.
  • Losing tissue concentrates load on less cartilage.
  • Each episode of giving way can tear a meniscus.
  • Repairability falls with repeated tearing and with time.
  • Trimming is a loss rather than a fix.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Why is the meniscus more important than the ligament? Because the ligament can be replaced and the meniscus cannot. A reconstruction gives you a new ligament. Nothing gives you back meniscal tissue, and it is what protects the cartilage.

Q. Can a torn meniscus be repaired? Sometimes, depending on where the tear is, how long it has been there and the quality of the tissue. The outer part has a blood supply and heals better; the inner part has none.

Q. If I wait, does it get worse? It can, if the knee gives way in the meantime. A tear that was repairable can become one that is not. That is the argument for acting while the knee is still protecting its own tissue.

Q. What happens if the meniscus is removed? The cartilage in that compartment carries more load. That raises the risk of wear over years. It does not mean it will certainly happen, and it is the reason preservation is preferred wherever it is possible.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The menisci convert axial load into circumferential hoop tension, distributing force and reducing contact stress on articular cartilage. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

In the chronically cruciate deficient knee, repeated anterior subluxation shears the posterior horn of the medial meniscus, which acts as a secondary restraint. 3.7.3 covers the secondary restraints.

Secondary meniscal tearing is therefore the characteristic lesion of chronic deficiency rather than of the index injury. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

How It Presents

Mechanical symptoms, including locking and a block to extension, indicate a displaced fragment. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Examination

Joint line tenderness and provocative meniscal tests support the diagnosis and are less reliable in a knee that is also unstable. 4.10.3 covers chronic ACL deficiency.

Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging identifies tear pattern and location, and extrusion indicates root involvement. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Management Overview

Repair where the tear pattern, location and tissue permit. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Reconstruct the cruciate alongside, because a repair in an unstable knee is being asked to heal in the environment that tore it. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • The menisci convert axial load into hoop tension.
  • Repeated subluxation shears the medial posterior horn.
  • Secondary tearing characterises chronic deficiency.
  • Mechanical symptoms indicate a displaced fragment.
  • A repair in an unstable knee heals in the environment that tore it.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer names the meniscus as the thing being protected.

The expected points are the hoop tension mechanism, the medial posterior horn's restraint role, secondary tearing as the characteristic lesion, the falling repairability, and the need to address instability alongside. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Say that the meniscus is why chronic deficiency is treated rather than observed. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Say that repairability is time dependent, which converts the decision into one with a deadline. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Management Overview

Preserve wherever possible; resect only what cannot be preserved. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Address the instability at the same operation. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Complications and Prognosis

Meniscal loss is the strongest modifiable predictor of later compartmental degeneration in this group. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

A repair that fails returns the knee to where it was and has cost a rehabilitation, which is an acceptable trade in a young knee and less so in an old one. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Name the meniscus as the thing being protected.
  • It is why chronic deficiency is treated rather than observed.
  • Repairability is time dependent and gives the decision a deadline.
  • Preserve wherever possible and resect only what cannot be preserved.
  • Meniscal loss is the strongest modifiable predictor of degeneration here.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level this is where the candidate shows whether they are treating a ligament or a joint.

Every decision in chronic deficiency is justified by what it does for the meniscus, and a candidate who argues reconstruction on laxity alone has missed the argument. 6.5.1 covers instability.

A knee with an intact meniscus and continued instability is the clearest indication for reconstruction in this chapter. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

A knee that has already lost both menisci and its cartilage is a different problem, and reconstructing it restores stability to a joint whose damage is done. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Complications and Prognosis

Repair in a chronically deficient knee has a lower success rate than in an acute one, because the tissue has been tearing repeatedly. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

That difference is itself an argument for not waiting. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether meniscal preservation in this setting changes long term radiographic outcome. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

The second is the threshold of cartilage loss beyond which preservation stops being worthwhile. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The third is whether repair of a degenerate posterior horn in a chronic knee outperforms selective resection. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Every decision here is justified by what it does for the meniscus.
  • Arguing reconstruction on laxity alone misses the argument.
  • Intact meniscus with continued instability is the clearest indication.
  • Repair in a chronic knee succeeds less often than in an acute one.
  • That difference is itself an argument for not waiting.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the meniscus is where this group's time pressure actually lives, and it is not where patients think it is.

Patients believe the urgency is about the ligament, which does not deteriorate, and the urgency is about tissue that does. 6.5.1 covers instability.

That mismatch produces the characteristic late presentation: a patient who waited sensibly, whose knee gave way a handful of times, and whose meniscus is now beyond repair. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Nothing in the subsequent operation recovers what those episodes cost, and the reconstruction they then receive is a good operation arriving after the thing it was protecting has gone. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The sentence worth saying early is that the clock is on the meniscus and not on the ligament, because patients can act on that and cannot act on a laxity grade. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The time pressure is on tissue that deteriorates, not on the ligament.
  • Patients believe the urgency is about the ligament.
  • The late presentation is a sensible wait and a meniscus beyond repair.
  • No later operation recovers what the episodes cost.
  • Say early that the clock is on the meniscus.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant problem is conveying a deadline without manufacturing alarm.

Saying there is no rush is wrong, because repairability falls. Saying it is urgent is wrong too, because a stable knee is under no pressure at all. 6.5.1 covers instability.

The accurate version is conditional and therefore harder to deliver: there is no deadline while the knee is stable, and a deadline starts at the first episode of giving way. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The five things set how hard to press. The patient's age and physiology, because a young meniscus is worth more years and repairs better. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which decide how soon the first episode is likely. Their psychological state, because a conditional deadline is harder to act on than a fixed one. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which often include a belief that waiting is free.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and give them the condition to watch for rather than a date. 6.5.2 is this page, and 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Key Learning Points

  • Saying there is no rush is wrong because repairability falls.
  • Saying it is urgent is wrong because a stable knee is under no pressure.
  • The accurate version is conditional and harder to deliver.
  • Patients often believe waiting is free.
  • Give them the condition to watch for rather than a date.
