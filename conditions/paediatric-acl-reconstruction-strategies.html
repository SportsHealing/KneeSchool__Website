Reconstruction Strategies

Page 6.7.3 · Paediatric ACL Injuries · condition page

A decision about where the graft is allowed to pass rather than a choice of operation, and the technique itself belongs to the surgical academy.

Reconstruction Strategies

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

To make a new ligament, a surgeon needs to pass a graft through the knee from the thigh bone to the shin bone. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

In a growing knee the growth plates are in the way, so the route has to be chosen with them in mind. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

Management Overview

There are several routes. Some go around the growth plates, some pass straight through them with soft tissue only, and the choice depends on how much growing is left. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

The one thing avoided in a young knee is placing anything hard across a growth plate. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • A graft runs from the thigh bone to the shin bone.
  • The growth plates are in the way in a growing knee.
  • Some routes go around them and some pass through them.
  • The choice depends on how much growing is left.
  • Nothing hard is placed across a growth plate.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Reconstruction strategy in a growing child means the route the graft takes relative to the growth plates, and that is what distinguishes the paediatric operation from the adult one. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

The strategies described are physeal sparing, where the graft avoids the growth plates; all-epiphyseal, where tunnels stay within the bone ends; partial transphyseal; and transphyseal, where the tunnels cross as in an adult. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Management Overview

Skeletal age selects between them. A child with a great deal of growth left is usually offered a strategy that avoids the plates, and an adolescent near maturity is usually offered the adult operation. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Graft choice follows the same logic. Soft tissue grafts are generally preferred in a growing knee because a bone block across a growth plate is the specific thing being avoided. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

How each operation is performed is not described here. This page is about which strategy is chosen and why, and the technique belongs with the surgeons who perform it.

Rehabilitation Outline

Rehabilitation after a paediatric reconstruction is broadly the adult programme, with two differences. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Children are harder to restrain in the early phase, and return to pivoting sport is the decision with the highest stakes in the chapter because the second injury rate in this group is the highest. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • Strategy means the graft's route relative to the growth plates.
  • Physeal sparing, all-epiphyseal, partial transphyseal and transphyseal.
  • Skeletal age selects between them.
  • Soft tissue grafts are generally preferred in a growing knee.
  • Return to pivoting sport is the highest stakes decision in the chapter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Which strategy is best? There is no single best. The strategies exist because children arrive at different stages of growth, and the one chosen is the one matched to how much growth is left.

Q. Does avoiding the growth plates make the knee less stable? That is one of the open questions. The strategies that avoid the plates are not the adult operation, and whether they control rotation as well is still being studied.

Q. Why not use a graft with bone on the end, as in adults? Because a bone block sitting across a growth plate is the specific risk the strategy is built to avoid. Soft tissue grafts carry no bone across the plate.

Q. Will she need another operation when she stops growing? Not as a matter of routine. A second operation becomes a question if the graft fails or if a growth problem develops, and surveillance is how the second is found.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The strategies are grouped by their relationship to the physes rather than by graft or fixation. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

Physeal sparing techniques keep the graft outside both physes, classically using the iliotibial band through an extra-articular route and over the top of the femur. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

All-epiphyseal techniques place both tunnels entirely within the epiphyses, reproducing the anatomical position without crossing a physis. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Partial transphyseal crosses one physis and spares the other, usually sparing the distal femoral one because it carries most of the remaining growth. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Transphyseal crosses both, with a soft tissue graft and fixation away from the physes, and it is the strategy used as maturity approaches. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Management Overview

Selection is by skeletal age and remaining growth rather than by chronological age. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

The general pattern taught is physeal sparing or all-epiphyseal for substantial remaining growth, transphyseal with a soft tissue graft near maturity, and the adult operation once the physes are closed. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Graft selection avoids bone blocks across a physis and avoids large tunnel diameters relative to physeal width. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Complications and Prognosis

The complication specific to this page is growth disturbance, and the ones shared with adults are graft failure, stiffness and the meniscal consequences of any delay. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Re-rupture rates reported in adolescent athletes are the highest in this chapter, which belongs in the strategy discussion rather than after it. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Strategies are grouped by their relationship to the physes.
  • Physeal sparing keeps the graft outside both physes.
  • All-epiphyseal places both tunnels within the epiphyses.
  • Partial transphyseal usually spares the distal femoral physis.
  • Selection is by skeletal age rather than by birthday.

MRCS Level

Management Overview

In a viva the expected answer classifies the strategies before choosing one.

Say physeal sparing, all-epiphyseal, partial transphyseal and transphyseal, then say that skeletal age selects between them. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Say soft tissue graft, no bone block across a physis, fixation away from the physis, and tunnel diameter considered against physeal width. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Do not describe the technique. The question is a decision question and the technique belongs to the operative syllabus. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Rehabilitation Outline

Say criteria based return rather than time based, and name the criteria, because this is the group where the second injury rate makes that distinction matter most. 6.6.3 covers neuromuscular factors.

Say that compliance with restriction in a child is a genuine problem and is managed by involving the family rather than by instruction. 6.6.4 covers prevention.

Complications and Prognosis

Pair every strategy with its own risk: physeal sparing trades anatomical position for physeal safety, and transphyseal trades physeal safety for anatomical position. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Saying that trade out loud is what separates a classification answer from an understanding one. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • Classify the strategies before choosing one.
  • Skeletal age selects between them.
  • Soft tissue graft, no bone across a physis, fixation away from it.
  • Criteria based return matters most in this group.
  • Each strategy trades physeal safety against anatomical position.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is given a specific skeletal age and asked to choose and justify.

The answer that holds up states the remaining growth, names the strategy, states what it trades, and states the surveillance that follows. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

A candidate who names a favourite strategy for every age has answered a different question, because the strategies exist to be matched. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Where the knee is also meniscally injured the priority shifts, since the meniscus is the tissue whose loss is permanent and the ligament is the one that can be revised. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Complications and Prognosis

The two sided risk has to be quantified as far as the evidence allows and declared as unquantified where it does not. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The prognosis statement that gets missed is the second injury risk in this age group, and the family will measure the whole operation by it. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether physeal sparing strategies control rotation as well as anatomical transphyseal reconstruction. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The second is whether the growth disturbance rate is low enough with modern transphyseal technique to make the sparing strategies unnecessary. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The third is whether graft choice or return to sport behaviour explains the high re-rupture rate in adolescents. 6.6.1 covers the epidemiology.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Key Learning Points

  • State remaining growth, the strategy, its trade, and the surveillance.
  • A favourite strategy for every age answers a different question.
  • Meniscal injury shifts the priority, because its loss is permanent.
  • Declare the risk as unquantified where the evidence does not quantify it.
  • The family will measure the operation by the second injury risk.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level these knees arrive for revision, and the strategy used first time determines what is still possible.

An all-epiphyseal reconstruction leaves epiphyseal bone already tunnelled, and a physeal sparing reconstruction leaves a non-anatomical graft path. Neither is apparent from an operation note that says ACL reconstruction. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The habit worth keeping is to record the strategy by name, each tunnel's relationship to the physis, the graft and its diameter, and the fixation and its position. Four items, which together let the next surgeon plan. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The second habit is to keep the intra-operative images, because tunnel position is the single most useful fact in a revision and a later scan shows the result rather than the aim. 5.7.12 covers the tunnel assessment.

Both habits are for a surgeon who is not yet involved, which is why they survive only as a deliberate routine. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • The first strategy determines what revision is still possible.
  • An operation note saying ACL reconstruction distinguishes none of them.
  • Record the strategy, each tunnel's physeal relationship, the graft, the fixation.
  • Keep the intra-operative images.
  • Tunnel position is the most useful fact in a revision.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that strategy choice is partly a question about the surgeon rather than about the child, and that part is rarely said aloud. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

The strategies that avoid the physes are performed well by people who perform them often, and a child near the middle of the range can reasonably be offered either the strategy the evidence slightly favours or the one the available surgeon does reliably. Those are different answers and both are defensible. 6.7.2 covers physeal injuries.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which set the remaining growth and therefore narrow the strategies. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which bear on graft choice and on the return decision more than on the route. Their psychological state, and the family's, since a family that has researched one strategy arrives wanting it. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which in this chapter often include a specific named technique found online.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and say plainly which part of the choice is about the knee and which part is about who is operating. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • Part of the choice is about the surgeon rather than the child.
  • Sparing strategies are done well by those who do them often.
  • The evidence favoured strategy and the reliable local one can differ.
  • Families arrive wanting a named technique found online.
  • Say which part of the choice is about the knee and which about the surgeon.
