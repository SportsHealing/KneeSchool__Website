Instability

Page 6.5.1 · Chronic ACL Deficiency · condition page

Giving way is an event rather than a feeling, and counting the events is more informative than measuring the laxity.

Instability

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

A knee without a working ACL can let the shin bone shift forward and twist when the leg turns. That shift is what people call giving way. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

It does not usually happen walking. It happens on a turn, a sidestep or a landing. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

How It Presents

The knee suddenly feels like it has moved, the person often stumbles or falls, and the knee can swell afterwards. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Each of those episodes can damage something inside the knee, which is why they are counted rather than tolerated. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Key Learning Points

  • A knee without a working ACL can let the shin shift and twist.
  • That shift is what giving way means.
  • It happens on turns and landings rather than when walking.
  • The knee may swell after an episode.
  • Each episode can damage something inside.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

Instability after an ACL injury means the shin bone shifts forward and rotates under the thigh bone during a turn, which the ligament would have prevented. 3.7.9 covers the pivot shift mechanics.

Laxity and instability are different things. Laxity is how much the knee moves when a clinician tests it. Instability is whether it gives way when you use it. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

A knee can be lax and stable in practice, and that is why the examination alone does not decide treatment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

How It Presents

An episode is sudden: the knee shifts, often with a stumble or a fall, sometimes with swelling over the following day. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Many people avoid the movements that cause it without realising, so a knee that has not given way may simply not have been asked to. 6.1.5 covers the mechanisms of injury.

Management Overview

Counting episodes is the most useful single thing. A knee that gives way repeatedly is being damaged; a knee that does not may need nothing. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Rehabilitation reduces episodes for some people by improving control and strength. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Reconstruction is offered where episodes continue despite that, or where the demand makes them likely. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Instability is the shin shifting and rotating during a turn.
  • Laxity is measured; instability is experienced.
  • A lax knee can be stable in practice.
  • Counting episodes is the most useful single thing.
  • A knee that has not given way may not have been asked to.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. My knee feels fine. Does that mean it is stable? It means it has not given way during what you have done so far. If you have not returned to turning or pivoting, the ligament's job has not been tested yet.

Q. How many episodes are too many? There is no agreed number, and each one risks damage to the meniscus. The practical position is that repeated episodes are a reason to act rather than a count to reach.

Q. Can I stop it giving way without surgery? Sometimes. Strength and control work reduces episodes for some people, particularly those not returning to pivoting sport. It works better for some knees than others and it is a legitimate first attempt.

Q. Why does it swell after an episode? Because something inside has usually been irritated or injured, often the meniscus or the joint lining. Swelling after an episode is a reason to be reviewed rather than to wait.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

Functional instability is symptomatic giving way during activity, and it is distinct from measurable laxity. 3.7.8 covers the anterior stability.

The event is an anterior subluxation of the tibia with internal rotation, reducing as the knee flexes, which is what the pivot shift reproduces. 3.7.9 covers the pivot shift mechanics.

Copers maintain function without reconstruction; the term describes an observed outcome rather than an identified group. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Examination

The Lachman test measures translation and the pivot shift reproduces the episode, which is why the second correlates better with the complaint. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

A high grade pivot shift suggests anterolateral involvement alongside. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

Guarding suppresses the pivot shift, so a negative test in an apprehensive patient is uninformative. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Management Overview

The history of episodes, not the grade of laxity, drives the decision. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Rehabilitation targets quadriceps and hamstring strength, neuromuscular control and movement retraining. 6.6.3 covers the neuromuscular factors.

Reconstruction is indicated for continued instability or for demand that makes it likely. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Key Learning Points

  • Functional instability is distinct from measurable laxity.
  • The event is anterior subluxation with internal rotation.
  • The pivot shift reproduces it and correlates with the complaint.
  • A negative pivot shift in a guarded patient is uninformative.
  • The history of episodes drives the decision.

MRCS Level

Examination

In a viva the expected answer separates laxity from instability in the first sentence.

The expected points are the definition of an episode, the pivot shift correlation, the effect of guarding, the coper concept, and the primacy of the history. 4.10.6 covers the pivot shift test.

Say that laxity is a sign and instability is a symptom, and that treatment follows the symptom. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Say that an examination under anaesthesia is how the sign is obtained reliably when it matters. 4.10.10 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Management Overview

Count episodes, assess demand, trial rehabilitation where appropriate, reconstruct for continued instability. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The meniscus is what the decision protects, which is the reason episodes are not simply tolerated. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Complications and Prognosis

Each episode risks meniscal and chondral injury, and that risk is the whole argument for intervening. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

Patients who modify activity to avoid episodes have reduced their risk and their function together, which is a trade rather than a cure. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Separate laxity from instability in the first sentence.
  • Laxity is a sign and instability is a symptom.
  • An examination under anaesthesia obtains the sign reliably.
  • The meniscus is what the decision protects.
  • Activity modification trades function for risk rather than curing anything.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to decide for a described patient and to say what would change the decision.

A patient with recurrent episodes has an indication regardless of laxity grade. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

A patient with no episodes and no intention to pivot has no instability to treat, whatever the Lachman test shows. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Between them, demand decides, and demand is a prediction obtained by asking rather than a measurement. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

An already damaged meniscus raises the urgency, because the remaining tissue is what the operation is now protecting. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

Complications and Prognosis

Delay in a genuinely unstable knee costs meniscus, and that loss is permanent where everything else in this decision is recoverable. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Reconstruction in a stable knee costs a graft, a donor site and a recovery for no instability. 6.3.2 covers the mid-substance tear.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is whether copers can be identified prospectively. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

The second is how many episodes justify abandoning a rehabilitation trial. 6.5.3 covers cartilage degeneration.

The third is whether early reconstruction reduces meniscal injury enough to change long term joint outcome. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Recurrent episodes are an indication regardless of laxity grade.
  • No episodes and no intention to pivot is no instability to treat.
  • Demand is a prediction obtained by asking.
  • Delay costs meniscus permanently.
  • Reconstruction in a stable knee costs a graft for no instability.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level instability is the group's central variable and the one most poorly recorded.

Everything downstream follows from how often this knee gives way, and almost nobody writes that number down, so a patient returning after years arrives with an account rather than a record. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The practical habit is to ask for episodes by count and by circumstance at every review, and to record both, because the trend is what decides and a single answer cannot show a trend. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The second habit is to ask what the patient has stopped doing, since avoidance suppresses the symptom without reducing the underlying deficiency, and a patient who has quietly given up sport reports a stable knee. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Taken together those two questions carry more of the decision than any examination finding on this page. 6.1.3 covers the epidemiology.

Key Learning Points

  • Instability is the central variable and the most poorly recorded.
  • A patient returning after years brings an account rather than a record.
  • Ask for episodes by count and circumstance at every review.
  • Ask what the patient has stopped doing.
  • Avoidance suppresses the symptom without reducing the deficiency.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is that the deciding variable is reported by the patient and cannot be verified.

A patient who wants an operation can describe episodes, and a patient who does not can minimise them, and in both cases the account is the evidence. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Corroboration is available and underused: swelling after episodes, a meniscal tear that was not there before, a change in what they do. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which set how many years of instability are at stake. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which is the variable the whole decision rests on and is obtained by asking what they intend rather than what they did. Their psychological state, because fear of giving way produces avoidance that looks like stability. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which often arrive with a decision already made.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and ask what they have stopped doing. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Key Learning Points

  • The deciding variable is reported and cannot be verified.
  • Both wanting and not wanting an operation shape the account.
  • Corroboration exists and is underused.
  • Fear of giving way produces avoidance that looks like stability.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations frame it.
