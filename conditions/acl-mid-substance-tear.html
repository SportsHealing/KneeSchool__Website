Mid-substance Tear

Page 6.3.2 · ACL Tear Patterns · condition page

The default pattern, the one reconstruction was designed around, and the reason repair fell out of use in the first place.

Mid-substance Tear

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

Most ACL tears happen across the middle of the ligament rather than at either end. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

The middle has the poorest blood supply, so the two torn ends have little to heal with. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

That is why surgeons replace the ligament with a new one rather than sewing the old one back together. 6.1.1 covers what the ACL is.

How It Presents

A pop, swelling within hours, and a knee that cannot be trusted on turning. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

It feels no different from the other patterns, which is why a scan is used to tell them apart.

Key Learning Points

  • Most ACL tears happen across the middle.
  • The middle has the poorest blood supply.
  • The torn ends have little to heal with.
  • That is why the ligament is replaced rather than repaired.
  • The patterns feel the same and are told apart on a scan.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

A mid-substance tear means the ligament has torn across its body, leaving two ends that retract away from each other. 6.3.1 covers the femoral avulsion.

This is the commonest pattern and the one most people mean when they say a torn ACL. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

How It Presents

The presentation is that of any acute ACL injury, with rapid swelling and later instability on turning. 6.2.5 covers acute haemarthrosis.

Management Overview

A completely torn ACL in its mid-substance does not reliably heal back to its original strength. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Reconstruction replaces it with a graft, usually taken from a tendon elsewhere in the same knee or leg. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Rehabilitation matters as much as the operation does, whichever route is taken, and it runs for months rather than weeks. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Not every mid-substance tear needs an operation. The decision rests on instability and on demand rather than on the scan alone. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • The ligament tears across its body and the ends retract.
  • This is the commonest pattern.
  • It does not reliably heal back to its original strength.
  • Reconstruction replaces it with a graft rather than repairing it.
  • Whether to operate rests on instability and demand.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Why can the torn ends not simply be stitched together? They were stitched, historically, and the results were poor. The mid-substance has little blood supply, the ends fray and retract, and the repaired ligament tended to stretch out. Reconstruction replaced the practice for that reason.

Q. Where does the graft come from? Usually from a tendon in the same leg, most often the hamstrings, the patellar tendon or the quadriceps tendon. Donor tissue is also used in some circumstances. Each has trade-offs that are worth discussing specifically.

Q. Does the new ligament ever become like the original? It remodels over months and ends up functionally similar rather than identical. It does not carry the original's nerve supply, which is part of why a reconstructed knee can feel different even when it tests as stable.

Q. How long before I am back to sport? Months, and the number varies with the sport, the graft and the person. Return is judged on what the knee can do rather than on the calendar, which is why no single figure is useful here.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

A mid-substance rupture disrupts the ligament between its two footprints, leaving retracted ends and no apposition. 2.7.1 covers the gross anatomy.

The mid-substance is the least vascular part of the ligament, which underlies its poor intrinsic healing. 2.7.4 covers the blood supply.

This is the pattern around which reconstruction technique was developed, and the reason primary repair was largely abandoned. 6.3.6 covers healing ACL tears.

Investigations and Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging shows discontinuity of the fibres with altered orientation of the remnant. 5.7.3 covers complete tears.

Secondary signs, including the bone bruise pattern and anterior tibial translation, support the diagnosis and are read alongside it. 6.4.1 covers bone bruising.

Management Overview

Reconstruction, not repair, is the operative treatment for this pattern. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Graft choice, tunnel position and fixation are the technical variables, and the first is the one patients are usually offered a say in. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Key Learning Points

  • The rupture lies between the two footprints with retracted ends.
  • The mid-substance is the least vascular part.
  • Poor intrinsic healing follows from that.
  • Reconstruction technique was developed around this pattern.
  • Repair was largely abandoned because of it.

MRCS Level

Management Overview

In a viva this is the reference pattern, and the expected answer uses it to frame the others.

The expected points are the retracted ends, the avascularity, the failure of historical repair, reconstruction as the operative standard, and the fact that non-operative management remains legitimate. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Say that graft choice is a trade-off rather than a hierarchy, and that the trade-offs differ by patient. 3.7.18 covers the patellar tendon graft biomechanics.

Say that tunnel position matters more to outcome than graft choice does. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Complications and Prognosis

Graft failure, stiffness and donor site morbidity are the three complications expected to be named, and they distribute differently between graft choices. 3.7.17 covers the hamstring graft biomechanics.

Re-rupture risk concentrates in young patients returning to pivoting sport, which is the group most likely to be reconstructed. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Key Learning Points

  • This is the reference pattern that frames the others.
  • Graft choice is a trade-off rather than a hierarchy.
  • Tunnel position matters more to outcome than graft choice.
  • Graft failure, stiffness and donor site morbidity are the three complications.
  • Re-rupture concentrates in the group most likely to be reconstructed.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is expected to justify a graft and a tunnel strategy for a specific patient rather than in general.

A patellar tendon graft offers bone to bone healing and an anterior knee pain and kneeling cost, which matters to some occupations a great deal and to others not at all. 3.7.18 covers the patellar tendon graft biomechanics.

A hamstring graft avoids that and costs hamstring strength, which matters in sprinting sports. 3.7.17 covers the hamstring graft biomechanics.

A quadriceps tendon graft sits between them and has the shortest body of evidence. 3.7.19 covers the quadriceps tendon graft biomechanics.

Tunnel position is the variable that is both most influential and most within the surgeon's control, which is why it dominates revision discussions. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Complications and Prognosis

Failure is concentrated in the first years after return to sport and in young patients, which makes return criteria part of the operation's result. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

Donor site morbidity is graft specific, persistent and underweighted at the point of consent. 6.1.2 covers the functions of the ACL.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is graft selection, where the trade-offs are documented and the ranking is not. 3.7.19 covers the quadriceps tendon graft biomechanics.

The second is whether an anterolateral procedure should be added routinely in high risk patients. 6.4.4 covers ALL injuries.

The third is whether failure after return to sport reflects the graft, the return criteria, or the sport, since the three are rarely separated. 6.8.1 covers graft failure.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

Key Learning Points

  • Justify a graft for a patient rather than in general.
  • Patellar tendon costs kneeling and anterior knee pain.
  • Hamstring costs hamstring strength in sprinting sports.
  • Tunnel position is the most influential variable under the surgeon's control.
  • Donor site morbidity is persistent and underweighted at consent.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the mid-substance tear is where technique is least interesting and selection is most, because the operation is standardised and the results are not.

Two surgeons performing the same reconstruction on two patients get different results, and most of the difference lives in the patients rather than in the tunnels. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The variables that move outcome most are age, return to pivoting sport, and whether a meniscus was preserved, and only the last is under operative control. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

That makes meniscal preservation the most consequential thing a surgeon does in a mid-substance tear, which is not how the operation is usually described. 6.4.3 covers meniscal root tears.

A fellow whose attention is on graft choice while the meniscus is being resected has the priorities inverted. 6.4.2 covers ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • Technique is standardised and results are not.
  • Most of the difference lives in the patients.
  • Age, return to pivoting and meniscal preservation move outcome most.
  • Only meniscal preservation is under operative control.
  • Attention on graft choice during a meniscal resection is inverted.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty with the commonest pattern is that it is the one where the operation is most easily offered without the alternative being examined.

Reconstruction is reliable, familiar and expected by patients, which makes offering it the path of least resistance in clinic. 6.3.5 covers complete tears.

Rehabilitation alone remains a legitimate outcome for a patient whose knee is stable in what they need to do, and it is under-offered rather than under-performing. 6.1.4 covers the natural history.

The five things decide which conversation to have. The patient's age and physiology, which set healing and how many decades the knee must last. Their medical comorbidities, which change graft healing and operative risk. The demands they put on the knee, which is the single most informative item and is obtained by asking. Their psychological state, because fear of reinjury governs what the knee is used for whichever route is taken. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which frequently already include an operation before the consultation starts.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and name the non-operative route even when the patient has not asked about it. 6.5.1 covers instability.

Key Learning Points

  • The commonest pattern is where the alternative is least examined.
  • Reconstruction is the path of least resistance in clinic.
  • Rehabilitation alone is under-offered rather than under-performing.
  • Age and physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and the

patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations decide the conversation.

  • Name the non-operative route even when it is not asked about.
