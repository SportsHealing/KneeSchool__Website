Physeal Injuries

Choose a depth

For Young Learners

Definition

The growth plate is the layer where a bone gets longer, and it sits near each end of the bone. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

If it is injured, that part of the bone can stop growing properly while the rest of the leg carries on, which makes the leg shorter or bent. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

How It Presents

It does not show up straight away. It shows up months or years later as a difference between the two legs. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

That is why a growing child who has had a knee operation is checked again as they grow, even when the knee itself feels fine. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Key Learning Points

  • The growth plate is where a bone gets longer.
  • Injuring it can stop part of a bone growing properly.
  • The leg can end up shorter or bent.
  • The problem appears months or years later.
  • Children are checked as they grow for that reason.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.

For Patients

Definition

A physis is a growth plate. Physeal injury here means damage to it during or after an operation near it, and the two consequences described are a shorter limb and an angled one. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

The concern is specific rather than general: a tunnel drilled across a growth plate, or hardware placed across it, can cause a localised arrest. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

How It Presents

Nothing is noticed at the time. A difference emerges over the remaining years of growth, which may be several. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

It is found by measuring rather than by asking, because a child with a developing limb length difference walks and plays normally for a long time. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Management Overview

Prevention is the main strategy: techniques are chosen so that tunnels and fixation avoid or minimally cross the growth plates in a child with substantial growth remaining. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Surveillance is the second strategy, and it means scheduled clinical measurement through remaining growth rather than a scan. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Where a problem develops, options exist to address the length or the angle, and they belong to the surgeons who provide them. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Key Learning Points

  • A physis is a growth plate.
  • The two consequences are a shorter limb and an angled one.
  • Nothing is noticed at the time of surgery.
  • It is found by measuring rather than by asking.
  • Prevention is technique choice and surveillance is scheduled measurement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How likely is this? Uncommon with techniques chosen for a growing knee, and the reported figures come from small series. This page carries no rate, because none has been verified here.

Q. When would we know? Over the remaining years of growth rather than at the operation. That is why follow up continues after the knee itself has recovered.

Q. Could it be fixed if it happened? There are established ways of addressing a length difference or an angular deformity. Finding it early makes the correction smaller, which is the point of the surveillance.

Q. Does avoiding surgery avoid this risk? It avoids this particular risk and accepts a different one, because a knee that keeps giving way loses meniscal tissue permanently. Neither path is risk free.

If your knee cannot take your weight after an injury, if it is hot and swollen with a fever, or if you develop new numbness or coldness in the foot, arrange urgent assessment.

For Medical Students

Definition

The physis is the cartilaginous growth plate, and iatrogenic physeal injury is the complication that distinguishes paediatric from adult reconstruction. 1.2.10 covers the growth plates.

Two patterns are described. A central arrest shortens the bone symmetrically. An eccentric or peripheral arrest tethers one side and produces angular deformity. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Causes and Risk Factors

The described risk factors are a large tunnel diameter relative to the physis, a tunnel crossing the physis peripherally rather than centrally, hardware or a bone block placed across the physis, and soft tissue stripping around the periphery. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

The distal femoral physis carries most of the remaining growth in the limb and is therefore the one that matters most. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The amount of growth remaining converts any arrest into a deformity, so the same technical event matters more in a younger child. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Investigations and Imaging

Surveillance is clinical first: standing assessment, block testing for limb length, and a recorded measurement rather than an impression. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Where a difference is suspected, long leg imaging quantifies length and alignment together. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

MRI shows the physis directly and is the test that demonstrates a bony bar across it. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Key Learning Points

  • Iatrogenic physeal injury distinguishes paediatric from adult reconstruction.
  • Central arrest shortens and eccentric arrest angulates.
  • Tunnel size, peripheral position and hardware across the physis are the risks.
  • The distal femoral physis carries most of the remaining growth.
  • Remaining growth converts an arrest into a deformity.

MRCS Level

Investigations and Imaging

In a viva the expected answer makes surveillance clinical and imaging consequential.

Say recorded clinical measurement at intervals through remaining growth, long leg films where a difference is suspected, and MRI to demonstrate a physeal bar. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Say that a single post-operative scan does not constitute surveillance, because the finding develops over years. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Management Overview

The management is prevention, surveillance and early correction, in that order. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Prevention is the technique choice matched to skeletal age, and the detail of each technique belongs to the surgical academy. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Complications and Prognosis

A small limb length difference is usually tolerated without intervention, and an angular deformity is less forgiving because it changes load distribution across the joint. 3.13.8 covers alignment and OA.

That is the link worth making in a viva: an angular deformity in a young knee feeds the same compartment loading problem the deficiency chapter ends on. 6.5.4 covers osteoarthritis.

Key Learning Points

  • Surveillance is clinical measurement over years, not one scan.
  • Long leg films quantify length and alignment together.
  • MRI demonstrates a physeal bar.
  • Prevention, surveillance, early correction, in that order.
  • An angular deformity feeds the compartment loading problem.

FRCS Level

Management Overview

At examination level the candidate is given a child with a growing limb length difference after a reconstruction and asked what to do.

The structured answer measures before deciding, quantifies the remaining growth, and separates a length problem from an angular one, because they are addressed differently. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Then it states the options by magnitude and remaining growth, and refers the growth modulation question to those who do it. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

The candidate who starts with an operation rather than a measurement has missed what the question tests. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Complications and Prognosis

The medicolegal dimension is real here and the examination does not hide it: this is a complication of an elective operation in a child. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

The defence is the record: skeletal age documented, technique chosen for it, risk discussed, and surveillance arranged. Where those four exist the complication is a known risk, and where they do not it is something else. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Controversies and Evidence

The first open question is the true incidence with current physeal respecting techniques, which small series cannot settle. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

The second is whether tunnel diameter relative to physeal width predicts arrest usefully enough to guide graft choice. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

The third is what surveillance interval is sufficient, which is set by custom rather than by evidence. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

This page states the three and settles none, because no source has been checked in this environment. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

Key Learning Points

  • Measure before deciding and quantify remaining growth.
  • Separate a length problem from an angular one.
  • Starting with an operation misses what the question tests.
  • The defence is skeletal age, technique, risk discussion and surveillance.
  • The surveillance interval is set by custom rather than evidence.

Fellowship Level

Complications and Prognosis

At fellowship level the child arrives with a deformity and no baseline, which makes the first question unanswerable: how much of this was there before the operation.

Mild limb length difference is common in the general population, so a difference found after surgery is not automatically caused by it, and without a pre-operative measurement nobody can say. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

The habit worth keeping is therefore pre-operative: a recorded limb length and alignment measurement in every skeletally immature knee before any reconstruction. One measurement, once. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

The second habit is to record each tunnel's relationship to the physis at the time, in words, because a later scan shows the result and not the intention. 6.8.2 covers tunnel malposition.

Both habits serve a consultation years away with somebody else in the room. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Key Learning Points

  • The child arrives with a deformity and no baseline.
  • Mild limb length difference is common in the population anyway.
  • Record limb length and alignment before every immature reconstruction.
  • Record each tunnel's relationship to the physis in words.
  • A later scan shows the result and not the intention.

Consultant Perspective

Controversies and Evidence

The consultant difficulty is consenting for a risk that is small, delayed, hard to quantify and attributable to the surgeon, against a risk that is larger, nearer, and attributable to the injury. 6.5.2 covers meniscal damage.

Families do not weigh those two symmetrically, and nor do surgeons, because one of them appears in a complaint and the other does not. 6.7.1 covers skeletally immature patients.

The five things frame it. The patient's age and physiology, which set both the remaining growth at risk and the years of instability avoided. Their medical comorbidities. The demands they put on the knee, which determine how soon the alternative risk is realised. Their psychological state, and the parents', since a family already frightened by the injury hears a growth risk as a reason to do nothing. And their own ideas, concerns and expectations, which commonly rank a visible deformity far above an invisible meniscal loss.

Take all five explicitly rather than by impression, and state both risks in the same sentence rather than in sequence. 6.7.3 covers the reconstruction strategies.

Key Learning Points

  • One risk appears in a complaint and the other does not.
  • Families and surgeons both weigh them asymmetrically.
  • Demand determines how soon the alternative risk is realised.
  • A frightened family hears a growth risk as a reason to do nothing.
  • State both risks in the same sentence rather than in sequence.
