Chronic Effusions

Page 4.8.8 · Effusion Assessment · examination page

A knee that keeps filling, which is usually not a mechanical problem and is usually treated as one.

Chronic Effusions

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

A chronic effusion is one that persists or recurs over weeks to months, and its assessment is a differential diagnosis rather than a test.

The important division is between a joint that is producing fluid because of a mechanical irritant and one producing it because of a synovial process, since the treatments are opposite. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

The examination's contribution is establishing whether there is anything mechanical to find, because a knee with no mechanical findings and a persistent effusion is not a surgical problem. 4.6.1 covers systematic palpation.

How It Is Performed

Establish the pattern from the history: persistent or recurrent, related to activity or not, and how long. 4.2.3 covers swelling as a symptom.

Assess the effusion's size and compliance, since a large compliant effusion suggests a slow accumulation. 4.8.6 covers ballottement.

Assess the temperature, because warmth moves the differential towards inflammation, crystal disease or infection. 4.6.2 covers it.

Perform the full mechanical examination: the joint line, the menisci, the ligaments and the patellofemoral joint. 4.6.4 covers joint line tenderness.

Examine the other joints and ask about systemic symptoms, since a knee is one joint of several in inflammatory disease. 4.2.14 covers inflammatory pain.

Examine the popliteal fossa, since a chronic effusion commonly fills a cyst. 4.6.10 covers it.

What a Positive Means

A chronic effusion with mechanical findings indicates a mechanical irritant, and treating it may settle the effusion. 4.9.11 covers meniscal examination in osteoarthritis.

A chronic effusion with no mechanical findings indicates a synovial, inflammatory or crystal process, and arthroscopy for it is futile. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

A warm chronic effusion raises inflammatory arthritis, crystal disease or low grade infection, and the last has to be excluded in an operated knee. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A chronic effusion with other joints involved is a systemic disease presenting at the knee. 4.2.14 covers inflammatory pain.

A chronic effusion with a popliteal cyst is the joint's production filling the cyst, and treating the cyst alone recurs. 4.6.10 covers the fossa.

Key Learning Points

  • The division is between a mechanical irritant and a synovial process.
  • The examination's contribution is establishing whether anything mechanical is there to find.
  • A chronic effusion with no mechanical findings is not a surgical problem.
  • A warm chronic effusion raises inflammation, crystal disease or low grade infection.
  • A popliteal cyst reflects the joint's production, so treating it alone recurs.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva the chronic effusion is a differential diagnosis station, and listing the non mechanical causes is what the examiner is waiting for.

The expected points are the pattern, the size and compliance, the temperature, the full mechanical examination, the other joints, and the popliteal fossa.

Say that you would examine other joints and ask about systemic features, since that is the step most candidates omit. 4.2.14 covers inflammatory pain.

Say that a knee with no mechanical findings and a persistent effusion needs an inflammatory and crystal assessment rather than an arthroscopy. 4.8.10 covers crystal arthropathy.

Say that low grade infection has to be excluded in a knee with a prosthesis or a graft. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that an effusion persists when production exceeds clearance, so a chronic effusion means an ongoing stimulus to the synovium rather than a residual volume. 4.8.1 covers normal fluid physiology.

A mechanical irritant such as an unstable meniscal fragment or a loose body provides that stimulus intermittently, which is why mechanical effusions are often activity related. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

A synovial process provides it continuously, which is why inflammatory effusions are less related to activity and more persistent. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

Crystal deposition provides it episodically and with inflammation, which is why crystal effusions are warm and recurrent. 4.8.10 covers crystal arthropathy.

How Good It Is

The examination is good at establishing the presence of an effusion and at excluding mechanical findings, and it cannot determine the cause of a non mechanical one.

Its most valuable contribution is a negative one: a thorough mechanical examination that finds nothing is what moves the patient out of the surgical pathway. 4.6.1 covers palpation.

Aspiration and blood tests answer what the examination raises, and this is a presentation where the investigations lead. 4.1.6 covers the relationship with imaging.

The pattern from the history carries more weight than any finding, which is a recurring feature of the effusion chapter. 4.2.3 covers it.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • A chronic effusion means an ongoing stimulus rather than a residual volume.
  • A mechanical irritant stimulates intermittently, so those effusions are activity related.
  • A synovial process stimulates continuously, so those are persistent.
  • Crystal deposition stimulates episodically and with inflammation.
  • The examination's most valuable contribution is a thorough negative.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the chronic effusion decides whether a patient belongs in a surgical pathway at all, which is the largest decision an examination can contribute.

A chronic effusion with clear mechanical findings is a surgical problem, and treating the irritant settles the effusion. 4.9.11 covers one such case.

A chronic effusion with no mechanical findings is not, and the recognisable pattern of futility in this area is repeated arthroscopy in exactly that patient. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

A chronic effusion in a knee with a prosthesis or a graft raises low grade infection, and excluding it precedes any revision rather than following it. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A chronic effusion also blocks rehabilitation, so in a surgical patient it has to be settled before a reconstruction rather than during the recovery. 4.3.9 covers wasting.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is arthroscoping a knee with a chronic effusion and no mechanical findings.

The second is not examining the other joints.

The third is attributing a chronic effusion in an operated knee to activity without excluding infection.

The fourth is treating a popliteal cyst as the diagnosis. 4.6.10 covers it.

The fifth is aspirating repeatedly without addressing the cause, which refills predictably. 4.8.1 covers the physiology.

Key Learning Points

  • The chronic effusion decides whether a patient belongs in a surgical pathway at all.
  • Repeated arthroscopy in a knee with no mechanical findings is the recognisable futility here.
  • A chronic effusion with a prosthesis or a graft raises low grade infection.
  • An effusion blocks rehabilitation, so it is settled before a reconstruction.
  • Repeated aspiration without addressing the cause refills predictably.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice the chronic effusion is the commonest presentation in which the referral's premise is wrong.

A patient referred for a mechanical opinion with a persistent effusion, normal ligaments and no mechanical symptoms has a rheumatological problem, and the examination has established that before the consultation begins. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

A patient with several previous arthroscopies for a recurrent effusion has a history that is itself the diagnosis, since a mechanical cause would have been found. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

A patient with a chronic effusion after an arthroplasty or a reconstruction has infection until it is excluded, and the number of years since surgery does not exclude it. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A patient whose chronic effusion fills a popliteal cyst that has been excised and recurred has had the consequence treated twice, and the joint is where the problem is. 4.6.10 covers the fossa.

In medicolegal work a chronic effusion attributed to an injury years earlier is difficult to support unless the mechanical cause is demonstrable, and saying so is the honest position. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

Key Learning Points

  • The chronic effusion is the commonest presentation in which the referral's premise is wrong.
  • Several arthroscopies for a recurrent effusion is a history that is itself the diagnosis.
  • A chronic effusion after surgery is infection until excluded, however many years have passed.
  • An excised and recurred cyst means the consequence was treated twice.
  • Attribution to an old injury is hard to support without a demonstrable mechanical cause.
