Revision Knee Surgery

Page 4.20.9 · Examination of the Post-operative Knee · examination page

Examining a knee that has been operated on more than once, where the examination's job is to find the reason the last operation did not work.

Revision Knee Surgery

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

A revision assessment examines a knee with a surgical history, so the findings are read against that history rather than against a normal knee. 4.23.5 covers the revision patient.

The examination is establishing what has been done, what is failing now, and whether the original problem was correctly identified. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

It matters that the commonest reason an operation did not work is that the diagnosis was incomplete, so the examination starts from the beginning. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

How It Is Performed

Establish the full history: every operation, its date, what it was for, and what changed afterwards. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

Map the scars, because they are the most reliable record when the notes are incomplete. 4.3.7 covers surgical scars.

Assess the skin and soft tissue envelope, since multiple incisions limit what is possible. 4.3.6 covers skin changes.

Assess the temperature and the effusion, because infection is a cause of failure that must be excluded early. 4.6.2 covers temperature assessment.

Measure the range and record both deficits, since a revision often has to address stiffness as well. 4.7.10 covers the post operative range assessment.

Assess the alignment standing, since an uncorrected alignment is a common reason a procedure failed. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Assess the ligaments systematically, because a missed ligament injury is a common reason a reconstruction failed. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Assess the neurovascular state and record it, because that record matters before a revision. 4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa examination.

What a Positive Means

A knee with a clear single failure, a competent soft tissue envelope and no infection is the straightforward revision assessment.

An uncorrected alignment with a failed reconstruction or a failed cartilage procedure suggests the load was the unaddressed cause. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

A missed ligament injury alongside a failed reconstruction suggests the original diagnosis was incomplete. 4.13.4 covers the posterolateral corner assessment.

Warmth, an effusion and a poor result from the outset raise infection, which changes the whole plan. 4.6.2 covers temperature assessment.

Stiffness with a technically satisfactory previous operation is its own problem and is addressed as one. 4.7.5 covers the end feel.

Key Learning Points

  • The findings are read against the surgical history, not against normal.
  • Scars are the most reliable record when the notes are incomplete.
  • An incomplete original diagnosis is the commonest reason an operation failed.
  • An uncorrected alignment is a common unaddressed cause.
  • Infection is excluded early, because it changes the plan.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the failed previous surgery station and the expected answer re-examines the knee from the beginning.

The expected points are the full surgical history, the scar map, the soft tissue envelope, the infection assessment, the range, the alignment, the systematic ligament examination, the extensor mechanism, the neurovascular record and the function.

Say that you would re-examine for the original diagnosis rather than assuming it was right. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Say that you would assess the alignment, since it is a common unaddressed cause. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Say that you would record the neurovascular findings before any revision. 4.6.10 covers the popliteal fossa examination.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that an operation addresses one part of a problem, so a failure means either that part failed or another part was never addressed. 4.1.5 covers common examination pitfalls.

An uncorrected alignment loads any reconstruction or resurfacing, which is why it causes a second failure if it caused the first. 3.13.8 covers alignment and arthritis.

A missed secondary restraint overloads a primary reconstruction, which is why the systematic ligament examination is repeated. 3.7.3 covers the secondary restraints.

Infection produces failure that looks mechanical, which is why it is excluded on the pathway rather than on the examination. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

How Good It Is

The examination is the most important part of a revision assessment, because it is where the unaddressed cause is usually found.

Its best contribution is the systematic re-examination, since the failure is more often an omission than a technical error. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

It cannot assess fixation, tunnel position, component position or bone stock, all of which govern what a revision can do.

Cross sectional imaging and radiographs carry those, so the examination establishes the reason and the imaging the feasibility. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • A failure means either the addressed part failed or another part was never addressed.
  • An uncorrected alignment that caused one failure will cause another.
  • A missed secondary restraint overloads a primary reconstruction.
  • Infection produces failure that looks mechanical.
  • The failure is more often an omission than a technical error.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the examination establishes why the last operation failed, and that determines whether a revision is worth doing.

An unaddressed alignment or an unaddressed ligament is a correctable reason, and the examination identifies it. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

A failure with nothing unaddressed and a technically satisfactory previous operation is a different and worse position, and the examination establishes that by exclusion. 4.2.10 covers severity.

A compromised soft tissue envelope or a poor extensor mechanism limits what a revision can achieve, and both are clinical findings. 4.3.6 covers skin changes.

Infection excluded or confirmed changes the entire pathway, so it comes first in every revision assessment. 4.6.2 covers temperature assessment.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is accepting the previous diagnosis and examining only the failed structure. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Scars are read as a complete record when a scar does not say what was done through it. 4.3.7 covers surgical scars.

Laxity in a multiply operated knee is hard to attribute, and it is attributed anyway. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

The alignment is omitted in ligament revision assessments, where it is a common contributor. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Low grade infection is excluded clinically, which cannot be done. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

Key Learning Points

  • Accepting the previous diagnosis is the commonest error.
  • A scar does not say what was done through it.
  • Laxity in a multiply operated knee is attributed more confidently than it should be.
  • Alignment is omitted in ligament revision assessments.
  • Low grade infection cannot be excluded clinically.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the revision examination is the most demanding in the section, because every normal reference has been removed.

The contralateral knee is often operated too, so the comparison the examination depends on is unavailable. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Laxity after multiple procedures reflects the previous operations as much as any injury, so an abnormal finding may be the expected state of that knee. 4.12.1 covers valgus stress testing.

Pain in a multiply operated knee has more candidate sources than the examination can separate, and attributing it confidently is the error this examination most invites. 4.2.8 covers the location of pain.

The examination cannot assess bone stock, fixation or tunnel position, which determine whether a revision is technically possible at all. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

A patient's expectation after a failed operation is different from a primary patient's, and it shapes the result as much as the findings do. 4.22.8 covers the use of these scores.

Key Learning Points

  • The contralateral knee is often operated too, removing the comparison.
  • An abnormal finding may be the expected state of that knee.
  • Pain has more candidate sources than the examination can separate.
  • Bone stock, fixation and tunnel position govern feasibility and are not clinical.
  • Expectation after a failed operation shapes the result as much as the findings.
