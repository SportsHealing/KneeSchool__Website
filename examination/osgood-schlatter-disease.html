Osgood-Schlatter Disease

Page 4.16.4 · Tendon Examination · examination page

A traction problem at the tibial tubercle in a growing knee, and a diagnosis the examination makes without an image.

Osgood-Schlatter Disease

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

This is a traction apophysitis at the tibial tubercle, where the patellar tendon inserts into a growth centre that has not yet fused. 4.6.7 covers the tubercle.

Examining for it means localising tenderness to the tubercle itself in a growing patient, and establishing that the pain is activity related and settles with rest. 4.2.11 covers aggravating factors.

It matters because it is common, self limiting and frequently investigated as something more serious. 4.19.1 covers growth plate assessment.

How It Is Performed

Establish the patient's skeletal maturity from their age and growth history, since the diagnosis belongs to a growing skeleton. 4.19.1 covers that assessment.

Palpate the tibial tubercle directly and note focal tenderness over it. 4.6.7 covers the tubercle.

Note whether the tubercle is enlarged or prominent compared with the other side.

Palpate the tendon above the tubercle, which is usually less tender than the tubercle itself, and that difference is the localisation.

Ask the patient to extend the knee against resistance and note whether that reproduces the pain.

Assess the range and look for an effusion, because both should be normal and an abnormal finding points elsewhere. 4.6.3 covers the effusion assessment.

What a Positive Means

Focal tenderness over the tubercle in a growing patient, with activity related pain and a normal joint, is the characteristic picture.

An enlarged tubercle supports it and often persists into adulthood after the condition has resolved. 4.6.7 covers that residue.

An effusion, a limited range or night pain points away from this diagnosis and should be pursued. 4.8.2 covers the effusion's significance.

Bilateral symptoms are common and are not a reason to doubt the diagnosis.

Tenderness at the lower pole of the patella rather than the tubercle in a growing patient is the equivalent condition at the other end of the tendon. 4.16.5 covers it.

Key Learning Points

  • It is a traction apophysitis at an unfused growth centre.
  • The tenderness is focal over the tubercle, more than over the tendon above it.
  • The joint should be normal: an effusion or a limited range points elsewhere.
  • An enlarged tubercle supports it and often persists after resolution.
  • Bilateral symptoms are common and not a reason to doubt it.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva this is a recognition station and the expected answer includes what would make you look further.

The expected points are the skeletal maturity, the focal tubercle tenderness, the enlargement, the resisted extension, and the normal joint.

Say what would change your mind: an effusion, night pain, rest pain, a limited range or systemic features. 4.2.13 covers night pain.

Say that the diagnosis is clinical and that radiographs are taken to exclude rather than to confirm, which is the accurate framing. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

Say that it is self limiting and resolves with skeletal maturity, which is what the family most needs to hear.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that in a growing skeleton the apophysis is the weakest link in the extensor chain, so repeated traction injures it rather than the tendon. 2.4.2 covers ossification.

That is why the condition belongs to adolescence and why the equivalent adult problem sits in the tendon instead. 4.16.3 covers the adult version.

The apophysis fuses with maturity, which is the mechanism behind the condition's resolution and behind the residual prominence. 4.6.7 covers it.

An avulsion of the apophysis is a different and acute injury, and it abolishes active extension, which is why that test is performed even here. 4.7.8 covers the lag.

How Good It Is

The clinical diagnosis is reliable in a growing patient with focal tubercle tenderness and a normal joint, and the examination is the reference.

Its reliability depends on the joint being normal, which is why the effusion and the range are part of the assessment rather than a formality. 4.6.3 covers the effusion.

Radiographs exclude an avulsion, a tumour and an infection rather than confirming this, and their role is that negative. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

The examination cannot say how long it will last, which is the question the family asks, and the honest answer is until maturity. 4.19.1 covers growth plate assessment.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • In a growing skeleton the apophysis is the weakest link in the extensor chain.
  • That is why this belongs to adolescence and the adult equivalent sits in the tendon.
  • Fusion at maturity is the mechanism behind resolution and behind the residual prominence.
  • An avulsion is a different acute injury and abolishes active extension.
  • Radiographs exclude rather than confirm, and that negative is their role.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this diagnosis is a reassurance and a referral filter rather than a surgical decision.

A growing patient with the characteristic findings and a normal joint needs explanation, activity modification and time, and nothing further.

A patient with an effusion, night pain or a limited range is a different problem, and the examination's contribution is to notice that. 4.8.2 covers the effusion.

An acute avulsion is the surgical diagnosis in this region, and it is separated by the mechanism and by the loss of active extension. 4.7.8 covers it.

An adult with a prominent tubercle and pain over a device after a tubercle osteotomy is a different matter entirely, and the history separates them. 4.6.7 covers the tubercle.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is imaging extensively rather than examining and reassuring.

The second is accepting the diagnosis in a patient with an effusion or night pain.

The third is attributing adult tubercle pain to a childhood apophysitis that has resolved.

The fourth is missing an avulsion by not testing active extension.

The fifth is treating a residual prominence as an active problem.

Key Learning Points

  • The diagnosis is a reassurance and a referral filter rather than a surgical decision.
  • An effusion, night pain or a limited range means a different problem.
  • An acute avulsion is the surgical diagnosis here, separated by the loss of active extension.
  • Adult tubercle pain is not a resolved childhood apophysitis.
  • A residual prominence is not an active problem.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice this diagnosis is usually right and occasionally the label on something that is not.

A growing patient with tubercle pain, an effusion and night pain needs a different assessment, and the possibilities include infection and a bone tumour. 4.19.1 covers that group.

A patient referred with persistent symptoms after skeletal maturity has either a separate ossicle at the tubercle, a tendinopathy, or an unrelated problem, and the palpation separates the first two. 4.16.3 covers the second.

A patient with a prominent tubercle and pain on kneeling has a mechanical consequence of the healed condition, which is a real complaint and not an active apophysitis. 4.7.9 covers functional range.

An adolescent athlete whose symptoms are being managed by rest alone may do better with load modification, and the examination's role is identifying that the joint is normal so load can be worked with. 4.4.10 covers screening.

In medicolegal work this condition is developmental and is not attributable to an injury, which is a clear statement worth being able to make. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

Key Learning Points

  • Tubercle pain with an effusion and night pain in a growing patient needs a different assessment.
  • Persistent symptoms after maturity suggest a separate ossicle, a tendinopathy or something unrelated.
  • A prominent tubercle with pain on kneeling is a mechanical consequence, not an active apophysitis.
  • A normal joint is what allows load to be worked with rather than rest alone.
  • The condition is developmental and is not attributable to an injury.
