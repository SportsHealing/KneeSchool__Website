Synovitis

Page 4.8.9 · Effusion Assessment · examination page

An inflamed lining rather than an injured structure, and the examination finding that keeps producing futile operations.

Synovitis

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

Synovitis is inflammation of the joint lining, and it produces an effusion, warmth, a boggy thickening that can be felt, and pain that is not mechanical. 4.8.1 covers normal fluid physiology.

Examining for it means feeling for the thickened synovium as well as for the fluid, which is a different sensation and is the finding most often missed. 4.6.3 covers the effusion assessment.

It belongs in a mechanical section because it presents mechanically and is treated mechanically far too often, and the examination is what should prevent that. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

How It Is Performed

Assess the effusion in the usual way, noting its size and compliance. 4.8.5 covers the sweep test.

Feel along the joint margins and around the patella for a boggy, doughy thickening, which is synovium rather than fluid and does not move when the knee is swept.

Assess the temperature, comparing both knees and the limb above and below. 4.6.2 covers it.

Perform the full mechanical examination, because a negative one is the finding that matters here. 4.6.1 covers systematic palpation.

Examine the other joints and the skin, and ask about systemic symptoms, morning stiffness and family history. 4.2.14 covers inflammatory pain.

What a Positive Means

A boggy thickening that does not move with the fluid indicates synovial thickening rather than an effusion alone.

Warmth with an effusion and a boggy synovium indicates active synovitis, and the differential is inflammatory, crystal or infective. 4.8.10 covers crystal arthropathy.

A completely normal mechanical examination with these findings indicates a synovial process, and operating on that knee achieves nothing. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

Other joints involved, or a rash, or nail changes indicates a systemic disease, and the knee is one joint of several. 4.3.6 covers skin changes.

Synovitis in an operated knee raises low grade infection, and the number of years since the operation does not exclude it. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

Key Learning Points

  • Synovitis produces an effusion, warmth, a boggy thickening and non mechanical pain.
  • The thickening is felt as doughy and does not move when the knee is swept.
  • A normal mechanical examination with these findings indicates a synovial process.
  • Other joints, a rash or nail changes indicates a systemic disease.
  • In an operated knee it raises low grade infection, however long ago.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva this is a station about recognising a non mechanical problem, and the systemic examination is what demonstrates the recognition.

The expected points are the effusion, the boggy thickening, the temperature, the negative mechanical examination, the other joints and the systemic questions.

Say that you would distinguish synovial thickening from fluid by whether it moves, which is the practical discriminator.

Say that you would exclude infection in an operated knee before anything else. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

Say that you would refer for a rheumatological assessment rather than arrange an arthroscopy, since that is the clinically correct next step. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the synovium produces the joint's fluid, so an inflamed synovium produces an effusion continuously rather than in response to an event. 4.8.1 covers normal fluid physiology.

Inflamed synovium is thickened and vascular, which is why it is both palpable and warm, and why those two findings travel together.

The pain of synovitis is present at rest and in the morning rather than on loading, which is the clinical discriminator from mechanical pain. 4.2.13 covers night pain.

A chronically inflamed synovium damages cartilage over time, which is why the diagnosis matters beyond the immediate symptoms and why delay has a cost. 4.9.11 covers one consequence.

How Good It Is

The clinical recognition of synovitis is reliable when the thickening is substantial and poor when it is subtle, which means a normal examination does not exclude it.

Its most valuable use is the negative mechanical examination, which is what should stop the surgical pathway. 4.6.1 covers palpation.

It cannot distinguish the causes, which aspiration, blood tests and imaging do between them, and this is a presentation where the investigations lead. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

Magnetic resonance imaging shows synovial thickening well, and this is one of the clearer cases where the imaging adds a diagnosis the examination suspects. 4.1.6 covers it.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • The synovium produces the fluid, so an inflamed synovium produces an effusion continuously.
  • Inflamed synovium is thickened and vascular, so it is palpable and warm together.
  • The pain is present at rest and in the morning rather than on loading.
  • A chronically inflamed synovium damages cartilage, so delay has a cost.
  • A normal examination does not exclude subtle synovitis.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level recognising synovitis moves a patient out of the surgical pathway, and failing to recognise it is the commonest route to a futile operation in this chapter.

A knee with synovitis and no mechanical findings should not be arthroscoped, and the examination is what establishes that. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

A knee with synovitis after an arthroplasty or a reconstruction needs infection excluded, and that exclusion precedes any revision. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A knee with synovitis and genuine mechanical findings presents a judgement, because treating the mechanical cause may not settle the effusion and the patient should be told so. 4.9.11 covers one such knee.

Synovectomy has a place in specific diagnoses and is not an answer to a non specific synovitis, which is a distinction the examination cannot make and the investigations can.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is arthroscoping a synovitic knee with no mechanical findings.

The second is not feeling for the thickening, so synovitis is recorded as an effusion.

The third is not examining the other joints.

The fourth is attributing synovitis in an operated knee to activity without excluding infection.

The fifth is treating a mechanical finding in a synovitic knee as the whole explanation, which sets an expectation the operation will not meet.

Key Learning Points

  • Failing to recognise synovitis is the commonest route to a futile operation in this chapter.
  • A knee with synovitis and no mechanical findings should not be arthroscoped.
  • Synovitis after an arthroplasty or reconstruction needs infection excluded first.
  • Treating a mechanical finding in a synovitic knee may not settle the effusion.
  • Not feeling for the thickening records synovitis as an effusion.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice synovitis is the diagnosis that explains the knee with several normal arthroscopies.

A patient with a recurrent effusion, a boggy synovium and three previous arthroscopies has had a rheumatological problem treated surgically, and the examination at each visit would have said so. 4.8.8 covers chronic effusions.

A patient with synovitis after a reconstruction has either infection, a reaction to the graft or fixation, or an inflammatory process, and the sequence of exclusion matters. 4.6.2 covers temperature.

A patient with localised rather than generalised synovitis raises a focal process, which is a different differential and includes conditions that need specific treatment.

A patient with a haemorrhagic synovitis has a different differential again, and the aspiration rather than the examination makes that distinction. 4.8.7 covers the acute haemarthrosis.

In medicolegal work synovitis attributed to an injury is difficult to support, and the distinction between a post traumatic and a systemic process is usually the contested point. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

Key Learning Points

  • Synovitis explains the knee with several normal arthroscopies.
  • Synovitis after a reconstruction is infection, a reaction, or an inflammatory process, in that order of exclusion.
  • Localised rather than generalised synovitis raises a focal process.
  • A haemorrhagic synovitis has its own differential, distinguished by aspiration.
  • The distinction between a post traumatic and a systemic process is the usual contested point.
