Deep MCL Assessment

Page 4.12.4 · Medial Knee Examination · examination page

The structure no test isolates, and the one whose injury explains why a medial knee can be painful and stable at once.

Deep MCL Assessment

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

The deep medial ligament lies beneath the superficial one and attaches to the medial meniscus above and below it, so assessing it means assessing the meniscus and the joint line together.

No clinical test isolates it. Valgus stress reads the superficial ligament because that is the structure resisting most of the load, and the deep ligament contributes too little to show up as a separate grade. 3.9.4 covers its function.

What the deep ligament does contribute is rotational restraint and a fixed relationship between the meniscus and the tibia, which is why its injury presents as pain and a rotational complaint rather than as valgus laxity. 3.9.2 covers rotational stability.

How It Is Performed

Palpate the medial joint line directly, from the anterior horn of the meniscus round towards the posteromedial corner, with the knee flexed to about ninety degrees.

Note where the tenderness sits. Tenderness at the joint line itself is the deep ligament or the meniscus; tenderness above or below it along the ligament's course is the superficial ligament.

Perform valgus stress at thirty degrees and in extension, and note that a deep injury alone does not increase the opening at either. 4.12.1 covers the test.

Add rotation. Hold the knee flexed and rotate the tibia externally, and ask whether that reproduces the pain rather than whether it opens the joint.

Examine the meniscus deliberately, because the two structures are attached and the examination cannot separate them on laxity alone. 4.12.7 covers that separation.

What a Positive Means

Joint line tenderness with no increase in valgus opening indicates the deep ligament or the meniscus and not the superficial ligament.

Pain on external rotation of the flexed tibia points the same way, because the deep ligament and the meniscal attachments resist that movement.

A knee with joint line tenderness, normal valgus stress and a positive meniscal test is a meniscal injury, and the deep ligament is part of the same anatomical complex rather than a separate diagnosis. 4.9.2 covers joint line tenderness.

The clinical conclusion to record is a posteromedial soft tissue injury rather than a named structure, because the examination cannot be more specific than that and pretending otherwise is the error here.

Key Learning Points

  • No clinical test isolates the deep medial ligament, and the examination should not claim to.
  • It attaches to the meniscus above and below, so the two are assessed together.
  • Its contribution is rotational restraint rather than valgus restraint.
  • Joint line tenderness with normal valgus opening points at the deep ligament or the meniscus.
  • Record a posteromedial soft tissue injury rather than a named structure.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva the right answer begins by saying that the structure cannot be isolated, and a candidate who describes a test for it has misunderstood the anatomy.

The expected points are the palpation sequence along the joint line, the absence of a valgus finding, the rotational pain, and the meniscal examination.

Say that the deep ligament has meniscofemoral and meniscotibial parts and that tenderness at the meniscotibial level is the more useful localisation. 3.9.4 covers the anatomy of its function.

Say that you would examine for a Segond type lateral finding and for the medial equivalent if the mechanism was a twisting valgus injury, since these injuries travel together. 3.11.6 covers the lateral lesion.

Say that magnetic resonance imaging identifies the deep ligament where the examination cannot, and that this is one of the places where imaging genuinely adds rather than confirms. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the deep ligament is a capsular thickening rather than a discrete cord, so it fails over a length and produces pain rather than a measurable laxity.

Its meniscotibial part holds the meniscus to the tibia, and its disruption allows the meniscus to move abnormally, which is the mechanism behind a meniscal extrusion rather than a tear. 3.9.4 covers its function.

That mechanism explains why a patient can have a painful, mechanically abnormal medial compartment with an entirely normal valgus examination, which is otherwise a confusing presentation.

A deep injury with a superficial injury is the usual combination, and the examination will read the superficial one and say nothing about the deep one. 4.12.3 covers that grade.

How Good It Is

The clinical examination for this structure is poor, and saying so is the accurate position rather than a failure of technique.

Palpation localises to the joint line reliably and cannot distinguish the ligament from the meniscus, because the two are the same tissue complex at that point.

Rotational pain is suggestive and not specific, since it is positive in meniscal tears, in early degenerative change and in posteromedial corner injury.

Magnetic resonance imaging is the only assessment that identifies the structure directly, and this is the clearest case in the chapter where the examination should defer to it.

No figures appear for any of these comparisons, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • It is a capsular thickening rather than a cord, so it fails over a length and gives pain, not laxity.
  • The meniscotibial part holds the meniscus to the tibia, and its loss allows extrusion.
  • That explains a painful, mechanically abnormal compartment with a normal valgus examination.
  • Palpation cannot distinguish the ligament from the meniscus, because they are one complex there.
  • This is the clearest case in the chapter where the examination defers to imaging.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the deep ligament rarely changes a plan on its own, and it changes two plans when it is recognised.

The first is meniscal repair. A meniscus that has lost its tibial attachment is a different repair from a tear within the body, and the examination's contribution is to raise the question rather than answer it. 4.9.2 covers joint line tenderness.

The second is a medial reconstruction, where the deep ligament's reconstruction is a separate decision from the superficial one and rests on the rotational examination rather than the valgus grade. 4.12.6 covers that pattern.

Outside those two, the structure is part of the injury complex and is treated as such.

The honest position for a clinic letter is a posteromedial injury with the findings listed, because a letter claiming a deep ligament injury from clinical findings alone is claiming more than the examination supports.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is naming the structure from a normal valgus test and joint line tenderness, which is a guess presented as a finding.

The second is attributing joint line tenderness to the ligament and stopping, which misses a meniscal tear that needs treatment.

The third is the reverse: attributing everything to the meniscus and missing a meniscotibial separation, where the meniscus itself is intact and the attachment is not.

The fourth is expecting the deep ligament to show up as a valgus grade. It does not, and waiting for it to is how the injury gets missed.

Key Learning Points

  • The structure changes two plans: a meniscal repair and the content of a medial reconstruction.
  • A letter claiming a deep ligament injury from clinical findings alone claims more than the examination supports.
  • Attributing joint line tenderness to the ligament and stopping misses a meniscal tear.
  • Attributing everything to the meniscus misses a meniscotibial separation.
  • It does not show up as a valgus grade, and waiting for it to is how it gets missed.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice this structure appears as the explanation for a medial compartment that hurts out of proportion to everything measurable about it.

Meniscal extrusion with an intact meniscus is the finding that most often sits behind that presentation, and it is a meniscotibial problem rather than a meniscal one. The examination finds the tenderness and the imaging names it. 4.1.6 covers that division.

A patient with a repaired meniscus and persistent posteromedial pain may have an unaddressed meniscotibial separation, and that possibility is the reason to examine the joint line posteriorly rather than only at the mid point.

In a medial reconstruction the deep ligament's role in rotational control means that a knee reconstructed for valgus laxity alone can remain rotationally lax, and the patient's complaint of the knee opening on turning survives a technically sound operation. 4.12.8 covers that assessment.

In early medial compartment degeneration the same joint line tenderness appears, and separating a structural posteromedial injury from early wear is a judgement on age, mechanism and radiographs rather than an examination finding. 4.11.6 covers a parallel problem.

In medicolegal work the honest statement is that the examination cannot identify this structure, and an opinion that claims it can is vulnerable for that reason. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

Key Learning Points

  • Meniscal extrusion with an intact meniscus is a meniscotibial problem, not a meniscal one.
  • Persistent posteromedial pain after a meniscal repair may be an unaddressed separation.
  • A knee reconstructed for valgus laxity alone can stay rotationally lax.
  • Early medial compartment wear produces the same joint line tenderness.
  • The honest medicolegal statement is that the examination cannot identify this structure.
