Cartilage Surgery

Page 4.20.5 · Examination of the Post-operative Knee · examination page

Examining a knee after a cartilage lesion has been treated, where the result takes longer to declare itself than any other knee operation.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Cartilage Surgery

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

After cartilage surgery the examination is checking the effusion, the range, the loading pain and the alignment, because those are what the result shows itself through. 4.17.5 covers articular cartilage defects.

The examination is read against a long timeline, since the repair tissue matures over many months. 4.2.34 covers previous rehabilitation.

It matters that the lesion's cause is usually still present, so the alignment and the meniscal state are examined as part of the follow up. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

How It Is Performed

Establish what was done, where, how large the lesion was and when. 4.2.30 covers previous surgery.

Look at the scars and ask about any concurrent osteotomy or meniscal procedure. 4.3.7 covers surgical scars.

Assess the effusion, since a recurrent activity related effusion is the common finding when the result is not holding. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Measure the range, recording any restriction, because stiffness after cartilage surgery is common. 4.7.10 covers the post operative range assessment.

Assess the painful arc, since a treated lesion is loaded at a particular flexion angle. 4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

Palpate the compartment joint line and the patellofemoral joint, depending on the lesion's site. 4.6.4 covers joint line tenderness.

Assess the alignment, because the load that produced the lesion has not changed unless it was corrected. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

Assess function, because the patient's judgement of the result is functional rather than structural. 4.7.9 covers the functional range.

What a Positive Means

A settled knee with a full range, no effusion and reduced loading pain is the expected result, and it takes many months.

A recurrent activity related effusion with pain at the same arc suggests the treated area is not tolerating load. 4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

Stiffness without an effusion is a rehabilitation problem rather than a failure of the graft or the repair tissue. 4.7.5 covers the end feel.

Compartment pain with a malaligned limb suggests the load was never addressed. 4.18.3 covers the varus worn knee.

A mechanical symptom after cartilage surgery raises a loose fragment or a delaminated graft. 4.17.2 covers mechanical symptoms.

Key Learning Points

  • The result takes many months to declare itself.
  • A recurrent activity related effusion is the common sign of a result not holding.
  • Stiffness without an effusion is a rehabilitation problem.
  • The lesion's cause persists unless the alignment was corrected.
  • A mechanical symptom raises a loose fragment or a delaminated graft.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva this is the post cartilage surgery station and the expected answer knows the timeline and examines the cause.

The expected points are the procedure and its date, the lesion's site and size, the scars, any concurrent procedure, the effusion, the range, the painful arc, the compartment, the alignment and the function.

Say that you would read the examination against a long timeline, since the repair tissue matures slowly. 4.2.9 covers the onset.

Say that you would examine the alignment, because the load that caused the lesion persists. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

Say that you would record function, since that is what the patient judges the result by. 4.7.9 covers the functional range.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that repair tissue is mechanically inferior to the cartilage it replaces, so it tolerates less load and takes longer to mature. 3.5.1 covers contact areas.

The load on a treated area depends on the flexion angle, which is why the painful arc localises the lesion and follows it. 3.5.4 covers the effects of flexion.

A malaligned limb concentrates load on the treated compartment, which is why correction accompanies treatment of a lesion in a malaligned knee. 3.5.6 covers the effects of malalignment.

A meniscus deficient compartment loads a treated lesion more, which is why the meniscal state is part of the assessment. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

How Good It Is

The examination follows the knee's symptomatic recovery reliably and says nothing about the repair tissue.

Its best contribution is the effusion and the painful arc together, which track whether the treated area is tolerating load. 4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

It cannot assess graft integration, fill or delamination.

Magnetic resonance imaging assesses the repair tissue, so the examination establishes the tolerance and the imaging the structure. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • Repair tissue tolerates less load than the cartilage it replaces.
  • The load depends on the flexion angle, which is why the arc follows the lesion.
  • A malaligned limb concentrates load on the treated compartment.
  • A meniscus deficient compartment loads a treated lesion more.
  • The examination tracks tolerance and not structure.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the examination separates a maturing result, a failing one, stiffness and an unaddressed cause.

A recurrent effusion with pain at the lesion's arc after the expected maturation period suggests the treatment has not held. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Stiffness with a quiet joint is a rehabilitation problem, and the end feel distinguishes a contracture from a block. 4.7.5 covers the end feel.

Compartment pain with an uncorrected malalignment is a load problem, and that is a different operation. 4.20.4 covers the osteotomy assessment.

A mechanical symptom raises a delaminated graft or a loose body, which is urgent. 4.17.2 covers mechanical symptoms.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is judging the result too early, since the repair tissue has not matured. 4.2.9 covers the onset.

An effusion is read as failure when it follows an increase in activity during rehabilitation. 4.8.3 covers the bulge sign.

Pain at a different arc from the lesion is attributed to the treated area. 4.7.6 covers the painful arc.

The alignment is omitted, so a load problem is called a treatment failure. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

A concurrent meniscal or ligament procedure is not accounted for, and all the findings are assigned to the cartilage. 4.20.2 covers the meniscal repair assessment.

Key Learning Points

  • Judging the result too early is the commonest error.
  • An effusion after an activity increase is not automatically failure.
  • Pain at a different arc is not the treated area.
  • Omitting the alignment turns a load problem into a treatment failure.
  • A concurrent procedure makes attribution unreliable.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the examination's limitation is that the question is structural and the findings are symptomatic.

Repair tissue quality determines the durability and is invisible to the examination, so the clinical result and the structural one can diverge for years. 4.17.5 covers articular cartilage defects.

The lesion's size and containment determine the expected result, and the examination does not measure either. 4.17.3 covers osteochondral lesions.

Symptoms from the treated lesion, the surrounding cartilage and the subchondral bone are not separable clinically, and each has a different implication. 4.17.1 covers the cartilage presentation.

A concurrent osteotomy changes every alignment finding and much of the symptom pattern, so the cartilage result is harder to isolate afterwards. 4.20.4 covers that assessment.

Function and structure diverge in both directions here, so a satisfied patient with a failing repair and a dissatisfied one with a good repair both occur. 4.7.9 covers the functional range.

Key Learning Points

  • Repair tissue quality is invisible to the examination.
  • Lesion size and containment govern the result and are not clinically measurable.
  • Lesion, surrounding cartilage and subchondral pain are not separable clinically.
  • A concurrent osteotomy makes the cartilage result harder to isolate.
  • Function and structure diverge in both directions.
