Hyperextension

Page 4.5.5 · Alignment Assessment · examination page

A knee that goes beyond straight, which is normal in many people and a posterolateral corner sign in a few.

Hyperextension

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

Hyperextension is extension beyond the neutral position, and assessing it in an alignment examination asks whether it is symmetrical and whether the knee is rotating as it goes.

Symmetrical hyperextension is usually constitutional and is common, particularly in women and in people with generalised hypermobility. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

Asymmetrical hyperextension, especially with external rotation of the tibia, points at the posterolateral corner, which resists both. 4.13.7 covers the test built on it.

How It Is Performed

Stand the patient up with the feet together and look from the side at knee level, comparing the two knees. 4.3.3 covers standing alignment.

Lay the patient supine, lift each leg by the heel in turn, and allow the knee to hang, comparing how far each goes. 4.13.7 covers that test.

Note whether the tibia rotates externally as the knee hyperextends, because the rotation is what distinguishes a corner sign from hypermobility.

Measure the hyperextension in degrees on each side and record both figures. 4.1.8 covers the record.

Assess the other joints for hypermobility, since a generalised pattern changes the interpretation entirely. 4.23.3 covers it.

What a Positive Means

Symmetrical hyperextension of a few degrees is normal and should be recorded as the patient's baseline rather than as a finding.

Asymmetrical hyperextension on the injured side indicates posterolateral corner deficiency. 4.13.4 covers that assessment.

Hyperextension with external rotation of the tibia is the characteristic corner sign and is more specific than hyperextension alone. 4.13.7 covers it.

Hyperextension in a knee with a cruciate injury indicates a larger injury, since the cruciates contribute to resisting it. 4.10.4 covers the Lachman test.

Progressive hyperextension developing over years indicates a corner that is stretching, and that trajectory is a stronger argument for treatment than a single reading. 4.13.8 covers combined injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • Symmetrical hyperextension is usually constitutional and common.
  • Asymmetrical hyperextension points at the posterolateral corner.
  • The external rotation of the tibia is what distinguishes a corner sign from hypermobility.
  • Hyperextension with a cruciate injury indicates a larger injury.
  • Progressive hyperextension over years indicates a corner that is stretching.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva hyperextension is a trap, because the expected answer establishes that it is bilateral before treating it as pathology.

The expected points are the standing comparison, the supine heel lift, the rotation of the tibia, the measurement on both sides, and the assessment for generalised hypermobility.

Say that you would examine the other knee first, since the comparison is the test. 4.1.2 covers the principle.

Say that you would assess the elbows, thumbs and spine for hypermobility, which takes seconds and changes the interpretation. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

Say that hyperextension is also a consequence of a chronic posterior cruciate or corner deficiency rather than only a sign of an acute one. 4.11.6 covers that progression.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the posterior capsule and the posterolateral structures tighten in extension, so they are the restraint to hyperextension and their loss permits it. 3.10.6 covers the corner.

The same structures resist external rotation, which is why the two appear together and why the combination is more specific than either. 3.10.2 covers external rotation stability.

The cruciates contribute to the restraint, so hyperextension in a cruciate deficient knee is expected to be greater and that is a severity marker rather than a separate diagnosis. 3.11.5 covers internal rotation control.

Generalised hypermobility produces the same appearance without any injury, which is why the systemic assessment is part of the examination rather than an optional extra. 4.23.3 covers it.

How Good It Is

Hyperextension is easy to see, easy to measure and frequently over interpreted, which is an unusual combination of properties.

It is specific when it is asymmetrical and with external rotation, and non specific otherwise, so the qualifiers carry the whole finding.

It is reproducible as a measurement, which makes it one of the better findings to follow over time in a corner injury. 4.13.7 covers that use.

It cannot grade a corner injury, which the dial test does, so its place is as a corroborating finding. 4.13.5 covers the dial test.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • The posterior capsule and posterolateral structures are the restraint to hyperextension.
  • The same structures resist external rotation, which is why the combination is more specific.
  • Hyperextension in a cruciate deficient knee is a severity marker rather than a separate diagnosis.
  • Generalised hypermobility produces the same appearance without injury.
  • It is reproducible over time and cannot grade a corner injury.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level hyperextension contributes to the corner assessment and to the question of whether a reconstruction will be over constrained or under constrained.

A hyperextending knee tensions a graft differently from a neutral one, which is why the finding matters in planning rather than only in diagnosis. 3.10.8 covers reconstruction biomechanics.

Asymmetrical hyperextension in a knee listed for a cruciate reconstruction is a reason to assess the corner properly before operating. 4.13.4 covers that assessment.

A recurvatum deformity standing moves the decision towards a bony correction, since a soft tissue reconstruction will not hold a knee that hyperextends structurally. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

In generalised hypermobility the finding is a reason to expect a different outcome rather than a reason to operate differently, and that belongs in the consent. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating symmetrical hyperextension as pathology.

The second is measuring one side.

The third is reading hyperextension without the rotation as a corner sign.

The fourth is missing generalised hypermobility, which takes seconds to establish and changes the interpretation of the whole examination.

The fifth is lifting by the ankle rather than the heel or toe, which supports the tibia and prevents the rotation appearing. 4.13.7 covers the technique.

Key Learning Points

  • Hyperextension changes how a graft is tensioned, so it matters in planning.
  • Asymmetrical hyperextension before a cruciate reconstruction prompts a corner assessment.
  • A structural recurvatum deformity moves the decision towards bony correction.
  • In generalised hypermobility it predicts a different outcome rather than a different operation.
  • Lifting by the ankle supports the tibia and prevents the rotation appearing.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice hyperextension is most useful as a serial measurement, because it is one of the few findings that is easy to reproduce exactly.

A patient whose hyperextension has increased after a corner reconstruction has a reconstruction that is stretching, and that is clearer than any rotational measurement. 4.13.7 covers it.

A patient with a recurvatum that preceded the injury has a constitutional finding, and attributing it to the injury is wrong in a way that matters for both treatment and attribution. 4.23.7 covers the medicolegal setting.

A knee with hyperextension and a chronically deficient posterior cruciate has a combination that progresses, since each permits the other, and that is an argument for treating it earlier than the complaint alone would justify. 4.11.6 covers that knee.

In a knee with a previous osteotomy or a growth disturbance the hyperextension may be bony, and a soft tissue reconstruction into it fails for reasons the examination can predict. 4.5.1 covers the measurement.

In generalised hypermobility a reconstruction tensioned to the contralateral side reproduces the hyperextension, and tensioning to a neutral target instead is a decision that has to be taken deliberately. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

Key Learning Points

  • It is one of the few findings easy to reproduce exactly, which makes it good to follow.
  • Increasing hyperextension after a corner reconstruction is clearer evidence of stretch than rotation is.
  • A recurvatum preceding the injury is constitutional, and attribution matters.
  • Hyperextension with posterior cruciate deficiency progresses, since each permits the other.
  • In hypermobility, tensioning to the other side reproduces the hyperextension.
