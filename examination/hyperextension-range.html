Hyperextension

Page 4.7.3 · Range of Motion · examination page

The far end of the extension range, measured as a range rather than read as a deformity.

Hyperextension

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

Hyperextension measured as part of the range of motion asks how far the knee goes beyond neutral and whether that is the same on both sides.

The alignment chapter treats hyperextension as a deformity and as a corner sign. This page treats it as a number in the range assessment, which is where it is recorded and compared. 4.5.5 covers the alignment reading.

The distinction matters because a few degrees of hyperextension is normal in most people, so the measurement is only interpretable against the other knee. 4.23.3 covers hypermobile patients.

How It Is Performed

Lay the patient supine with both legs straight and nothing under the knees. 4.3.5 covers the supine assessment.

Measure the active extension first, then the passive extension, and record hyperextension as a negative value or explicitly as hyperextension. 4.7.1 covers active motion.

Lift the leg by the heel and allow the knee to hang, which gives the passive hyperextension under the limb's own weight. 4.13.7 covers the test that uses the same manoeuvre.

Note whether the tibia rotates externally as the knee hyperextends, since that changes the finding from a range to a corner sign. 4.5.5 covers it.

Measure both knees and record both figures, because only the comparison is interpretable. 4.1.8 covers the record.

What a Positive Means

A few degrees of symmetrical hyperextension is normal and is the patient's baseline.

Asymmetrical hyperextension on the injured side indicates posterior or posterolateral deficiency. 4.13.4 covers the corner.

Hyperextension with external rotation of the tibia is a corner sign rather than a range finding. 4.13.7 covers it.

Loss of a previously present hyperextension indicates a developing extension deficit, and in a reconstructed knee it is an early sign of stiffness. 4.7.10 covers post-operative range.

A large symmetrical hyperextension raises generalised hypermobility, which changes the interpretation of every laxity measurement in the examination. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

Key Learning Points

  • A few degrees of symmetrical hyperextension is normal and is the baseline.
  • Record hyperextension explicitly so it is not confused with an extension deficit.
  • Asymmetrical hyperextension indicates posterior or posterolateral deficiency.
  • Loss of a previously present hyperextension is an early sign of stiffness.
  • Large symmetrical hyperextension raises generalised hypermobility.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva the expected answer records hyperextension explicitly and compares the two sides, and a candidate who reports a range as zero to one hundred and thirty has lost the information.

The expected points are the supine position, the active and passive measurements, the heel lift, the rotation of the tibia, and both sides.

Say how you are recording it, since a negative number and the word hyperextension are both used and confusing them inverts the finding. 4.1.8 covers the record.

Say that you would assess generalised hypermobility, which takes seconds and changes the interpretation. 4.23.3 covers it.

Say that hyperextension is a normal variant in many people and a sign in a few, and that the comparison distinguishes them. 4.5.5 covers that argument.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that extension beyond neutral is limited by the posterior capsule, the posterolateral structures and the cruciates together, so increased hyperextension means one of those is deficient. 3.10.6 covers the corner.

Normal variation in hyperextension reflects normal variation in capsular laxity, which is why the figure is useless without the other side. 4.23.3 covers it.

The screw home mechanism locks the knee in terminal extension through rotation, so hyperextension involves rotation and the two findings are related. 3.3.5 covers the mechanism.

A knee that loses hyperextension is losing the last part of its range, which is the part lost first in any stiffening process and therefore the earliest sign. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

How Good It Is

Hyperextension is easy to measure and reproducible, which makes it a good figure to follow.

Its interpretation depends entirely on the comparison, so a single sided measurement is close to useless.

It is the earliest part of the range to be lost, which makes it a sensitive early indicator of stiffness after surgery. 4.7.10 covers it.

It cannot by itself distinguish a corner injury from hypermobility, which the rotation and the systemic assessment do. 4.13.7 covers the rotation.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • Extension beyond neutral is limited by the posterior capsule, the corner and the cruciates together.
  • Normal variation reflects normal capsular laxity, so the other side is essential.
  • The screw home mechanism means hyperextension involves rotation.
  • It is the earliest part of the range to be lost, making it a sensitive indicator of stiffness.
  • It cannot alone distinguish a corner injury from hypermobility.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level hyperextension contributes to graft tensioning, to the detection of early stiffness, and to a corner assessment.

A hyperextending knee tensions a graft differently from a neutral one, so the figure belongs in the planning rather than only in the record. 3.10.8 covers reconstruction biomechanics.

Loss of hyperextension after a reconstruction is the earliest measurable sign of stiffness, and acting on it early is what prevents an established contracture. 4.7.7 covers it.

Asymmetrical hyperextension before an operation prompts the corner assessment, and the dial test grades it. 4.13.5 covers the dial test.

In a hypermobile patient a graft tensioned to the contralateral side reproduces the hyperextension, and tensioning to a neutral target instead is a deliberate decision. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is recording a range starting at zero when the knee hyperextends, which loses the figure.

The second is measuring one side.

The third is reading symmetrical hyperextension as pathology.

The fourth is missing the external rotation that turns the finding into a corner sign. 4.13.7 covers it.

The fifth is treating a small loss of hyperextension after surgery as insignificant, when it is the earliest sign of a problem. 4.7.10 covers it.

Key Learning Points

  • A hyperextending knee tensions a graft differently, so the figure belongs in the planning.
  • Loss of hyperextension after reconstruction is the earliest measurable sign of stiffness.
  • Asymmetrical hyperextension before an operation prompts the corner assessment.
  • In a hypermobile patient the tensioning target is a deliberate decision.
  • Recording a range starting at zero in a hyperextending knee loses the figure.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice hyperextension is most useful as the figure that shows a reconstruction has either stretched or stiffened.

An increase in hyperextension after a corner reconstruction indicates the reconstruction is stretching, and it is easier to reproduce exactly than any rotational measurement. 4.13.7 covers it.

A loss of hyperextension in a reconstructed knee that previously had it is an early contracture, and the referral is often late because the pre-operative figure was never recorded. 4.7.7 covers contractures.

A patient with a hypermobile uninjured knee and a reconstructed knee tensioned to neutral has an asymmetry by design, and that has to be in the notes or it will be read as a failure. 4.23.3 covers those patients.

In a knee with a previous osteotomy or a growth disturbance the hyperextension may be bony, so a soft tissue reconstruction will not control it. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis.

In medicolegal work a recorded pre-injury or pre-operative hyperextension figure is rare, which makes any claim about a changed range hard to support. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

Key Learning Points

  • Increasing hyperextension after a corner reconstruction is easy to reproduce and shows stretch.
  • Losing hyperextension in a knee that previously had it is an early contracture.
  • A knee tensioned to neutral in a hypermobile patient is asymmetrical by design.
  • A bony hyperextension will not be controlled by a soft tissue reconstruction.
  • A recorded pre-operative figure is rare, which makes claims about a changed range hard to support.
