Joint Line Tenderness

Page 4.6.4 · Palpation · examination page

The most useful tender point in the knee and the least specific, because four structures meet there.

Joint Line Tenderness

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Tests

Joint line tenderness is tenderness elicited by pressing directly on the line between the femur and the tibia, with the knee flexed so that the line is open.

Four things meet there: the meniscus, the deep part of the collateral ligament, the capsule and the articular surfaces of both bones. Tenderness belongs to any of them. 2.5.1 covers the medial meniscus.

The meniscal chapter covers this finding as a meniscal test and its performance in that context. This page covers it as a palpation finding and what else it means. 4.9.2 covers the meniscal reading.

How It Is Performed

Flex the knee to about ninety degrees with the foot on the couch, which opens the joint line and brings the anterior horns forward.

Find the joint line with a finger: the soft gap between the femoral condyle above and the tibial plateau below, just either side of the patellar tendon.

Palpate systematically from the tendon round to the posteromedial corner, then from the tendon round laterally, noting the exact position of any tenderness.

Repeat with the knee in a different flexion angle if the tenderness is posterior, since deeper flexion brings the posterior horns more accessible.

Record the location precisely, because anterior, mid and posterior joint line tenderness mean different things. 4.1.8 covers the record.

What a Positive Means

Tenderness exactly on the joint line indicates the meniscus, the deep collateral ligament, the capsule or the joint surface.

Tenderness above or below the line, along a collateral ligament's course, indicates the ligament rather than the joint. 4.12.3 covers the grade it is distinguished from.

Posteromedial joint line tenderness with a twisting mechanism suggests a posterior horn tear, and this is the commonest useful version of the finding. 4.9.2 covers it.

Tenderness that sits more posteriorly than the usual posterior horn position suggests a root or a meniscotibial problem. 4.9.9 covers meniscal root tears.

Diffuse tenderness along the whole joint line in an older patient suggests the joint surface rather than a tear. 4.9.11 covers meniscal examination in osteoarthritis.

Key Learning Points

  • Four structures meet at the joint line, so tenderness there belongs to any of them.
  • Flex the knee to about ninety degrees, which opens the line and brings the anterior horns forward.
  • Tenderness above or below the line along a ligament's course indicates the ligament.
  • Posteromedial tenderness with a twisting mechanism suggests a posterior horn tear.
  • Diffuse tenderness along the whole line in an older patient suggests the joint surface.

MRCS Level

How It Is Performed

In a viva the position of the knee and the precision of the location are the two things that separate a good answer from a mention.

The expected points are the flexed knee, finding the line with a finger, the systematic sweep medially and laterally, the deeper flexion for posterior tenderness, and the precise record.

Say why the knee is flexed, because the reason is the content of the question. 3.3.2 covers roll and glide.

Say that you would distinguish joint line tenderness from ligament tenderness by the level rather than by the side. 4.12.7 covers that separation.

Say that you would combine the finding with a provocation test, since tenderness localises and a provocation test says which structure is loaded. 4.9.3 covers the McMurray test.

What a Positive Means

The reasoning expected is that the joint line is the only place where the meniscus is palpable, so it is the only direct access the examination has to that structure. 2.5.1 covers it.

The deep collateral ligament attaches to the meniscus at the same point, which is why the two cannot be separated by palpation and why the distinction is made by provocation. 3.9.4 covers deep medial function.

Flexing the knee rolls the femur backwards and opens the anterior joint line, which is what brings the anterior horn within reach. 3.3.2 covers the mechanism.

A posterior horn is reached only in deeper flexion, and even then imperfectly, which is why posterior tears are tender more variably than anterior ones. 2.5.7 covers root attachments.

How Good It Is

Joint line tenderness is the most sensitive single clinical finding for a meniscal tear and among the least specific, which is the combination that makes it a screen. 4.9.2 covers its meniscal performance.

Its specificity improves when the location is precise and the mechanism fits, which is why recording the exact position is worth the extra few seconds.

It is unreliable in a knee that is tender everywhere, in an acutely injured knee and in an obese patient. 4.23.4 covers the last of those.

It cannot distinguish the meniscus from the deep ligament or from the joint surface, which is a statement about anatomy rather than about technique. 4.12.4 covers the deep ligament.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 023, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • The joint line is the only place where the meniscus is palpable.
  • The deep collateral ligament attaches to the meniscus there, so palpation cannot separate them.
  • Flexing the knee rolls the femur back and brings the anterior horn within reach.
  • Posterior horns are reached imperfectly even in deep flexion.
  • Specificity improves when the location is precise and the mechanism fits.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the finding contributes to who gets imaged and rarely to who gets operated on, with one exception.

The exception is the acute knee with a locked joint and joint line tenderness, where the combination raises a displaced tear that needs urgent treatment. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

Otherwise the finding narrows the differential and the imaging confirms it, which is a reasonable division. 4.1.6 covers that relationship.

In the chronic knee the pattern of tenderness contributes to whether the problem is a tear or the surface, and that decides the category of treatment. 4.9.11 covers it.

In the post-meniscectomy knee localised tenderness at the site of a previous resection raises an unstable remnant or early compartment change, and the examination cannot separate those two. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is palpating with the knee extended, where the line is closed.

The second is recording tenderness without a position, which discards most of the information.

The third is attributing joint line tenderness to the meniscus by default. 4.12.7 covers the alternatives.

The fourth is reading diffuse tenderness in an older knee as a tear, which leads to arthroscopies that do not help. 4.9.11 covers it.

The fifth is relying on the finding in a knee too painful to localise anything.

Key Learning Points

  • The finding contributes to who gets imaged and rarely to who gets operated on.
  • The exception is a locked knee with joint line tenderness, which raises a displaced tear.
  • In the chronic knee the pattern separates a tear from a surface problem.
  • Localised tenderness after a meniscectomy raises a remnant or early compartment change.
  • Diffuse tenderness in an older knee read as a tear leads to arthroscopies that do not help.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

In referred practice the precise position of joint line tenderness is often the most useful thing in the examination, and it is almost never recorded precisely.

Tenderness sitting posterior to the usual posterior horn position suggests a root avulsion or a meniscotibial separation, both of which are missed by the usual framing and both of which matter. 4.9.9 covers root tears.

Tenderness at the site of a previous partial meniscectomy in a young patient raises early compartment change with a narrow window for joint preservation. 4.9.10 covers meniscal deficiency.

Tenderness along the whole joint line in a knee with a normal arthroscopy raises the surface or a synovial process, and the examination has already said so. 4.8.9 covers synovitis.

In a knee with a meniscal repair persistent localised tenderness at the repair site is expected for months, and distinguishing expected from failed needs the timeline rather than the finding. 4.7.10 covers post-operative assessment.

In medicolegal work tenderness is a reported symptom rather than an observed sign, and that limit should be stated when an opinion rests on it. 4.23.7 covers that setting.

Key Learning Points

  • The precise position is often the most useful thing in the examination and is almost never recorded.
  • Tenderness posterior to the posterior horn suggests a root avulsion or meniscotibial separation.
  • Tenderness at an old meniscectomy site in a young patient raises early compartment change.
  • Whole joint line tenderness with a normal arthroscopy raises the surface or a synovial process.
  • After a repair, persistent local tenderness is expected for months.
