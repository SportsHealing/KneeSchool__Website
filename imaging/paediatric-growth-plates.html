Growth Plates

Page 5.15.2 · Paediatric MRI · imaging page

The one structure whose injury changes the shape of the limb, and it is normally bright and irregular.

Growth Plates

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

The physis is the cartilage plate through which a bone lengthens, and it lies between the metaphysis and the epiphysis until it closes. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

It has its own signal, brighter than bone and irregular in outline, which is what a reader unfamiliar with it mistakes for injury. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

It matters because an injury through it can stop part of the plate growing while the rest continues, so the consequence is a change in the limb's shape rather than a painful knee. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

How It Is Acquired

Cover the whole plate in both femur and tibia, including its periphery where injury concentrates. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

Read it on more than one plane, because an undisplaced line through it follows the plate and hides in the plane that contains it. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Use a fluid sensitive sequence, since marrow oedema either side of the plate is often the only sign. 5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

Look at the perichondral ring at the margin, which is where a bridge forms if one is going to. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Compare with the other side when the question is whether the plate is widened. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

What Normal Looks Like

A continuous plate of uniform thickness, undulating rather than straight. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

Interdigitation of the plate with the metaphysis, which increases towards closure. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Marrow signal either side that matches the rest of the metaphysis. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

Progressive narrowing and then segmental closure as maturity approaches, which is a stage rather than a fusion defect. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Focal widening of the plate, which is the most reliable sign of injury through it. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

A line crossing from metaphysis into epiphysis, which development does not produce. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Marrow oedema confined to one side of the plate at one point. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

A bony bridge spanning the plate, which is the finding that predicts the deformity. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

Loss of the plate's continuity in one region while the rest is open. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

Key Learning Points

  • The physis is cartilage and lengthens the bone.
  • It is normally bright, undulating and continuous.
  • Injury through it can arrest part of the plate.
  • The consequence is the limb's shape rather than the knee's comfort.
  • A bony bridge across it predicts the deformity.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer separates a normal plate from an injured one by continuity and width rather than by brightness.

The expected points are the normal signal, the undulating outline, the focal widening, the crossing line, and the bridge.

Say that the plate is normally the brightest structure in the metaphyseal region. 5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

Say that an undisplaced physeal injury can show only as oedema. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

Say that in a child a ligamentous force is taken by the plate or the attachment before the ligament. 5.15.5 covers paediatric cruciate injury.

How Good It Is

The physis is the structure magnetic resonance imaging adds most to in a child, because it is cartilage and a radiograph shows only the bone either side of it.

Its best contribution is the undisplaced injury, which a film can miss entirely and which carries the growth risk. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Its second contribution is the bridge, which is the finding that converts an injury into a prognosis and which is best seen on cartilage sensitive sequences. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

It is limited because the bridge is a later diagnosis than the injury, so the first study answers what happened and a later one answers what it will cost. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no physeal width, bridge dimension, age at closure or follow up interval appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The plate is cartilage, so a radiograph shows only the bone beside it.
  • The undisplaced injury carries the growth risk and can be invisible on a film.
  • The bridge converts an injury into a prognosis.
  • The bridge is a later diagnosis than the injury.
  • The first study says what happened and a later one what it will cost.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level every paediatric knee injury is read twice, once for the structure and once for the plate, and the second reading changes the plan more often.

An injury crossing the plate is reported with its location around the plate, because the periphery and the centre do not carry the same risk. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

An undisplaced injury with oedema alone is still an injury to the plate and is reported as one. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

A bony avulsion at a ligament attachment in a child is assessed for whether the plate is involved, which is a different question from whether the fragment is displaced. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Surveillance for arrest is a decision taken on the first study and acted on later, so the first report should say whether the plate is at risk. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the normal plate as a fracture. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

A closing plate is reported as abnormal because its outline becomes irregular. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

An undisplaced injury is called normal because nothing is displaced. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

The plate is excluded from the field of view, so the question is not asked. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

A bridge is looked for on the first study, which is too early, and not looked for later, which is when it appears. 5.1.4 covers the imaging pathways.

Key Learning Points

  • The normal plate is reported as a fracture.
  • A closing plate is reported as abnormal.
  • An undisplaced injury is called normal because nothing moved.
  • The plate is left out of the field of view.
  • The bridge is sought too early and not sought later.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the physis is the reason paediatric knee practice differs from adult practice at all, and imaging's contribution is location rather than diagnosis.

Whether a plate will arrest is not an imaging question on the day of injury, so a report that implies it can answer one is wrong in a way that is hard to correct later. 5.1.7 covers evidence-based imaging.

The plate's position constrains every operation around it, which is why a reconstruction in a child is planned from the imaging rather than adapted during it. 5.15.5 covers paediatric cruciate injury.

A physis crossed by a tunnel, a screw or a drill is a surgical decision informed by the study, and the useful report describes the plate's state and geometry rather than recommending the approach. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Serial imaging is the only thing that answers the question that matters, and the first report's job is to make the comparison possible by describing the plate precisely enough to be matched. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Key Learning Points

  • The physis is why paediatric practice differs from adult practice.
  • Imaging contributes location rather than prognosis on the day.
  • A report implying it can predict arrest is hard to correct later.
  • The plate's geometry constrains the operation.
  • The first report's job is to make the later comparison possible.
