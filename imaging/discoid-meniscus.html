Discoid Meniscus

Page 5.15.4 · Paediatric MRI · imaging page

A shape rather than a disease, which becomes a diagnosis when it tears or will not stay where it belongs.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Discoid Meniscus

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A discoid meniscus covers more of the tibial plateau than a normal one and is thicker through its body, most often laterally. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

It is a developmental shape that the child is born with rather than something acquired. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

It matters because the shape itself needs nothing, and the tear and the instability that the shape predisposes to are what present. 4.19.3 covers the discoid meniscus examination.

How It Is Acquired

Read the meniscus on sequential coronal images and count how far it extends towards the centre of the plateau. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

Read the sagittal images for the continuity of the body across slices, which is the feature that distinguishes the shape. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Assess the peripheral attachments, since instability rather than shape is the operative question. 5.6.10 covers the ramp lesions.

Use a fluid sensitive sequence for a tear within the thickened body. 5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

Examine the other knee, because the shape is sometimes bilateral. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

What Normal Looks Like

A normal meniscus tapering to a free edge with a crescent outline on the plateau. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

A discoid meniscus with no tear, which is a shape and not a finding that requires anything. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

Uniform internal signal within the thickened body, even though the body is thick. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Intact peripheral attachments holding the meniscus at the joint margin. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A tear within the thickened body, which is what usually brings the child in. 5.6.3 covers the horizontal tears.

Displacement of the meniscus or a fragment of it into the joint. 5.6.6 covers the bucket handle tears.

Loss of peripheral attachment with abnormal position of the meniscus. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Cartilage change on the covered compartment, which follows an abnormal load distribution. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

Cystic change within the meniscus accompanying a tear. 5.7.7 covers the ganglion cysts.

Key Learning Points

  • A discoid meniscus covers more of the plateau and is thicker.
  • It is a developmental shape rather than an acquired condition.
  • The shape alone requires nothing.
  • A tear within the thick body is what usually presents.
  • Instability rather than shape is the operative question.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer separates the shape, the tear and the instability as three findings rather than one diagnosis.

The expected points are the coronal extent, the sagittal continuity, the peripheral attachment, the tear, and the contralateral knee.

Say that the shape is described and the tear is diagnosed. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

Say that a thickened meniscus carries internal signal more readily, which is a source of over-reporting. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Say that the peripheral attachment decides whether the meniscus is stable. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging identifies the shape reliably and reports the tear less reliably, which is the opposite of the order a referrer expects.

Its best contribution is the shape, which is a geometric finding and needs no judgement once the planes are read together. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

It is limited on the tear, because a thickened meniscus carries more internal signal and the distinction between signal and a tear is the same distinction that troubles adult meniscal imaging. 5.19.5 covers the meniscal imaging.

It is limited on instability, since the attachments are assessed statically and the instability is a movement. 5.6.10 covers the ramp lesions.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no meniscal coverage proportion, body thickness, signal grade or age appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The shape is identified reliably and the tear less so.
  • The shape is geometric and needs no judgement.
  • A thickened meniscus carries more internal signal.
  • Signal against tear is the same problem as in the adult meniscus.
  • Instability is a movement assessed on a static study.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the operation preserves meniscal tissue in a child, so the report's job is to say how much tissue there is and what state it is in.

The extent of the thickened tissue and the location of the tear within it decide what can be saved. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

The peripheral attachment decides whether stabilisation is needed alongside, and it is reported separately from the tear. 5.6.10 covers the ramp lesions.

Cartilage change in the covered compartment is a reason to act sooner and is easily omitted from a report focused on the meniscus. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

A symptomless discoid shape found incidentally is a reason to say so plainly, because the word discoid in a report invites intervention on its own. 5.1.4 covers the imaging pathways.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting internal signal in a thickened body as a tear. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

The shape is reported as a diagnosis rather than as a shape. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

The peripheral attachment is not assessed, so instability is not addressed. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

The covered compartment's cartilage is not examined. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

A bilateral shape is reported as bilateral pathology. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

Key Learning Points

  • Internal signal in a thick body is reported as a tear.
  • The shape is reported as a diagnosis.
  • The peripheral attachment is not assessed.
  • The covered compartment's cartilage is not examined.
  • A bilateral shape is called bilateral pathology.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this page is about the wording of a report rather than about an appearance, because the appearance is usually correct and the wording drives the referral.

A report that names the shape and stops leaves the referrer to infer a problem, and a report that says the shape is present without a tear closes the question. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The tissue is irreplaceable in a child, so an operation justified by signal rather than by a tear costs the meniscus that the shape at least provided. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

The child who has symptoms and a discoid meniscus with no tear is the hardest version, and the scan contributes by excluding the things it can exclude rather than by explaining the symptom. 5.1.2 covers clinical examination versus imaging.

The useful report gives the extent, the tear with its location, the peripheral attachment, the compartment's cartilage and the other knee. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

Key Learning Points

  • The appearance is usually right and the wording drives the referral.
  • Naming the shape and stopping invites an inference.
  • The tissue is irreplaceable, so an operation on signal costs the meniscus.
  • A symptomatic knee with an untorn discoid meniscus is the hardest version.
  • The scan excludes rather than explains the symptom.
