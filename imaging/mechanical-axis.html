Mechanical Axis

Page 5.3.7 · Plain Radiography · imaging page

The line from hip centre to ankle centre, and where it crosses the knee.

Mechanical Axis

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

The mechanical axis is the straight line from the centre of the femoral head to the centre of the ankle. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Where that line crosses the knee indicates which compartment carries more of the load. 3.13.1 covers the mechanical axis.

It matters because load distribution is what drives compartment wear, so this line connects the radiograph to the patient's future. 3.13.8 covers alignment and osteoarthritis.

How It Is Acquired

Identify the femoral head centre and the ankle centre on a standing long leg film. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Draw the line between them and note where it passes relative to the knee centre. 3.13.1 covers the mechanical axis.

Describe the deviation by direction, medial for varus and lateral for valgus, rather than by a remembered number. 3.13.3 covers varus alignment.

Separate the mechanical axis from the anatomical axis, which is a different line with a different purpose. 5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

Check the positioning conditions before trusting the result. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

What Normal Looks Like

A line passing close to the centre of the knee, through or near the intercondylar region.

Hip, knee and ankle centres that are identifiable rather than estimated.

A joint line roughly perpendicular to the axis. 5.3.9 covers the joint line obliquity.

Symmetry with the other limb, where both are imaged. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A line passing medial to the knee centre, which loads the medial compartment. 3.13.3 covers varus alignment.

A line passing lateral to the knee centre, which loads the lateral compartment. 3.13.4 covers valgus alignment.

Compartment changes on the loaded side, which is the finding that makes the axis clinically real. 5.3.20 covers the alignment changes.

An axis deviation with the joint line oblique rather than perpendicular. 5.3.9 covers the joint line obliquity.

Key Learning Points

  • The axis runs from femoral head centre to ankle centre.
  • Where it crosses the knee indicates which compartment is loaded.
  • Deviation is described by direction rather than by a remembered number.
  • The mechanical axis is a different line from the anatomical axis.
  • A deviation matters most when the compartment shows the matching change.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer defines the line, says what it means and names the positioning conditions.

The expected points are the two centres, the crossing point, the direction, the distinction from the anatomical axis, and the positioning.

Say that it requires a long standing film. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Say that the deviation direction names the loaded compartment. 3.13.3 covers varus alignment.

Say that deviation and compartment change should agree, and that a disagreement is informative. 5.3.20 covers the alignment changes.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that load passes roughly along this line, so moving the line towards a compartment increases that compartment's share. 3.4.9 covers the force transmission.

Increased load accelerates wear in that compartment, which is why deviation and compartment loss usually match. 3.13.8 covers alignment and osteoarthritis.

Wear then increases the deviation, so alignment and arthritis reinforce each other and the direction of causation cannot be read off one film. 5.3.20 covers the alignment changes.

Realignment works by moving the line, which is why the measurement is the plan. 3.13.9 covers the osteotomy biomechanics.

How Good It Is

The mechanical axis is the single most useful measurement on a knee radiograph series and is only as good as the film it is drawn on.

Its best contribution is identifying the loaded compartment and quantifying by how much, which no clinical assessment does reliably. 4.5.1 covers the mechanical axis assessment.

It is limited by being coronal only, by being a projection, and by not distinguishing deformity that developed from deformity that was always there. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no axis deviation values or normal ranges appear either: those are published quantities and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Load passes roughly along the line, so moving it changes compartment share.
  • Deviation and compartment loss usually match.
  • Wear increases the deviation, so causation cannot be read off one film.
  • Realignment works by moving the line.
  • The measurement is coronal only and cannot date the deformity.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this measurement decides whether realignment is part of the operation.

Varus with medial compartment loss and a correctable deformity is the osteotomy picture, and the axis is what establishes the first two. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Varus with a cruciate deficient knee is a mechanical cause of graft loading, so the axis belongs in a revision assessment. 5.7.14 covers revision cruciate imaging.

A patient's own pre-arthritic alignment is now a planning target, which makes the contralateral limb and the constitutional question part of the assessment. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

Realigning without addressing the compartment, or the reverse, is the decision the axis plus the compartment views settle together. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is estimating the axis from a short film. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

A rotated film is measured and the deviation is wrong in an unknown direction. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Constitutional varus is treated as acquired deformity. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

The deviation is corrected without locating where the deformity is. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

The sagittal and rotational components are ignored because this measurement is coronal. 5.16.3 covers rotational profiles.

Key Learning Points

  • The axis is estimated from a short film.
  • A rotated film gives a wrong deviation in an unknown direction.
  • Constitutional varus is treated as acquired.
  • The deviation is corrected without locating the deformity.
  • The sagittal and rotational components are ignored.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the mechanical axis is a well defined measurement whose clinical targets have moved while the measurement stayed the same.

Neutral alignment was a single target for arthroplasty and is now one option among several, so the same measurement supports different plans depending on the philosophy applied to it. 3.13.10 covers the alignment correction principles.

Constitutional varus means a deviation can be normal for a patient, and no film distinguishes a lifelong deviation from an acquired one without an older image. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

Standing alignment includes compartment laxity, so part of what is measured is soft tissue rather than bone, and a stress view separates them. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

Dynamic alignment during gait differs from static standing alignment and predicts compartment load better, and it is measured in gait laboratories rather than in clinics. 3.13.7 covers the dynamic alignment.

The honest summary is that the measurement is reliable, the target is contested, and the quantity that matters most is the one least often measured. 3.13.8 covers alignment and osteoarthritis.

Key Learning Points

  • Neutral alignment is now one target among several.
  • A deviation can be constitutional, and one film cannot date it.
  • Standing alignment includes compartment laxity as well as bone.
  • Dynamic alignment predicts load better and is rarely measured.
  • The measurement is reliable and the target is contested.
