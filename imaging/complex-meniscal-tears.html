Complex Tears

Page 5.6.8 · Meniscal MRI · imaging page

A meniscus torn in more than one plane, which is the end state of degeneration rather than a distinct injury.

Complex Tears

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A complex tear combines two or more patterns in the same meniscus, most often a horizontal split with a radial or flap component. 5.6.3 covers horizontal tears.

It is a degenerate finding, so it appears in older knees alongside cartilage loss rather than after a single event. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

It matters because the description stops being useful at this point: what matters is whether anything displaces and what the compartment looks like. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

How It Is Acquired

Assess the meniscus in both planes and establish how many distinct tear planes are present. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Identify whether any component reaches the capsular margin, since a complete radial component changes the mechanics. 5.6.5 covers radial tears.

Look for displaced tissue in the notch, the gutter and beneath the body, because a complex tear has more ways to displace. 5.6.7 covers flap tears.

Measure extrusion, which in this setting is more informative than the tear description. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Assess the cartilage and the bone in the compartment, because they are the reason this meniscus looks like this. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Signal running in more than one direction within the same horn, with the meniscus losing its triangular profile.

A meniscus that is shortened, thinned or fragmented rather than cleanly split.

Extrusion beyond the tibial margin, which usually accompanies this appearance. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Cartilage loss, subchondral signal change and osteophyte in the same compartment, which is the setting this tear belongs to. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

Key Learning Points

  • A complex tear combines two or more patterns in one meniscus.
  • It is a degenerate finding rather than a single injury.
  • The meniscus loses its triangular profile.
  • Extrusion is more informative here than the tear description.
  • It belongs with cartilage loss in the same compartment.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the degenerate knee and the expected answer stops describing the tear and starts describing the compartment.

The expected points are the number of planes, any complete radial component, displaced tissue, extrusion, and the cartilage and bone.

Say that a complex tear is a degenerate finding and that the compartment is the subject. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

Say that you would report extrusion. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Say that the tear's description does not decide the management here. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that degeneration weakens the collagen throughout, so failure occurs in several planes rather than along one, which is why the pattern is multiplanar. 3.6.3 covers the circumferential fibres.

Once several planes have failed the meniscus no longer carries hoop tension, so it behaves like a meniscus deficient compartment whatever tissue remains. 3.6.2 covers hoop stress.

Extrusion follows, and extrusion rather than morphology is what tracks the compartment's state. 3.6.10 covers meniscal extrusion.

The cartilage loss and the meniscal failure are the same process, which is why separating cause from effect is not possible from the images. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

How Good It Is

The appearance is easy to detect and hard to use, because it names a state rather than a lesion.

Its best contribution is signalling that the compartment rather than the meniscus is the subject, which redirects the assessment. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

It cannot say which component, if any, is producing symptoms. 5.6.7 covers flap tears.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • Degeneration weakens the collagen throughout, so failure is multiplanar.
  • The meniscus stops carrying hoop tension whatever tissue remains.
  • Extrusion tracks the compartment's state better than morphology.
  • Cartilage loss and meniscal failure are the same process.
  • The appearance names a state rather than a lesion.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level a complex tear is usually a reason not to operate on the meniscus, and the imaging's job is to say what else is there.

A complex tear with established cartilage loss is an arthritic compartment, and the meniscal description adds nothing to that decision. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

A displaced component with mechanical symptoms is the exception, and it is the one thing in this appearance worth looking for deliberately. 5.6.7 covers flap tears.

Extrusion with a complex tear marks a compartment that is losing its protection, which is prognostic rather than operative information. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Alignment matters more than the tear, because a malaligned compartment with a failed meniscus has a mechanical problem the meniscus cannot be blamed for. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is operating on the meniscal description in an arthritic compartment. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

A displaced component is missed because the report stops at complex. 5.6.7 covers flap tears.

Extrusion is not reported, so the mechanical state is unrecorded. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

The cartilage is described in less detail than the meniscus, which inverts their importance here. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

A recent injury is accepted as the cause because the patient dates the symptoms to it. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

Key Learning Points

  • Operating on the meniscal description in an arthritic compartment is the commonest error.
  • A report that stops at complex can hide a displaced component.
  • Unreported extrusion leaves the mechanical state unrecorded.
  • Describing the cartilage in less detail than the meniscus inverts their importance.
  • A remembered injury does not make a degenerate pattern traumatic.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the complex tear is where the chapter's descriptive scheme runs out, and recognising that is the useful skill.

The morphological classification was built for tears in otherwise normal menisci and is applied to menisci that have failed throughout, where it describes appearance without predicting anything. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

Extrusion, cartilage state and alignment carry the prognosis, and all three are reported less consistently than the tear pattern. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

A complex tear in a young knee after a significant injury is a genuinely different lesion with the same name, and the age and the history are what separate them. 5.6.4 covers vertical tears.

Partial meniscectomy here removes tissue from a compartment that has already lost its load distribution, which is the mechanical argument the negative literature rests on. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

The one thing worth asking the images is whether anything is displaced, because that is the only component with a clear operative answer. 5.6.7 covers flap tears.

Key Learning Points

  • The classification was built for tears in otherwise normal menisci.
  • Extrusion, cartilage and alignment carry the prognosis and are reported less.
  • A complex tear in a young knee after injury is a different lesion with the same name.
  • Resecting here removes tissue from an already compromised compartment.
  • The question worth asking is whether anything is displaced.
