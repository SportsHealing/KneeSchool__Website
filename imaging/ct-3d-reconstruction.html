3D Reconstruction

Page 5.2.25 · Imaging Physics for Knee Specialists · imaging page

Turning a volume into a surface, which is what surgical planning and printing are built on.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

3D Reconstruction

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A three dimensional reconstruction displays the volume as a surface by deciding which voxels are bone and which are not. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

That decision is a threshold, so the surface is an interpretation of the data rather than a direct image of it. 5.2.22 covers the computed tomography acquisition.

It matters because the surface looks definitive and depends on a setting, which is the same lesson the radiographic pages taught about display. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

How It Is Acquired

An isotropic volume is needed, otherwise the surface is smoother in one direction than another. 5.2.22 covers the computed tomography acquisition.

A threshold separates bone from soft tissue, and moving it thickens or thins every cortex in the model. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional reconstructions.

Segmentation separates the bones from each other, which is manual, automated or both. 5.16.7 covers robotic surgery planning.

The resulting surface can be measured, printed or used to plan an implant position. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

Metal in the volume corrupts the surface locally, so an implant bearing knee needs the artefact reduced version. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

Key Learning Points

  • A surface is produced by deciding which voxels are bone.
  • That decision is a threshold, so the surface is an interpretation.
  • An isotropic volume is needed for an even surface.
  • Moving the threshold thickens or thins every cortex.
  • Metal corrupts the surface locally.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the page behind the answer that a printed model is only as good as its segmentation.

The expected points are the isotropic volume, the threshold, the segmentation, the uses, and metal.

Say that the threshold changes the apparent cortical thickness. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional reconstructions.

Say that the surface looks more certain than the data beneath it. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

Say that a planning model carries the acquisition's limitations forward. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

How Good It Is

A three dimensional reconstruction communicates geometry better than any other display and adds no information to the volume it came from.

Its best contribution is communication and planning, where a surgeon's understanding of a complex fracture or deformity improves markedly. 5.16.2 covers tibial plateau fractures.

It is limited by being a rendering, so a fine detail or a thin cortex may be present in the data and absent from the surface. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no threshold values appear either, in Hounsfield units: those are protocol and vendor specific and this environment cannot check published figures.

Key Learning Points

  • It communicates geometry better than any other display.
  • It adds no information to the volume it came from.
  • Planning and communication are its real contributions.
  • A thin cortex can be in the data and absent from the surface.
  • The threshold is not stated here because it cannot be checked.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level a reconstruction is a planning aid and should not be the only thing looked at.

A complex articular fracture is understood faster from a surface than from slices, which changes the approach chosen. 5.16.2 covers tibial plateau fractures.

Deformity correction planning uses the surface for the plan and the slices for the detail, and both are needed. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Robotic and patient specific planning depend on segmentation accuracy, so the plan inherits every segmentation error silently. 5.16.7 covers robotic surgery planning.

A revision with widened tunnels needs the slices, because the surface will render a wall the data does not support. 5.7.12 covers tunnel assessment.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is planning from the surface without reviewing the slices. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional reconstructions.

A threshold set high thins the cortex and overstates bone loss. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

A threshold set low fills a defect and understates it. 5.16.2 covers tibial plateau fractures.

Metal corruption is read as the shape of the bone. 5.7.14 covers revision cruciate imaging.

An automated segmentation is accepted without checking it against the slices. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • Planning from the surface without the slices is the commonest error.
  • A high threshold thins the cortex and overstates bone loss.
  • A low threshold fills a defect and understates it.
  • Metal corruption is read as the shape of the bone.
  • An automated segmentation is accepted unchecked.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the three dimensional surface is where this chapter's argument has the largest practical consequence, because a plan is built on it and executed.

Patient specific instrumentation and robotic planning both convert a segmentation into a cut, so a segmentation error becomes a bone cut rather than a misread image. 5.16.7 covers robotic surgery planning.

Segmentation is increasingly automated, and its errors are systematic by anatomy and by population rather than random, which makes them hard to catch by inspection. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in imaging.

The threshold and the reconstruction method travel with the model and are not recorded in the plan, so a measurement taken from a model has an unstated provenance. 5.2.23 covers the reconstruction.

The surface carries no uncertainty, which is its advantage for communication and its hazard for measurement, and nothing in the workflow restores the uncertainty. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

So the chapter ends where it started, with the point that every imaging appearance is made jointly by the tissue and the instrument, and that a plan built on one should name both. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

Key Learning Points

  • A segmentation error becomes a bone cut rather than a misread image.
  • Automated segmentation errors are systematic by anatomy and population.
  • The threshold and method are not recorded in the plan.
  • The surface carries no uncertainty and nothing restores it.
  • An appearance is made jointly by the tissue and the instrument.
