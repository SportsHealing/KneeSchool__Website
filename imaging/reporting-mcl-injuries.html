Reporting MCL Injuries

Page 5.20.4 · Imaging Reporting Academy · reporting page

A layered structure reported as one ligament, and the posteromedial corner that decides whether the knee is operated on.

Reporting MCL Injuries

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What the Report Is For

An MCL report states which layer is injured, where along its length, and whether the posteromedial structures are involved. 5.9.1 covers the MCL anatomy.

It is built from the coronal images with the deep and superficial layers assessed separately. 5.9.3 covers deep MCL injuries.

It matters because a superficial injury is usually managed without an operation and a posteromedial one often is not. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

How the Report Is Built

Assess the superficial and the deep layer as separate structures on coronal images. 5.9.3 covers deep MCL injuries.

State the site along the ligament: femoral, mid substance or tibial. 5.9.1 covers the MCL anatomy.

Assess the posterior oblique ligament and the rest of the posteromedial corner explicitly. 5.9.4 covers POL injuries.

Report whether the medial meniscus is attached to the deep layer or separated from it. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Report the associated cruciate injury, because an isolated grade means little beside a combined one. 5.20.3 covers reporting ACL injuries.

What a Good One Looks Like

The two layers reported separately with the injured one named. 5.9.3 covers deep MCL injuries.

The site along the ligament stated. 5.9.1 covers the MCL anatomy.

The posteromedial corner reported as its own item. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

The meniscal attachment addressed rather than assumed. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

A conclusion that names the pattern rather than quoting a grade. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

What a Poor One Looks Like

A report that says MCL sprain with no layer and no site. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

A grade quoted as though the grading system were radiological. 4.12.1 covers the valgus stress testing.

Oedema in the medial soft tissues reported as a ligament injury. 5.14.1 covers synovitis.

The posteromedial corner unmentioned in a knee with a combined injury. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

A meniscocapsular separation reported as an MCL injury. 5.9.3 covers deep MCL injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The superficial and the deep layer are separate structures.
  • The site along the ligament belongs in the report.
  • The posteromedial corner decides much of the management.
  • Medial oedema is not by itself a ligament injury.
  • The grading systems for this ligament are clinical.

MRCS Level

How the Report Is Built

In a viva the expected answer separates the layers and the corner before grading anything.

The expected points are the two layers, the site, the posteromedial corner, the meniscal attachment, and the associated cruciate injury.

Say that the clinical grade comes from the examination and the report describes structure. 4.12.1 covers the valgus stress testing.

Say that laxity at full extension is a clinical finding with no imaging equivalent. 4.12.2 covers the assessment in full extension.

Say that a medial meniscal tear and an MCL injury produce overlapping tenderness. 4.12.7 covers the medial meniscus versus MCL injury.

What a Poor One Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the medial side resists valgus and rotation through different layers, so the layer injured predicts the instability pattern. 5.9.6 covers the AMRI imaging.

A superficial injury heals reliably and a posteromedial one often does not, which is the whole reason the report separates them. 5.9.4 covers POL injuries.

The deep layer carries the meniscal attachment, so its injury raises the meniscus as a question. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

An injury at the tibial insertion behaves differently from one at the femoral origin, which the report should let the surgeon see. 5.9.7 covers the post-operative medial reconstructions.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance reporting of the medial side shows the structures well and routinely compresses them into one word.

Its best contribution is the layer, the site and the posteromedial corner when each is looked for. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

It is limited by reporting convention rather than by the images, which makes this the most recoverable weakness in the chapter. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no grading thresholds appear either: those are clinical grades with published definitions and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The layer injured predicts the instability pattern.
  • A superficial injury heals more reliably than a posteromedial one.
  • The deep layer carries the meniscal attachment.
  • Tibial and femoral injuries behave differently.
  • The weakness here is convention rather than the images.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the question is whether this medial side will be operated on, and the report either addresses it or leaves it open. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

A posterior oblique ligament injury with a cruciate tear is the pattern that moves the discussion. 5.9.4 covers POL injuries.

A tibial sided superficial injury is the one that is slow to heal and is worth naming. 5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

Anteromedial rotatory instability is a pattern rather than a structure and needs the examination beside it. 5.9.6 covers the AMRI imaging.

The meniscal attachment and the ramp region belong in the same report as the ligament. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is an isolated MCL grade in a knee with a combined injury. 5.20.3 covers reporting ACL injuries.

Resolving oedema is read as healing in a ligament that is lax. 4.12.3 covers the assessment in flexion.

A chronic injury with a thickened ligament is reported as normal. 5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

Calcification at the femoral origin is reported as an acute injury. 5.9.1 covers the MCL anatomy.

A medial reconstruction is read against a native ligament. 5.9.7 covers the post-operative medial reconstructions.

Key Learning Points

  • An isolated grade in a combined injury misdirects.
  • Resolving oedema is not restored competence.
  • A chronic thickened ligament is reported as normal.
  • Old calcification is reported as acute.
  • A reconstruction is read against the wrong reference.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the medial side arrives already reported as a sprain, and the referral question is about rotation. 5.9.6 covers the AMRI imaging.

The posteromedial corner has no agreed reporting template, so its absence from a report carries no information. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

A valgus examination under anaesthesia answers what the scan cannot, and the two are recorded in different vocabularies. 4.14.6 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

After a medial reconstruction the expected appearance is unpublished, so the report describes a structure against no reference. 5.9.7 covers the post-operative medial reconstructions.

Serial scans of a healing medial side measure the interval rather than the outcome. 5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

The useful report gives the layer, the site, the corner, the meniscal attachment, the associated injuries, and the interval since injury. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Key Learning Points

  • Absence of the corner from a report carries no information.
  • The scan and the examination use different vocabularies.
  • A reconstructed medial side has no published reference appearance.
  • Serial scans measure interval rather than outcome.
  • The report should name the layer and the site.
