Clinical Examination Versus Imaging

Page 5.1.2 · Introduction to Knee Imaging · imaging page

Two assessments that answer different questions, and what to do when they disagree.

Clinical Examination Versus Imaging

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

The examination localises and grades by hand, and the imaging names a structure and shows its state. Neither is a better version of the other. 4.1.6 covers the examination's side.

The examination reaches the patient's experience, the function and the symptom pattern. The imaging reaches the anatomy. 4.18.6 covers functional limitation.

The useful comparison is not which is more accurate but which answers the question in front of you. 5.1.3 covers choosing the investigation.

Where It Misleads

Where the examination has localised the problem, imaging confirms the structure and the agreement makes both findings stronger.

Where the examination is equivocal, imaging finds abnormalities whose relevance cannot be established, which is weaker rather than stronger. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Where they disagree, one is wrong, and which one depends on the knee: a degenerate older knee favours the examination, an acute guarded knee favours the imaging. 4.23.1 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Key Learning Points

  • The examination localises and grades; the imaging names and shows.
  • Neither is a better version of the other.
  • Agreement makes both findings stronger.
  • Imaging after an equivocal examination is weaker, not stronger.
  • When they disagree, which one is wrong depends on the knee.

MRCS Level

How Good It Is

In a viva the expected answer refuses the premise that one replaces the other and says what each contributes.

The expected points are the examination's localisation and function, the imaging's structural diagnosis, the value of agreement, and what a disagreement means.

Say that a prevalent abnormality on imaging is not a diagnosis without a clinical finding to anchor it. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Say that a guarded acute examination is the case where imaging carries more weight, and say why. 4.23.1 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Where It Misleads

The reasoning expected is that imaging detects structure and symptoms come from function, so a structural finding is necessary and not sufficient.

A degenerate meniscal tear is present in a large share of asymptomatic older knees, which is why the report naming one does not make it the cause. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

A normal scan in a symptomatic knee does not end the assessment, because pain can precede a structural change and some sources are not imaged well. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

The examination's weakness is the reverse: it is defeated by guarding, by obesity and by a stiff knee, each of which reduces its sensitivity rather than its specificity. 4.23.4 covers the obese patient.

Key Learning Points

  • A structural finding is necessary and not sufficient.
  • A degenerate tear is prevalent in asymptomatic older knees.
  • A normal scan does not end the assessment of a symptomatic knee.
  • The examination loses sensitivity, not specificity, when it is limited.
  • The two instruments fail in opposite directions.

FRCS Level

Where It Misleads

At decision level a disagreement between the two is the finding, and resolving it is the work.

Imaging positive and examination negative is the commonest disagreement and usually means the finding is prevalent rather than causal, so the examination wins. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Examination positive and imaging negative means either the lesion is not imaged well or the examination is wrong, and the structure decides which: a cartilage lesion and a ramp lesion are both missed more often than a cruciate tear. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Both positive on different structures is the hardest case, because the plan then depends on which one is treated and the imaging cannot rank them. 4.2.8 covers the location of pain.

A repeat examination after the imaging is cheap and is skipped more often than anything else in this comparison. 4.1.4 covers the structured approach.

Key Learning Points

  • The disagreement is the finding, and resolving it is the work.
  • Imaging positive and examination negative usually means prevalent not causal.
  • Examination positive and imaging negative depends on which structure.
  • Two positives on different structures cannot be ranked by imaging.
  • Re-examining after the scan is cheap and usually skipped.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the two instruments are rarely used in sequence, which is the assumption the comparison is built on.

The imaging usually arrives first, so the examination is performed by someone who has already read the report, and that is not an independent assessment. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

A reported finding biases the examination towards it, and the effect is strongest for exactly the prevalent findings that most need an independent check. 5.6.2 covers meniscal signal grading.

Examining before reading the report is the defence and is impractical in most clinics, which makes this a service observation rather than a technique.

Neither instrument establishes causation, so a knee with a plausible finding and a matching examination is still an inference. 4.2.9 covers the onset.

The cases where both are wrong together are invisible, because nothing in the assessment disagrees with anything else. 4.1.5 covers common examination pitfalls.

Key Learning Points

  • The imaging usually arrives first, so the examination is not independent.
  • A reported finding biases the examination towards it.
  • The bias is strongest for the findings that most need a check.
  • Neither instrument establishes causation.
  • The cases where both are wrong together are invisible.
