Partial Tears

Page 5.8.2 · PCL MRI · imaging page

The loosest term in the section, because a thickened continuous ligament is what both a partial tear and a healing complete one look like.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Partial Tears

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A partial injury to the posterior cruciate means some fibres have failed and the ligament remains continuous. 5.8.1 covers the normal posterior cruciate.

On a scan that appears as thickening with internal signal and a preserved outline. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

It matters because a healing complete tear looks the same, so the term describes an appearance rather than a known extent of injury. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

How It Is Acquired

Read the ligament's continuity first, since continuity is the feature that puts an injury in this category at all. 2.8.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

Judge the thickening against the ligament's expected uniformity rather than against a dimension. 2.8.1 covers the gross anatomy.

Use a fluid sensitive sequence for the internal signal and a long echo time sequence to see whether it persists. 5.2.17 covers STIR imaging.

Read the marrow at the anterior tibia and the femoral condyle for the mechanism. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

Establish when the injury happened, because the same appearance means different things acutely and late. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

What Normal Looks Like

A uniform dark ligament with a smooth outline and no internal signal. 5.8.1 covers the normal posterior cruciate.

A curve appropriate to the knee's position in the scanner. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

Meniscofemoral ligaments running beside it, which are normal and separate structures. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Normal marrow at both attachments. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Thickening with internal signal and an intact outline, which is this page's appearance. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Signal that reaches the ligament's surface, which raises the question of extent rather than answering it. 5.2.14 covers T2 imaging.

Marrow change at the anterior tibia accompanying an acute injury. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

Injury to another ligament alongside, which is more informative than the grade. 4.11.7 covers the combined ligament injury assessment.

Thickening without any marrow change or effusion, which suggests an old injury rather than a partial one. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

Key Learning Points

  • A partial injury leaves the ligament continuous.
  • It appears as thickening with internal signal.
  • A healing complete tear looks the same.
  • The term describes an appearance rather than a known extent.
  • The timing of the injury changes what the appearance means.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer refuses to grade the injury from the image and says why.

The expected points are the preserved continuity, the thickening, the internal signal, the accompanying marrow change, and the overlap with a healing complete tear.

Say that continuity is the only feature that reliably separates this from a complete tear on one study. 5.8.3 covers the complete PCL tears.

Say that acute and chronic appearances overlap and the history resolves them. 4.11.6 covers chronic PCL deficiency.

Say that posterior laxity decides the grade and the examination measures it. 4.11.2 covers the posterior drawer test.

How Good It Is

This is the least reliable report in the chapter, and the reason is that the appearance has more than one cause.

Its best contribution is the acute injury with marrow change, where the appearance and the history agree and the extent is at least bounded by the mechanism. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

It is limited because thickening with internal signal is produced by a partial injury, a healing complete injury and an old injury, and the three have different consequences. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

It is limited again because the clinically important variable, how much posterior translation the knee allows, is not visible on an unloaded study at all. 3.8.6 covers the posterior drawer mechanics.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no grade, ligament thickness, signal ratio, translation distance or healing interval appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The appearance has more than one cause.
  • An acute injury with marrow change is the bounded case.
  • Partial, healing and old injuries share one appearance.
  • The three have different consequences.
  • Posterior translation is not visible on an unloaded study.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the word partial on a posterior cruciate report should be read as a description and not as a grade, because the decision is made on laxity.

A knee with this appearance and little laxity is managed as a functionally competent ligament. 4.11.6 covers chronic PCL deficiency.

A knee with this appearance and substantial laxity is not, and the appearance did not predict which it would be. 3.8.2 covers the posterior stability.

The accompanying injuries change the plan more than the ligament's grade does, and the corner is the one to look for. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

The useful contribution of this study is the mechanism, the associated structures, and the statement that the ligament is continuous. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is converting an appearance into a grade. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

A healing complete tear is reported as a partial one, which understates what happened. 5.8.3 covers the complete PCL tears.

An old injury is reported as acute because the ligament is thick. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

The meniscofemoral ligaments are read as residual fibres of a torn ligament. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

The corner is not assessed, because the report is organised around the cruciate. 5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

Key Learning Points

  • An appearance is converted into a grade.
  • A healing complete tear is reported as partial.
  • An old injury is reported as acute.
  • The meniscofemoral ligaments are read as residual fibres.
  • The corner is not assessed.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the partial posterior cruciate injury is the clearest example in the section of a report that sounds quantitative and is not.

The grading language came from the examination, where posterior translation is measured, and it migrated onto the scan, where nothing is measured. 4.11.2 covers the posterior drawer test.

The consequence is a referral that treats the scan as having graded the injury, so the examination is not repeated and the decision is taken on the wrong evidence. 5.1.2 covers clinical examination versus imaging.

Serial studies separate the causes that one study cannot, because a healing tear changes and an old injury does not. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

The useful report says the ligament is continuous, thickened and carries internal signal, gives the mechanism and the associated injuries, and leaves the grading to the examination. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Key Learning Points

  • The report sounds quantitative and is not.
  • The grading language migrated from the examination to the scan.
  • A graded scan stops the examination being repeated.
  • Serial studies separate causes that one study cannot.
  • The grading belongs to the examination.
