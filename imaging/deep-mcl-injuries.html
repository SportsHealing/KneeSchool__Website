Deep MCL Injuries

Page 5.9.3 · Medial Knee MRI · imaging page

The layer that carries the meniscus, which is why its injury raises the meniscus as a question and why it is the layer omitted.

Deep MCL Injuries

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

The deep medial collateral ligament is short, lies beneath the superficial one, and attaches to the medial meniscus above and the tibia below. 2.9.2 covers the deep MCL.

It appears on coronal images as two short bands separated by the meniscus. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

It matters because it links the medial side to the meniscus, so its injury can separate the meniscus from the capsule. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

How It Is Acquired

Assess it on coronal images at the joint line, deep to the superficial ligament. 2.9.2 covers the deep MCL.

Assess the meniscofemoral and meniscotibial parts separately. 3.9.4 covers the deep MCL function.

Assess the meniscus's attachment to the capsule. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Assess whether the meniscus has extruded. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Assess the superficial layer and the bursa separately from it. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

What Normal Looks Like

Two short bands running from the femur and the tibia to the meniscus. 2.9.2 covers the deep MCL.

A meniscus attached to the capsule along its periphery. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

No extrusion of the meniscal body. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

A clear plane between the deep and superficial layers. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Fluid or discontinuity in the meniscofemoral or the meniscotibial part. 2.9.2 covers the deep MCL.

Separation of the meniscus from the capsule. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Extrusion of the meniscal body, which follows losing the attachment. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Oedema confined to the deep layer with an intact superficial ligament. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Marrow change at the tibial attachment just below the joint line. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

Key Learning Points

  • The deep ligament has two parts separated by the meniscus.
  • It links the medial side to the meniscus.
  • Its injury can separate the meniscus from the capsule.
  • Extrusion follows losing the attachment.
  • It can be injured with the superficial layer intact.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer connects this layer to the meniscus.

The expected points are the coronal images at the joint line, the two parts, the meniscal attachment, the extrusion, and the superficial layer.

Say that this layer's injury raises the meniscus as a question. 4.12.7 covers the medial meniscus versus MCL injury.

Say that a meniscocapsular separation has no tear to find in the meniscus itself. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Say that the clinical assessment of this layer differs from the superficial one. 4.12.4 covers the deep MCL assessment.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the deep layer anchors the meniscus to the bone on both sides, so losing it lets the meniscus move and stop carrying load, which is a mechanical consequence rather than a ligamentous one. 3.9.4 covers the deep MCL function.

That is why extrusion is the finding to measure rather than the ligament's own signal. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Meniscal tenderness and ligament tenderness overlap on examination, which is why this layer is where imaging earns its place. 4.12.7 covers the medial meniscus versus MCL injury.

A ramp lesion sits at this junction and is among the commonest missed findings in an injured knee. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging shows this layer well and the report routinely omits it.

Its best contribution is the meniscal attachment and the extrusion. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

It is limited because the layer is thin, because acute oedema merges it with the superficial one, and because a meniscocapsular separation is easy to miss. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no extrusion values or layer thicknesses appear either: those are published quantities with competing conventions and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The layer anchors the meniscus on both sides.
  • Losing it is a mechanical consequence.
  • Extrusion is the finding to measure.
  • Meniscal and ligament tenderness overlap clinically.
  • A ramp lesion sits at this junction.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the question is whether the meniscus needs addressing. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

A meniscocapsular separation with a cruciate injury is repaired at the same operation. 3.9.10 covers the combined ACL and MCL injury.

Extrusion with an intact meniscus raises the attachment rather than a tear. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

A deep layer injury alone heals, which is why the meniscus rather than the ligament carries the decision. 3.9.4 covers the deep MCL function.

The posteromedial corner belongs in the same assessment. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is omitting this layer from the report. 5.20.4 covers reporting MCL injuries.

A meniscocapsular separation is reported as normal because the meniscus has no tear. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Extrusion is reported as a degenerative finding in a young injured knee. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

Bursal fluid between the layers is read as deep layer injury. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

The layer is merged with the superficial one in the report. 2.9.1 covers the superficial MCL.

Key Learning Points

  • The layer is omitted from the report.
  • A separation with no tear reads as normal.
  • Extrusion is misread as degenerative.
  • Bursal fluid is read as injury.
  • The layers are merged in the report.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the ramp lesion was missed at the index operation and the cruciate graft has stretched. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

The original scan usually shows the posteromedial finding that was not reported, which makes obtaining it the useful step. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Extrusion after a medial meniscal repair raises whether the attachment was restored. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

A knee with medial pain, a normal meniscus and a disrupted attachment is the presentation this page exists for. 4.12.7 covers the medial meniscus versus MCL injury.

A departmental protocol without thin posterior coronal images can miss this junction entirely. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

The useful report names both parts of the layer, the meniscal attachment, the extrusion and the posteromedial structures. 5.20.4 covers reporting MCL injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The missed ramp lesion stretches a cruciate graft.
  • The original scan shows the unreported finding.
  • Extrusion after repair questions the attachment.
  • Medial pain with a normal meniscus is the presentation.
  • A protocol without thin posterior images misses the junction.
