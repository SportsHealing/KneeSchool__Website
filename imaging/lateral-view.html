Lateral View

Page 5.3.2 · Plain Radiography · imaging page

The second standard image, and the one that carries patellar height and tibial slope.

Lateral View

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A lateral view projects the knee from the side, showing the patella in profile, the tibial plateau in profile and the posterior structures. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

It is the view that shows patellar height and the slope of the tibial plateau, neither of which the anteroposterior view can give. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

It matters because both of those are surgical measurements, and both depend on the view being a true lateral. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

How It Is Acquired

Position so that the femoral condyles are superimposed, which is the definition of a true lateral and is checkable on the image. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Keep the flexion angle consistent, because patellar height measurements change with flexion. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

Include enough tibia to assess the slope, since the slope is measured against the tibial shaft axis. 5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

Look at the soft tissue planes as well, because an effusion and a fat pad sign are visible here. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

Confirm the lateral is true before measuring anything, which is the step most often skipped. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

What Normal Looks Like

Superimposed femoral condyles, which is how a true lateral is recognised.

A patella of normal height relative to the joint, in profile with a clear articular surface. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

A tibial plateau in profile with a recognisable posterior slope. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

Clear soft tissue planes with no suprapatellar distension. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Non-superimposed condyles, which invalidates the measurements rather than indicating disease.

A high or low riding patella. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

A fracture line through the patella or the plateau in profile. 5.3.12 covers patellar fractures.

A distended suprapatellar region, indicating an effusion. 5.5.12 covers the bursa.

Key Learning Points

  • Superimposed condyles define a true lateral.
  • The view carries patellar height and tibial slope.
  • Patellar height measurements change with flexion.
  • An effusion is visible in the soft tissue planes.
  • Confirm the lateral is true before measuring.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the view to name for patellar height and tibial slope, with the true lateral condition stated first.

The expected points are the superimposed condyles, the consistent flexion, the tibial shaft, the soft tissues, and the confirmation step.

Say that a measurement from an oblique lateral is not a measurement. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Say that tibial slope is a cruciate relevant measurement. 5.7.14 covers revision cruciate imaging.

Say that patellar height is flexion dependent. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that both measurements are angles or ratios in the sagittal plane, so rotating out of that plane changes them without changing the knee. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Patellar height is a ratio precisely so that magnification cancels, which is why a ratio travels between departments where a length does not. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

Tibial slope is measured against the tibial shaft axis, so a short film gives a different answer from a long one. 5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

Increased slope loads the cruciate graft, which is why this view belongs in a revision assessment. 3.7.20 covers the reconstruction biomechanics.

How Good It Is

The lateral view supplies two surgically important measurements and supplies them only when the position is right.

Its best contribution is patellar height and tibial slope, which no other plain film gives. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

It is limited by its sensitivity to rotation, and by the slope answer depending on how much tibia is included. 5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no patellar height ratios or slope angles appear either: those are published quantities with several competing conventions and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Both measurements are sagittal, so rotation changes them without changing the knee.
  • Patellar height is a ratio so that magnification cancels.
  • Tibial slope depends on how much tibia is included.
  • Increased slope loads a cruciate graft.
  • The measurements are available only when the position is right.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this view contributes to two separate operations.

Patellar height decides whether a distalisation is part of an instability procedure, which makes the measurement a planning one. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

Tibial slope is a recognised contributor to repeated cruciate graft failure, and this is the film that measures it. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

A flexion deformity is visible here and is part of the deformity assessment. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

An effusion in an acute knee supports a significant injury and changes the threshold for further imaging. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is measuring from an oblique lateral. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

A patellar height ratio from one convention is compared with a threshold from another. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

Tibial slope is measured on a short film and taken as the true slope. 5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

Flexion differs between studies and the patellar height appears to change. 5.12.2 covers patella alta.

The view is not obtained at all before a revision cruciate reconstruction. 5.7.14 covers revision cruciate imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • Measuring from an oblique lateral is the commonest error.
  • Height ratios and thresholds from different conventions are mixed.
  • Slope from a short film is not the true slope.
  • Differing flexion makes patellar height appear to change.
  • The view is omitted before revision cruciate reconstruction.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this view's measurements are the clearest case in the chapter of competing conventions producing incompatible numbers.

Several patellar height ratios are in use and they do not agree, so a value without its method cannot be compared with a threshold. 5.21.4 covers the patellofemoral imaging literature.

Tibial slope has the same problem with a second variable, since the answer depends on the length of tibia included and on which cortex is used as the axis. 5.3.8 covers the anatomical axis.

Slope's contribution to graft failure is increasingly accepted, and the thresholds proposed for acting on it come from small series measured in different ways. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

Computed tomography and magnetic resonance based measurement of both is more reproducible and is not the convention the thresholds were derived on. 5.16.3 covers rotational profiles.

The discipline this argues for is to report the method alongside the value, which is the same conclusion the magnification page reached. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Key Learning Points

  • Several patellar height ratios are in use and do not agree.
  • Tibial slope depends on tibial length included and on the axis chosen.
  • The slope thresholds come from small series measured differently.
  • Cross sectional measurement is more reproducible and not what thresholds used.
  • Report the method alongside the value.
