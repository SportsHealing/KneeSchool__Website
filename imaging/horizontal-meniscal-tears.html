Horizontal Tears

Page 5.6.3 · Meniscal MRI · imaging page

A tear that splits the meniscus between its upper and lower halves, which is the degenerate pattern rather than the sporting one.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Horizontal Tears

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A horizontal tear runs parallel to the tibial surface and separates the meniscus into an upper and a lower leaf. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

It is the commonest pattern in a degenerate meniscus and is usually not the result of a single injury. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

It matters that this pattern is so common in older knees, because finding one does not establish that it is causing anything. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

How It Is Acquired

Look on the sagittal images, where a horizontal line through the substance of a horn is seen in profile. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Confirm on the coronal images, where the same tear appears as a line running from the free edge towards the capsule. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Decide whether the line reaches an articular surface, since that is what makes it a tear rather than intrasubstance degeneration. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

Look for an associated parameniscal cyst, which is a horizontal tear's characteristic companion and sometimes the presenting finding. 13.7.4 covers the meniscal cyst.

Assess the cartilage in the same compartment, because the tear and the wear usually travel together. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A linear area of signal running horizontally through a horn and reaching either the upper or the lower articular surface.

An upper leaf and a lower leaf of unequal size, since the split is rarely central.

A parameniscal cyst tracking away from the tear's peripheral end, which follows the tear's plane and is often larger than the tear. 13.7.4 covers the meniscal cyst.

Degenerate change elsewhere in the same meniscus and cartilage loss in the same compartment, which is the company this tear keeps. 5.6.8 covers complex tears.

Key Learning Points

  • A horizontal tear splits the meniscus into an upper and a lower leaf.
  • It is the degenerate pattern and rarely follows a single injury.
  • The surface rule decides whether it is a tear at all.
  • A parameniscal cyst is its characteristic companion.
  • It usually travels with cartilage loss in the same compartment.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the degenerate meniscus question and the expected answer links the pattern to the age and the compartment.

The expected points are the two planes, the surface rule, the parameniscal cyst, the cartilage in the compartment, and the patient's age.

Say that a horizontal tear is a degenerate pattern rather than a traumatic one. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

Say that you would assess the cartilage in the same compartment. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

Say that a parameniscal cyst points back to a tear even when the tear is small. 13.7.4 covers the meniscal cyst.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a horizontal split runs between the circumferential fibre bundles rather than across them, so it divides the meniscus without cutting the hoop. 3.6.3 covers the circumferential fibres.

That is why a horizontal tear impairs the meniscus less than a radial one of the same length, and why its leaves can remain stable. 3.6.12 covers the effects of radial tears.

The split provides a path for fluid, which is the mechanism of the parameniscal cyst. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

The pattern accumulates with age because it is a fatigue failure of degenerate tissue rather than an acute overload. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging detects this pattern readily, and detecting it is rarely the clinical problem.

Its best contribution is the associated findings, because the cartilage and the cyst change the management more than the tear does. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

It cannot say whether this tear is the symptom source, which is the question being asked of it. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • A horizontal split runs between the fibre bundles rather than across them.
  • It impairs the meniscus less than a radial tear of the same length.
  • The split gives fluid a path, which is how a parameniscal cyst forms.
  • The pattern is a fatigue failure of degenerate tissue.
  • Detecting it is rarely the clinical problem.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this tear is the one most often found and least often worth treating, and the imaging's job is to describe its company rather than itself.

A horizontal tear with preserved cartilage in a younger knee and a matching examination is the group where a decision might follow. 4.9.1 covers the clinical presentation.

The same tear with cartilage loss in the compartment is part of the wear, and treating the tear alone has been the field's most studied mistake. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

A symptomatic parameniscal cyst can be the reason to act, and the tear is then the route to it rather than the target. 13.7.4 covers the meniscal cyst.

A displaced leaf is a different proposition from a stable split, and displacement is what the imaging should be asked about. 5.6.7 covers flap tears.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is treating a horizontal tear found in an arthritic knee. 4.9.11 covers the examination in the worn knee.

A tear is attributed to an injury the patient remembers because the pattern is not read as degenerate. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

The cartilage is not assessed, so the compartment's real state is unreported. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

A parameniscal cyst is reported without the tear it arose from, which loses the mechanism. 5.20.2 covers reporting meniscal tears.

The free edge's partial volume creates apparent surface contact and upgrades degeneration to a tear. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Key Learning Points

  • Treating a horizontal tear in an arthritic knee is the commonest error.
  • The pattern is degenerate even when the patient remembers an injury.
  • An unassessed cartilage surface leaves the compartment's state unreported.
  • A cyst reported without its tear loses the mechanism.
  • Partial volume at the free edge upgrades degeneration to a tear.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the horizontal tear is the clearest case of a finding whose detection is easy and whose meaning is contested.

Its prevalence in asymptomatic older knees means a report naming one carries almost no diagnostic weight on its own. 5.6.2 covers signal grading.

Its relationship to the compartment's cartilage is the useful information and is reported less consistently than the tear. 5.11.2 covers chondral defects.

A partial meniscectomy for this pattern removes tissue from a compartment already losing its load distribution, which is the mechanical argument against operating. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Extrusion accompanies the degenerate meniscus and predicts the compartment's course better than the tear's morphology. 5.6.11 covers meniscal extrusion.

The literature on this pattern is the strongest negative evidence in knee surgery, and reading it as being about all meniscal tears rather than this one is the error it invites. 5.21.1 covers the meniscal imaging literature.

Key Learning Points

  • A reported horizontal tear carries almost no weight on its own.
  • The compartment's cartilage is the useful information and is reported less.
  • Resecting it removes tissue from a compartment already losing load distribution.
  • Extrusion predicts the compartment's course better than the morphology.
  • The negative literature is about this pattern, not about all meniscal tears.
