Sports Knee Protocol

Page 5.5.2 · MRI of the Knee · imaging page

The protocol for the athlete, which buys resolution and extra planes with scan time.

Sports Knee Protocol

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A sports knee protocol adds to the routine set for a young patient with a specific structural question. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

It typically adds thinner slices, an oblique plane along a structure of interest, and attention to the regions the routine set covers least. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

It matters because the lesions that decide an athlete's operation are the ones the routine protocol detects worst. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

How It Is Acquired

Reduce slice thickness, which reduces the partial volume averaging that hides a small lesion. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Add an oblique plane along the cruciate where a bundle level question is asked. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate tears.

Cover the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction and the meniscal roots deliberately, because those are the under-detected lesions. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Include the anterolateral structures, which matter for a rotational injury. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

Accept the longer scan time, which is the cost and the reason it is not the default. 5.1.5 covers the cost effectiveness of imaging.

What Normal Looks Like

The same normal appearances as the routine study, at higher resolution. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Cruciate bundles separable on the oblique plane. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

An intact posteromedial meniscocapsular junction. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Intact meniscal root attachments. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A ramp lesion at the posteromedial junction, which the routine protocol may not resolve. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

A root tear, seen better with thinner slices. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

A bundle level cruciate abnormality on the oblique plane. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate tears.

Anterolateral soft tissue injury alongside a cruciate tear. 2.11.3 covers the anterolateral ligament.

Key Learning Points

  • The protocol adds resolution and planes to the routine set.
  • Thinner slices reduce the averaging that hides a small lesion.
  • An oblique plane allows bundle level cruciate assessment.
  • The under-detected lesions are covered deliberately.
  • The cost is scan time, which is why it is not the default.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the protocol to name when asked how to improve detection of the lesions that change a cruciate operation.

The expected points are the thinner slices, the oblique plane, the posteromedial and root coverage, the anterolateral structures, and the time cost.

Say that the lesions that change the operation are the ones detected worst. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Say that it must be requested by name, since it is not the default. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Say that the request should state the suspected injury. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that detection of a small lesion is limited by partial volume averaging rather than by signal, so halving the slice thickness helps where more signal does not. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

An obliquely running structure is cut obliquely by every orthogonal plane, so a plane aligned to the structure is the only way to see it along its length. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

The posteromedial junction and the roots are at the edges of the routine coverage, so they are missed by field of view and attention rather than by resolution on its own. 5.6.9 covers root tears.

The whole protocol exists because the operative decision in a young athlete rests on the associated injuries rather than on the cruciate. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

How Good It Is

The sports protocol improves detection of the lesions that matter most and costs scan time.

Its best contribution is the posteromedial corner, the roots and bundle level cruciate assessment. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

It is limited by availability and by needing to be requested, so the commonest reason it does not help is that it was not used. 5.1.4 covers imaging pathways.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no slice thicknesses or scan times appear either: those are local protocol quantities and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Small lesion detection is limited by averaging rather than by signal.
  • An obliquely running structure is cut obliquely by every orthogonal plane.
  • The corner and the roots are missed by coverage and attention as well as resolution.
  • The operative decision rests on the associated injuries.
  • The commonest reason it does not help is that it was not used.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this protocol is worth requesting when the associated injuries will change the operation.

An acute cruciate injury in a young patient is the clearest indication, because the ramp lesion and the roots are repairable acutely. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

A rotational injury where an anterolateral procedure is being considered benefits from the anterolateral coverage. 5.7.11 covers graft failure.

A bundle level question about a partial tear needs the oblique plane. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate tears.

A degenerate knee does not need it, which is why it is a protocol rather than the protocol. 5.6.8 covers complex meniscal tears.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not requesting it in the patient it was designed for. 5.1.4 covers imaging pathways.

A routine study's negative result for a ramp lesion is accepted as an exclusion. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

The protocol is requested without the clinical question, so the department cannot tailor it. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The extra planes are acquired and not reviewed. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Scan time is spent on a patient whose decision it will not change. 5.1.5 covers the cost effectiveness of imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • It is not requested in the patient it was designed for.
  • A routine study's negative is accepted as an exclusion.
  • It is requested without the clinical question.
  • The extra planes are acquired and not reviewed.
  • Scan time is spent where the decision will not change.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this protocol is a known answer to a known detection problem that is unevenly adopted.

The detection gap for ramp lesions and root tears is established and the protocol changes that detection, so the limiting factor is the request and the availability rather than the knowledge. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Oblique cruciate imaging improves bundle level assessment and the partial tear diagnosis it supports remains the least reproducible call in the section, so a better acquisition feeds a weak definition. 5.7.2 covers partial cruciate tears.

Protocol content varies between departments under the same name, which makes a request for a sports protocol less specific than it sounds. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Three dimensional isotropic acquisition offers the same thin slices in every plane without the extra sequences, which is where this is heading and which changes the contrast again. 5.2.18 covers gradient echo imaging.

The useful practice is to name the suspected lesion rather than the protocol, because the lesion is what the department can act on. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

Key Learning Points

  • The detection gap is established and the protocol changes it.
  • The limiting factor is the request and availability, not knowledge.
  • A better acquisition feeds a weakly defined partial tear diagnosis.
  • Protocol content varies under the same name.
  • Name the suspected lesion rather than the protocol.
