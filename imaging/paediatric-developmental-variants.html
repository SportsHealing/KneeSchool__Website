Developmental Variants

Page 5.15.7 · Paediatric MRI · imaging page

A list of appearances that are normal and get reported, and knowing the list is what stops a child being investigated.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Developmental Variants

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A growing knee produces appearances that have no adult equivalent, so a reader working from adult experience meets them as abnormalities. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

They recur in predictable places at predictable stages, which is what makes them a list rather than a judgement. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

It matters because the consequence of not knowing them is an investigation, a follow up appointment or an operation for a knee that is developing normally. 5.1.6 covers the imaging pitfalls.

What Normal Looks Like

Irregular ossification at the posterior femoral condyles, which is the variant most often reported as a lesion. 5.15.3 covers paediatric OCD.

An irregular or multifocal ossification margin at the patella, which is not a fracture. 2.4.2 covers the ossification.

Red marrow filling the metaphyses and extending into the epiphysis at some stages. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

An open physis with an undulating, interdigitating outline. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Thick epiphyseal and articular cartilage with its expected signal, which is not swelling. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

What Abnormal Looks Like

An interface behind an irregularity, which separates a lesion from an ossification front. 5.11.5 covers the osteochondral lesions.

Asymmetry between the knees, since development is broadly symmetrical and disease is not. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

Marrow that replaces rather than fills, distinguished on distribution and on the adjacent cortex. 5.13.7 covers bone tumours.

A line crossing the physis, which no stage produces. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Cartilage of uneven thickness on one surface, where paediatric cartilage is thick and even. 5.11.2 covers the chondral defects.

Key Learning Points

  • The growing knee produces appearances with no adult equivalent.
  • They recur in predictable places at predictable stages.
  • Posterior condylar ossification irregularity is the commonest over-call.
  • Development is broadly symmetrical and disease is not.
  • An interface behind an irregularity separates a lesion from a front.

MRCS Level

What Normal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a variant is identified by its site, its stage and its symmetry together, and that no one of the three settles it alone.

Site narrows the differential, because each variant has a region it occupies. 5.15.3 covers paediatric OCD.

Stage decides whether the appearance belongs there now, which is why the report should state the stage it read against. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

Symmetry is the most practical test available in a single study, and it costs one additional sequence of the other knee. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

The absence of accompanying findings completes the argument, since a variant brings no oedema, effusion or interface with it. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

How Good It Is

These appearances are well described and the recognition is a knowledge problem rather than a technical one, which is an unusual position in this section.

The modality's contribution is that the discriminating features are visible on it: the interface, the marrow pattern, the cartilage and the contralateral comparison. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

It is limited by the reader, since nothing about the acquisition makes a variant easier or harder to recognise. 5.1.6 covers the imaging pitfalls.

It is limited again because a single study is one frame of a developing joint, so the appearance that is ambiguous now is often unambiguous on a later study. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no age, ossification timing, cartilage thickness or marrow distribution figure appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Recognition is a knowledge problem rather than a technical one.
  • Site, stage and symmetry together identify a variant.
  • A variant brings no oedema, effusion or interface with it.
  • Nothing about the acquisition changes how easy a variant is to spot.
  • An ambiguous appearance now is often unambiguous later.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the value of this page is entirely in what it prevents, and what it prevents is activity.

A variant recognised and named in the report closes the question, where a variant described without a conclusion opens one. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

A child referred because of a reported lesion has already had the cost of the referral, so the saving is in the first report rather than in the second opinion. 5.1.4 covers the imaging pathways.

The decision that matters is often whether to repeat the study rather than whether to operate, and a dated baseline makes that repeat worth doing. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

Where the appearance is genuinely ambiguous, naming the two possibilities and the test that separates them is more useful than choosing one. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting a variant as a lesion. 5.15.3 covers paediatric OCD.

A variant is recognised and still mentioned without a conclusion, which is read as doubt. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

A bilateral variant is reported as bilateral disease. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

The contralateral knee is not imaged, so the most useful discriminator is unavailable. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

A genuine lesion is dismissed as a variant, which is the error this page's own subject matter encourages. 5.11.5 covers the osteochondral lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • A variant is reported as a lesion.
  • A variant mentioned without a conclusion reads as doubt.
  • A bilateral variant is called bilateral disease.
  • The contralateral knee is not imaged.
  • A genuine lesion is dismissed as a variant.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this page closes the chapter, and the chapter's position is that paediatric knee imaging is mostly the discipline of not reporting things.

The structures are better seen in a child than in an adult, because cartilage is thick and marrow is active and the physis is open, so the difficulty is interpretive rather than technical. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

The two errors remain unequal, and this page sits on the side that is cheaper to get wrong, which is exactly why it is the side that gets over-corrected. 5.15.3 covers paediatric OCD.

The one thing that resolves most ambiguity is also the one thing most reports omit, which is the skeletal stage, and this chapter has said so on every page rather than once. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

The transferable position is that a report on a growing knee is a statement about a moment in a sequence, and a reader who treats it as a statement about a knee will act on it wrongly in both directions. 5.1.7 covers evidence-based imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • Paediatric knee imaging is mostly the discipline of not reporting things.
  • The structures are better seen in a child than in an adult.
  • The difficulty is interpretive rather than technical.
  • The cheaper error is the one that gets over-corrected.
  • A report on a growing knee describes a moment in a sequence.
