Joint Line Obliquity

Page 5.3.9 · Plain Radiography · imaging page

The tilt of the joint line relative to the limb, which decides whether a correction leaves a level knee.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Joint Line Obliquity

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

Joint line obliquity is the angle between the knee's joint line and a line perpendicular to the mechanical axis. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

An oblique joint line means the knee surface is tilted relative to the limb it sits in. 3.13.6 covers the joint line obliquity.

It matters because a correction that fixes the axis and leaves the joint line oblique produces a knee that is aligned and tilted. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

How It Is Acquired

Measure on a standing long leg film, because the reference is the limb's mechanical axis. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Define the joint line from the tibial plateau surface or from the femoral condyles, and state which. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

Compare with the perpendicular to the mechanical axis rather than to the film edge. 5.3.7 covers the mechanical axis.

Separate joint line obliquity from the bone deformities above and below it, which is what deformity analysis does. 5.3.10 covers the deformity analysis.

Note that obliquity can arise from the femur, the tibia, intra-articular wear or all three. 5.3.20 covers the alignment changes.

What Normal Looks Like

A joint line roughly perpendicular to the mechanical axis in the standing limb.

Femoral condyles and tibial plateau surfaces approximately parallel to each other.

No gap opening on either side under weight. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

Symmetry with the other limb. 3.13.5 covers constitutional varus.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A joint line tilted relative to the perpendicular, usually in the direction of the deformity. 3.13.6 covers the joint line obliquity.

Intra-articular wear producing the tilt within the joint rather than in the bones. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

A tilt that persists after the axis has been corrected, which is the surgical problem. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Asymmetric gapping under load, indicating a soft tissue contribution. 4.5.3 covers valgus alignment.

Key Learning Points

  • Obliquity is the joint line's angle to the perpendicular of the mechanical axis.
  • An oblique line means the knee surface is tilted in the limb.
  • Correcting the axis does not necessarily level the joint line.
  • Obliquity can arise in the femur, the tibia or within the joint.
  • The reference is the mechanical axis, not the film edge.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the measurement to raise when asked what else an alignment film shows besides the axis.

The expected points are the standing long film, the joint line definition, the perpendicular reference, the separation from bone deformity, and the sources of obliquity.

Say that an axis correction can leave an oblique joint line. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Say that obliquity can be intra-articular. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Say that the joint line definition must be stated. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that an oblique joint line puts the contact surface at an angle to the load, which introduces a shear component the joint is not built to carry. 3.4.9 covers the force transmission.

That shear is the argument for levelling the joint line rather than only neutralising the axis, and it is the mechanical reason obliquity is a separate target. 3.13.9 covers the osteotomy biomechanics.

Obliquity arising within the joint, from wear, cannot be corrected by an osteotomy, which is why its source has to be located. 5.3.20 covers the alignment changes.

Soft tissue laxity contributes to standing obliquity, so part of what is measured is correctable by position rather than by bone. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

How Good It Is

Joint line obliquity is a measurement that changes operative plans and is often not made at all.

Its best contribution is identifying the knee where axis correction alone would leave a tilted joint. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

It is limited by the joint line having several possible definitions and by the intra-articular and soft tissue contributions being hard to separate on a plain film. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no obliquity angles or acceptable limits appear either: those are published quantities and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • An oblique joint line introduces a shear component.
  • That shear is the mechanical argument for levelling the line.
  • Obliquity from wear cannot be corrected by an osteotomy.
  • Soft tissue laxity contributes to standing obliquity.
  • The measurement changes plans and is often not made.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this measurement separates the deformity that can be corrected from the deformity that cannot.

Obliquity arising in bone is correctable by an osteotomy at the responsible site. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Obliquity arising within the joint from wear is not, and in that knee the compartment rather than the bone is the problem. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Obliquity from soft tissue laxity is addressed by balancing rather than by cutting, which is an arthroplasty consideration. 5.16.6 covers arthroplasty planning.

Planning a correction without this measurement risks a technically successful axis and a poorly functioning knee. 3.13.10 covers the alignment correction principles.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is not measuring obliquity at all. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Obliquity from intra-articular wear is attributed to bone. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

The joint line is defined differently between two studies and the obliquity appears to change. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

The film is rotated and the obliquity is spurious. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Soft tissue contribution is not separated with a stress view. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

Key Learning Points

  • Obliquity is often not measured at all.
  • Intra-articular obliquity is attributed to bone.
  • Two definitions of the joint line make obliquity appear to change.
  • A rotated film gives spurious obliquity.
  • The soft tissue contribution needs a stress view to separate.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level joint line obliquity is the measurement that the move to patient specific alignment has made most important and least standardised.

Alignment philosophies differ in how much obliquity they accept, so the same measurement supports different plans and the disagreement is about targets rather than about the number. 3.13.10 covers the alignment correction principles.

The joint line can be defined from the femoral condyles or the tibial plateau, and in a worn knee those are not parallel, so the definition changes the answer in exactly the knees being planned. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Standing obliquity mixes bone geometry, cartilage loss and soft tissue laxity, and separating the three needs a stress view that is not routinely obtained. 4.5.2 covers varus alignment.

Three dimensional and weight bearing acquisition would resolve most of this and is not the basis of the published limits. 5.18.5 covers weight bearing imaging.

The position worth holding is that obliquity should be measured, its definition stated, and its source located before a correction is planned. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Key Learning Points

  • Alignment philosophies differ in how much obliquity they accept.
  • In a worn knee the femoral and tibial definitions are not parallel.
  • Standing obliquity mixes bone, cartilage and soft tissue.
  • A stress view would separate them and is not routine.
  • Measure it, state the definition, and locate its source.
