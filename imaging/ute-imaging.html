UTE Imaging

Page 5.18.4 · Advanced Imaging · imaging page

Sequences that capture signal from the tissues a conventional study renders black, which makes new anatomy visible with no normal to compare it against.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

UTE Imaging

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

Ultrashort echo time imaging captures signal from tissues whose signal decays too fast for a conventional sequence to record. 5.2.12 covers the basic principles.

Those tissues include the deep and calcified cartilage, the menisci, tendon, ligament and cortical bone. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

It matters because every conventional study shows these as dark, so their internal structure has never been part of routine reading. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

How It Is Acquired

Sample the signal almost immediately after excitation rather than after a conventional delay. 5.2.12 covers the basic principles.

Use a subtraction approach, so the long signal tissues do not obscure the short ones. 5.2.16 covers the fat suppression.

Accept a longer acquisition and a different artefact pattern from a conventional sequence. 5.2.19 covers the artefacts.

Image the structure of interest specifically, because the sequence is not a whole joint study. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Expect no established normal appearance for most of these tissues. 5.1.8 covers the future of knee imaging.

What Normal Looks Like

Signal where a conventional sequence shows none. 5.5.7 covers the cartilage.

A layered appearance at the deep cartilage and the calcified zone. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

Internal structure inside tendon and inside the meniscus. 5.5.8 covers the menisci.

Cortical bone carrying signal rather than appearing as an absence. 5.5.6 covers the bones.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Loss of the layered appearance at the osteochondral junction. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral lesions.

Signal change inside a tendon that a conventional sequence could not show. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

Change in the calcified cartilage and the subchondral plate together. 5.11.7 covers bone marrow lesions.

An appearance with no published normal to compare it against, which is the usual situation here. 5.1.8 covers the future of knee imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • The sequence samples before the short signals decay.
  • It shows tissues conventional sequences render black.
  • Subtraction separates short from long signal tissues.
  • It images a structure rather than a whole joint.
  • Most of these tissues have no published normal appearance.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer explains what is being recovered and why it is new.

The expected points are the immediate sampling, the subtraction, the target structures, the acquisition cost, and the absence of a reference.

Say that a dark structure on a conventional sequence is not a structureless one. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

Say that the osteochondral junction is the region this addresses. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral lesions.

Say that interpretation rather than acquisition is the limiting step. 5.1.8 covers the future of knee imaging.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that highly ordered collagen gives a very short signal, so the tissues with the most organised matrix are precisely the ones conventional imaging cannot characterise. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

The same organisation produces the magic angle behaviour, so both techniques exist because of one tissue property. 5.2.20 covers the magic angle effect.

The calcified cartilage and the subchondral plate form a mechanical unit that no other sequence resolves, which is where the clinical interest sits. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral lesions.

A new appearance with no normal reference cannot be called abnormal, which is the honest limit on reading these studies. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

How Good It Is

Ultrashort echo time imaging is the only way to see inside these tissues and has almost nothing established to compare an appearance against.

Its best contribution is the osteochondral junction and the deep cartilage. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral lesions.

It is limited by acquisition time, by unfamiliar artefacts, by its structure specific scope, and above all by the absence of a normal reference. 5.1.8 covers the future of knee imaging.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no echo times or signal values appear either: those are protocol quantities tied to particular implementations and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Ordered collagen gives the shortest signal.
  • One tissue property underlies this and the magic angle.
  • The osteochondral unit is where the interest sits.
  • With no normal reference an appearance is not abnormal.
  • Interpretation rather than acquisition is the limit.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this changes nothing today and may change what a cartilage procedure is assessed on. 5.17.5 covers the cartilage procedures.

The osteochondral junction decides whether a lesion is chondral or osteochondral, which changes the operation. 5.11.5 covers osteochondral lesions.

Tendon and ligament structure could support a return to play decision that currently rests on time and examination. 5.5.10 covers the tendons.

Meniscal matrix assessment could separate a degenerate meniscus from an acutely torn one. 5.6.2 covers the meniscal signal grading.

None of these uses is established, and the reason is the reference rather than the physics. 5.1.8 covers the future of knee imaging.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is calling a newly visible appearance abnormal. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Subtraction artefacts are read as signal inside the tissue. 5.2.19 covers the artefacts.

Motion over a long acquisition degrades exactly the fine structure being sought. 5.2.21 covers the advanced MRI techniques.

An appearance from one implementation is compared with one from another. 5.18.1 covers the quantitative MRI.

A structure specific study is read as a knee assessment. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Key Learning Points

  • A newly visible appearance is not an abnormal one.
  • Subtraction artefact is read as tissue signal.
  • Motion degrades the structure being sought.
  • Implementations are not comparable.
  • A structure specific study is not a knee assessment.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this appears in research collaborations and device material rather than in reports. 5.21.8 covers the future directions.

The tissues it shows are the ones orthopaedic practice cares most about and knows least about radiologically, which leaves the literature thin and the claims confident. 5.21.8 covers the future directions.

Normal variation in these tissues has not been mapped by age, activity or sex, so a cohort of young volunteers stands in for the population. 5.1.7 covers evidence-based imaging.

Combining it with quantitative mapping of the same tissue is the current direction and compounds both standardisation problems. 5.18.1 covers the quantitative MRI.

Automated analysis is being applied to appearances no reader has yet learned to call normal. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in imaging.

The honest position is that this is the most genuinely new technique in the chapter and the one with the least ground under it. 5.1.8 covers the future of knee imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • The literature is thin and the claims are confident.
  • Normal variation has not been mapped.
  • Combining techniques compounds both problems.
  • Automation is applied before readers have a normal.
  • The newest technique has the least ground under it.
