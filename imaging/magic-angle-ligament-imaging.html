Ligament Imaging

Page 5.19.4 · Magic Angle MRI Academy · imaging page

A ligament changes direction along its own length, so one structure carries affected and unaffected segments at once.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Ligament Imaging

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A ligament's fibres are ordered, so it shows the orientation effect in the same way a tendon does. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

A ligament's course is not straight, so the angle between its fibres and the field changes from one end of it to the other. 2.7.1 covers the gross anatomy.

It matters because the effect is therefore segmental within a single structure, and a segmental appearance is the pattern a partial tear is reported from. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

How It Is Acquired

Follow each ligament along its whole length rather than judging it from one image. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

The cruciates run obliquely through the joint by design, so part of each sits near the critical orientation in most knees. 2.7.8 covers the anteromedial bundle.

The collaterals run close to the field direction over their mid portion and fan out at their attachments. 2.9.1 covers the superficial MCL.

Expect the brightest region near a bony attachment, where the fibres spread. 2.7.7 covers the tibial footprint.

Confirm anything unexpected on a long echo time sequence before it reaches the report. 5.2.14 covers T2 imaging.

What Normal Looks Like

A normal cruciate is dark over most of its length with a brighter zone near its tibial attachment. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

A normal collateral is dark over its mid portion and less uniform where it attaches. 5.9.1 covers the MCL anatomy.

The fibres stay continuous through the bright zone, which is what makes it normal. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

The structure keeps its expected thickness and alignment throughout. 2.8.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A tear interrupts the fibres, and a bright segment with continuous fibres does not. 5.7.3 covers the complete tears.

Real injury carries marrow change, fluid or a torn neighbour with it. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

A ligament injured acutely is oedematous beyond its own outline. 5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

Mucoid change spreads through a ligament rather than occupying the segment at a particular angle. 5.7.6 covers mucoid degeneration.

A chronic ligament is thinner and more sharply defined, which orientation never makes it. 5.7.5 covers chronic ACL injury.

Key Learning Points

  • A ligament's angle to the field changes along its own length.
  • The effect is therefore segmental within one structure.
  • A segmental appearance is what a partial tear is reported from.
  • The brightest region is usually near a bony attachment.
  • Continuous fibres through a bright segment make it normal.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer distinguishes a bright segment from an interrupted one.

The expected points are the oblique course, the attachment fanning, the fibre continuity, the long echo time test, and the accompanying findings.

Say that part of each cruciate lies near the critical orientation in most knees. 2.7.9 covers the posterolateral bundle.

Say that the appearance near the tibial attachment is the one most often misread. 5.20.3 covers reporting ACL injuries.

Say that an acute injury is never confined to a single angled segment. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL injury.

How Good It Is

For ligaments the effect is harder to use and harder to dismiss than for tendons, because a ligament legitimately carries different appearances along its length.

Its best contribution is explaining an appearance that otherwise reads as a partial tear, which is the least reliable report in knee ligament imaging. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

It is limited by the overlap with genuine partial injury, since both produce a regional change within a continuous structure. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

It is limited again by the chapter's general problem: the knee's position sets every ligament's angle at once and no study records it. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no angle, ligament dimension or signal grade appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • A ligament legitimately carries different appearances along its length.
  • The effect explains an appearance that reads as a partial tear.
  • Genuine partial injury produces a regional change too.
  • The knee's position sets every ligament's angle at once.
  • No study records the position that produced the appearance.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the partial tear is the report this page is about, because it changes management and is the least reproducible thing a knee report contains.

A reported partial cruciate tear in a knee with a negative examination is checked on the long echo time sequence and against the clinical findings. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

A reported collateral injury with no mechanism and a stable knee follows the same route. 5.9.2 covers MCL sprains.

A segmental change near an attachment with intact fibres is a reason to re-read rather than to operate. 2.7.6 covers the femoral footprint.

The examination under anaesthesia answers the question the scan raises, and no imaging appearance replaces it. 4.14.6 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting a partial cruciate tear from a bright segment. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

The collateral is reported as sprained because its attachment is bright. 5.9.1 covers the MCL anatomy.

The appearance is confirmed by a second reader on the same sequence rather than tested on another. 5.2.14 covers T2 imaging.

A reconstruction graft is reported as failing because its new course brightens. 5.17.1 covers ACL reconstruction.

The reverse error follows: a genuine partial injury is written off as orientation because the reader has learned the first lesson and not the second. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Key Learning Points

  • A partial cruciate tear is reported from a bright segment.
  • A collateral is called sprained because its attachment is bright.
  • A second reader on the same sequence confirms nothing.
  • A graft is reported as failing because its new course brightens.
  • A genuine partial injury is written off as orientation.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the ligament version of this problem is worse than the tendon version, and the reason is that the ligament's variation is real.

A tendon that brightens at an angle is otherwise uniform, so the effect stands out against a plain background, and a ligament's background is not plain. 2.7.3 covers the histology.

A graft takes the course the tunnels give it, so after a reconstruction the affected segment moves and the pre-operative pattern no longer applies. 5.17.1 covers ACL reconstruction.

The appearance of a graft at an unrecorded angle is the single most common reason a well functioning reconstruction is reported as abnormal, and the chapter's rule is the remedy: record the position or do not quantify the signal. 5.19.9 covers the clinical translation.

The measurement that would help is of fibre order within the graft, which is what maturation means, and which no routine sequence supplies. 5.19.2 covers the collagen architecture.

Key Learning Points

  • A ligament's variation along its length is real, not artefactual.
  • The effect stands out less against a varying background.
  • A graft's affected segment moves with the tunnels.
  • A well functioning graft is reported abnormal from an unrecorded angle.
  • The useful measurement would be fibre order within the graft.
