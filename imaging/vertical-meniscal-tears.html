Vertical Tears

Page 5.6.4 · Meniscal MRI · imaging page

A tear running through the meniscus from top to bottom, which is the pattern a young knee produces and the one most often repairable.

Vertical Tears

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A vertical tear runs perpendicular to the tibial surface. Where it also runs along the meniscus it is longitudinal, and where it runs across the meniscus it is radial. 5.6.5 covers radial tears.

The longitudinal vertical tear is the classic sporting injury, often with a cruciate tear, and it is the pattern that can be repaired. 5.7.1 covers the normal cruciate.

It matters because the tear's position relative to the blood supply decides whether repair is possible. 2.5.3 covers the vascular supply.

How It Is Acquired

Look on the coronal images, where a vertical line through the full thickness of a horn is seen in profile. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Follow it across consecutive sagittal images to establish its length, since a longitudinal tear runs along the meniscus and its length is the surgical question. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

Decide how peripheral it is, because a tear at the capsular margin is in vascular tissue and one at the free edge is not. 2.5.3 covers the vascular supply.

Look for displacement of the inner fragment, since a long vertical tear can flip. 5.6.6 covers bucket handle tears.

Assess the cruciate, because this pattern and a cruciate tear travel together. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A vertical line of signal crossing the full height of the meniscus and reaching both the upper and the lower surface, or reaching one and the capsular margin.

A tear running along the meniscus over several slices, which is the longitudinal form and the one measured for length.

A peripheral tear close to the capsule, where the meniscus is vascular and the tissue is red, in contrast to a free edge tear in white tissue. 2.5.3 covers the vascular supply.

Fluid tracking into the tear on a fluid sensitive sequence, which makes it conspicuous. 5.2.16 covers fat suppression.

Key Learning Points

  • A vertical tear crosses the meniscus from top to bottom.
  • Longitudinal runs along the meniscus; radial runs across it.
  • The longitudinal vertical tear is the sporting pattern and the repairable one.
  • How peripheral it is decides whether repair is possible.
  • It travels with cruciate injury.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva this is the young knee with a twisting injury and the expected answer gets to repairability.

The expected points are the plane it is seen in, the length, how peripheral it is, any displacement, and the cruciate.

Say that you would measure the length and state how peripheral the tear is, because both decide the operation. 2.5.3 covers the vascular supply.

Say that you would look at the cruciate in the same study. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Say that a long tear can displace and name the appearance. 5.6.6 covers bucket handle tears.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that a longitudinal vertical tear runs between the circumferential bundles without cutting them, so the hoop mechanism is largely preserved and the tissue can be brought back together. 3.6.3 covers the circumferential fibres.

That is the mechanical reason this pattern is repaired and a radial tear through the same bundles often is not. 3.6.12 covers the effects of radial tears.

A peripheral tear sits in vascular tissue, which supplies the healing response a repair depends on. 3.6.13 covers meniscal repair biomechanics.

The association with cruciate injury follows from the mechanism, since the same rotational event loads both. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging detects this pattern well and estimates its length and position usefully.

Its best contribution is the information a repair decision needs, which is length, position and displacement rather than the diagnosis. 3.6.13 covers meniscal repair biomechanics.

It is least reliable at the extreme periphery, where a tear at the meniscocapsular junction is easy to miss. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

No figures appear for any accuracy comparison on this page, under decision 028, and the comparison is the claim being made.

Key Learning Points

  • A longitudinal tear runs between the fibre bundles without cutting them.
  • That is why it is repairable and a radial tear often is not.
  • A peripheral tear sits in vascular tissue, which a repair needs.
  • The cruciate association follows from the shared mechanism.
  • The useful output is length, position and displacement.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this is the pattern where imaging most directly shapes the operation.

A long peripheral vertical tear in a young knee is a repair candidate, and the imaging supplies the length and the position the decision rests on. 3.6.13 covers meniscal repair biomechanics.

A short stable tear may need nothing, and the imaging's job is establishing that it is short and stable. 5.6.1 covers the normal meniscus.

A displaced fragment changes the urgency, because a locked knee is a clinical emergency of a sort. 4.9.8 covers mechanical locking.

A vertical tear with a cruciate tear is addressed in one operation, so finding both changes the plan rather than adding to it. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is missing the peripheral end of the tear, which is where the repairability is decided. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

The length is estimated from one plane, which understates a tear running obliquely. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

A displaced fragment is missed because the empty parent site looks like a partial meniscectomy. 5.6.6 covers bucket handle tears.

The popliteal hiatus is read as a lateral vertical tear. 2.6.2 covers the popliteomeniscal fascicles.

The cruciate is not examined on the same study, so a combined injury is reported as isolated. 5.7.4 covers the acute cruciate injury.

Key Learning Points

  • Missing the peripheral end loses the repairability question.
  • A length estimated from one plane understates an oblique tear.
  • A displaced fragment leaves a parent site that mimics a meniscectomy.
  • The popliteal hiatus mimics a lateral vertical tear.
  • An unexamined cruciate turns a combined injury into an isolated one.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the question is not whether there is a vertical tear but whether this one can be repaired and should be.

Length, position and tissue quality decide repairability, and imaging estimates the first two and says little about the third. 3.6.13 covers meniscal repair biomechanics.

A tear that looks peripheral on a thick slice may be less peripheral than it appears, and the margin that matters is a few millimetres wide. 5.5.2 covers the sports knee protocol.

A chronic vertical tear in a knee with a long standing cruciate deficiency is not the same lesion as an acute one, and the imaging appearance converges. 5.7.5 covers the chronic cruciate injury.

The meniscocapsular junction posteromedially is the site where this pattern is most often missed and most consequential, which is why it has its own page. 5.6.10 covers ramp lesions.

Repair outcome depends on factors the imaging does not show, including the patient's age and the cruciate's state, so a repairable appearance is not a prediction. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • Imaging estimates length and position and says little about tissue quality.
  • The peripheral margin that matters is a few millimetres wide.
  • A chronic tear in a cruciate deficient knee is a different lesion.
  • The posteromedial meniscocapsular junction is where it is most often missed.
  • A repairable appearance is not a prediction of repair outcome.
