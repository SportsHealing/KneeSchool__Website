Normal Paediatric Anatomy

Page 5.15.1 · Paediatric MRI · imaging page

In a child normal is a sequence rather than a state, so a report that does not place the knee in it has not described it.

Normal Paediatric Anatomy

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A child's knee is a joint under construction, so every structure in it has an appearance that belongs to a stage rather than to the structure. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

The bone ends begin as cartilage and ossify from secondary centres, so what is bone in an adult is cartilage here and shows as cartilage. 5.5.7 covers the cartilage.

The physis separates the metaphysis from the epiphysis and is present until it closes. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

It matters because an appearance correct for one stage is abnormal at another, and most error in this chapter is a stage read as a lesion. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

How It Is Acquired

Image the whole physis and both secondary centres, because their state is what places the knee in the sequence. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

Use sequences that separate cartilage from fluid, since the unossified epiphysis is cartilage and an effusion is not. 5.2.15 covers proton density imaging.

Include an unsuppressed T1 weighted sequence, because marrow distribution is a developmental finding in its own right. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

Compare with the other knee where the question is whether an appearance is normal, since development is broadly symmetrical. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

Keep the study short, because movement degrades a child's images more often than anything else does. 5.2.19 covers the artefacts.

What Normal Looks Like

An open physis with its own signal, irregular in outline and continuous across the bone. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Epiphyseal cartilage where an adult has subchondral bone, with the expected cartilage signal rather than fluid signal. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

Red marrow filling the metaphyses and darker than adult marrow on unsuppressed sequences. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

Irregular ossification at the margin of a secondary centre, which is a front of ossification rather than a fragment. 5.15.3 covers paediatric OCD.

Thicker articular cartilage than an adult's, uniformly so across the joint. 5.5.7 covers the cartilage.

What Abnormal Looks Like

A line crossing the physis, which development does not produce. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Marrow that replaces rather than fills, which is distinguished by distribution and by asymmetry. 5.13.7 covers bone tumours.

An avulsed bony fragment at a ligament attachment, since in a child the bone often fails before the ligament. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Cartilage that varies in thickness across one surface, where normal paediatric cartilage is thick and even. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

Key Learning Points

  • Every appearance in a child's knee belongs to a stage.
  • What is bone in an adult may still be cartilage here.
  • The physis is present until it closes and has its own signal.
  • Red marrow fills the metaphyses and is normal.
  • Most paediatric error is a stage read as a lesion.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer treats the child's age as part of the image rather than as background information.

The expected points are the secondary centres, the open physis, the unossified epiphysis, the red marrow, and the comparison with the other side.

Say that the report should state the skeletal stage it is reading against. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Say that symmetry is the most useful single test of whether an appearance is developmental. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

Say that the thicker cartilage of a child changes what counts as thinning. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging shows the growing knee better than any other modality, because the structures that matter most in a child are the ones it resolves and a radiograph does not.

Its best contribution is the unossified cartilage, which carries the epiphysis and the trochlear shape and is invisible on a film. 5.12.1 covers the trochlear dysplasia.

It is limited by the reader rather than by the instrument, since the appearances are well described and the stage is rarely stated. 5.1.6 covers the imaging pitfalls.

It is limited again because the reference against which a finding is judged is the child's own developmental stage, and a report that omits it leaves the next reader without the comparison. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no age, cartilage thickness, physeal width or ossification timing appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The modality resolves what matters most in a child.
  • The unossified cartilage carries the epiphysis and the trochlear shape.
  • A radiograph cannot show it.
  • The limit is the reader rather than the instrument.
  • A report that omits the stage leaves the next reader without the comparison.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the question a child's knee study answers is whether anything present threatens growth, and everything else is secondary to that.

An injury at or across the physis changes the plan because the consequence is the limb's future shape. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

A ligamentous question in a child becomes a bony question, since the attachment usually fails before the ligament. 5.15.5 covers paediatric cruciate injury.

An appearance that is developmental needs no plan at all, and recognising it is therefore worth as much as recognising a lesion. 5.15.7 covers developmental variants.

The examination carries the findings a scan of an unloaded knee cannot, which in a child includes tolerance, gait and the contralateral comparison. 4.19.1 covers the growth plate assessment.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading an adult's normal onto a child's knee. 5.5.4 covers the paediatric protocol.

An ossification front is reported as a fragment. 5.15.3 covers paediatric OCD.

Red marrow is reported as infiltration. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

The thicker paediatric cartilage is reported as swollen or as a lesion. 5.11.1 covers the normal cartilage.

The physis is reported as a fracture, which is the single most consequential version of this error. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Key Learning Points

  • An adult's normal is read onto a child's knee.
  • An ossification front is reported as a fragment.
  • Red marrow is reported as infiltration.
  • Thick paediatric cartilage is reported as a lesion.
  • The physis is reported as a fracture.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the child's knee arrives with a report written by somebody who did not know the child's skeletal stage, and the first task is to establish it.

Stage is not age, and the two diverge enough that neither substitutes for the other, so the films rather than the date of birth settle it. 5.3.1 covers the AP view.

The asymmetry of the two errors governs practice here. Over-calling leads to investigation and sometimes an operation on a knee that would have matured normally; under-calling can cost growth, and the two are not equivalent. 5.1.4 covers the imaging pathways.

A single study of a growing knee is a frame from a sequence, so a repeat study later answers questions the first cannot, which makes the interval part of the investigation rather than a delay. 5.1.3 covers choosing the correct investigation.

The useful paediatric report names the stage, states which appearances it regards as developmental, and says what it is not calling. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

Key Learning Points

  • Stage is not age and the films settle it.
  • Over-calling and under-calling do not cost the same thing.
  • One study of a growing knee is a frame from a sequence.
  • The interval is part of the investigation rather than a delay.
  • A useful report says what it is not calling.
