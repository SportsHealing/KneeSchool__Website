Image Formation

Page 5.2.2 · Imaging Physics for Knee Specialists · imaging page

How absorption becomes a picture, which is why bone is white and a meniscus is not there at all.

Image Formation

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

A radiograph records how much of the beam reached the detector at each point, so it maps absorption rather than anatomy. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

Dense tissue absorbs more and leaves less beam, which is why cortical bone is the brightest structure on a knee radiograph. 5.3.1 covers the anteroposterior view.

It matters because tissues that absorb similarly cannot be separated however good the image is, and most of the knee's soft tissue falls in that group. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

How It Is Acquired

Absorption depends on the tissue's density and on its atomic composition, which is why calcium separates so sharply from everything else. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

Absorption also depends on the beam's energy, so the same knee at a different tube voltage gives a different contrast. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

The detector converts the transmitted beam into a signal, and a digital detector responds over a far wider range than film did. 5.2.3 covers the radiation dose.

The displayed image is then rescaled from that signal, which separates what was acquired from what is seen. 5.2.4 covers scatter.

Everything in the path lies along the beam, so the image is a projection and every structure is superimposed on the ones behind it. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Key Learning Points

  • A radiograph maps absorption rather than anatomy.
  • Absorption depends on density, composition and beam energy.
  • Calcium separates sharply and soft tissues do not.
  • The displayed image is rescaled from what was acquired.
  • Everything along the beam is superimposed.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer explains why a radiograph shows bone well and nothing else well.

The expected points are absorption by density and composition, the energy dependence, the detector, the rescaling, and the projection.

Say that superimposition is the reason two views are standard. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

Say that soft tissue contrast is a physical limit rather than a technique failure. 5.5.1 covers the standard protocol.

Say that the displayed contrast is adjustable and the acquired information is not. 5.2.3 covers the radiation dose.

How Good It Is

Radiography delivers bone architecture and joint space better than any other modality and soft tissue worse than all of them.

Its best contribution is the loaded joint space and the cortical outline, neither of which the cross sectional modalities give as directly. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

It is limited by projection, so a three dimensional relationship has to be inferred from two flat views. 5.16.8 covers three dimensional reconstructions.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no absorption coefficients appear either: those are tabulated physical quantities and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Superimposition is why two views are standard.
  • Poor soft tissue contrast is a physical limit, not a technique failure.
  • Displayed contrast is adjustable; acquired information is not.
  • The loaded joint space is radiography's own contribution.
  • A three dimensional relationship has to be inferred from two flat views.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the projection is what makes a radiograph both useful and treacherous.

A weight bearing view answers a question no supine cross sectional study answers, which is what the joint does under load. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Alignment is a projection measurement and is correct only when the limb was positioned correctly, so the radiograph's value depends on the radiographer. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Rotation changes the apparent joint space, which is why a serial comparison needs a reproducible position rather than a similar one. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

A displayed image can be rescaled to look adequate while carrying less information than it appears to, so the acquisition matters more than the appearance. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading an absent finding as absent anatomy, when the tissue simply does not absorb differently. 5.1.6 covers imaging pitfalls.

Rotation is read as a change in joint space. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

A supine film is compared with a standing one. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Superimposed structures produce a line that is read as a fracture. 5.3.13 covers femoral condyle fractures.

Post-processing makes an under-exposed study look normal. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

Key Learning Points

  • An absent finding is read as absent anatomy.
  • Rotation is read as a change in joint space.
  • A supine film is compared with a standing one.
  • Superimposition produces lines read as fractures.
  • Post-processing hides an inadequate acquisition.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level the gap between the acquired data and the displayed image is the part of this that causes real error.

Digital post-processing is applied per department and per vendor, so the same knee looks different in two hospitals and a serial comparison across them compares processing as much as pathology. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

The processing is not recorded where the reader looks, which makes it an uncontrolled variable in every quantitative radiographic measure. 5.20.8 covers common reporting errors.

Joint space measured on a projection depends on beam angle, limb rotation and flexion, and the published measurement conventions each fix some of those and not others. 5.21.5 covers the osteoarthritis imaging literature.

Automated grading inherits all of it, since a model trained on one department's processing and positioning meets another's. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in imaging.

The practical conclusion is that radiographic measurement is a protocol question rather than an imaging one, which is why the view pages in chapter 5.3 carry the technique in detail. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Key Learning Points

  • Post-processing differs per department and per vendor.
  • The processing is not recorded where the reader looks.
  • Projected joint space depends on beam angle, rotation and flexion.
  • The published conventions each fix some of those variables and not others.
  • Radiographic measurement is a protocol question.
