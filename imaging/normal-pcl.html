Normal PCL

Page 5.8.1 · PCL MRI · imaging page

The easiest ligament in the knee to see, which is why the rest of the chapter is about what seeing it does not tell you.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Normal PCL

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

The posterior cruciate is the thickest ligament in the knee and runs from the medial femoral condyle to the back of the tibia. 2.8.1 covers the gross anatomy.

It is uniformly dark on every sequence and continuous on almost every study, which makes it the most reliably visualised ligament there is. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

It matters because that reliability sets up the chapter's problem: the ligament is easy to see and its competence is not something an image reports. 3.8.2 covers the posterior stability.

How It Is Acquired

Read it on the sagittal images along its whole length, where its curve is seen in one plane. 2.8.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

Confirm the tibial attachment on coronal and axial images, since an avulsion there is a bony injury. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Identify the meniscofemoral ligaments beside it, which are normal structures mistaken for fragments or for tears. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Note the knee's position, because the ligament's curve changes with flexion and the degree of curve is used as a sign. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

Treat the ligament as the study's internal control: if it is not well seen, the study's quality is the first question. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

What Normal Looks Like

A thick, uniformly dark band with a smooth curve from femur to tibia. 2.8.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

Two functional bundles that are not separately resolved on a standard study. 2.8.2 covers the AL bundle.

A broad tibial attachment below the joint line, with no marrow change behind it. 2.8.1 covers the gross anatomy.

Meniscofemoral ligaments running close to it, thin and dark, continuing to the lateral meniscus. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

A curve that flattens as the knee extends and deepens as it flexes, which is position rather than pathology. 3.8.3 covers the bundle function.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Thickening with internal signal, where the normal ligament is thin walled and uniform. 5.8.2 covers partial PCL tears.

Discontinuity, which is conspicuous because the normal ligament is so uniform. 5.8.3 covers the complete PCL tears.

A bony fragment at the tibial attachment lifted with the ligament. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Marrow change at the anterior tibia or the femoral condyle, which records the mechanism. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

An abnormal curve in a knee positioned normally, which is a secondary sign and not a structural one. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

Key Learning Points

  • The posterior cruciate is the thickest ligament in the knee.
  • It is uniformly dark and visualised on almost every study.
  • Its curve changes with knee position.
  • The meniscofemoral ligaments run beside it and are mistaken for pathology.
  • Being easy to see does not make its competence visible.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer gives the appearance, then the limit of what the appearance means.

The expected points are the uniform dark signal, the sagittal course, the tibial attachment, the meniscofemoral ligaments, and the position dependence of the curve.

Say that the ligament is the study's internal control for quality. 5.5.9 covers the ligaments.

Say that the two bundles are a functional description rather than an imaging one. 2.8.3 covers the PM bundle.

Say that posterior laxity is measured clinically and not on this study. 4.11.2 covers the posterior drawer test.

What Normal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that the ligament's uniformity is what makes any internal signal conspicuous, and conspicuous is not the same as significant.

The ligament is thick and avascular through much of its substance, so it carries little internal signal when healthy. 2.8.4 covers the blood supply.

Its bundles tighten at different parts of the arc, which is why a single static appearance cannot report which part is competent. 3.8.4 covers the AL bundle mechanics.

Its curve is a function of tibial position relative to the femur, so the curve reports where the tibia was sitting rather than what the ligament can resist. 3.8.6 covers the posterior drawer mechanics.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging identifies this ligament more consistently than any other in the knee, and reports its function less well than it reports any other's.

Its best contribution is the normal study, since a well seen uniform ligament makes a structural injury unlikely. 2.8.5 covers the MRI anatomy.

It is limited because the ligament heals in continuity, so an appearance that is continuous does not establish that the ligament is competent. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

It is limited again because the knee is unloaded and positioned for the scanner, and posterior translation is what the examination measures. 4.11.3 covers the quadriceps active test.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no ligament thickness, translation distance, grade or flexion angle appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The ligament is identified more consistently than any other in the knee.
  • Its function is reported less well than any other's.
  • A well seen uniform ligament makes structural injury unlikely.
  • The ligament heals in continuity, so continuity is not competence.
  • Posterior translation is what the examination measures.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the posterior cruciate's appearance rarely decides anything on its own, and what it does is point at the structures that do.

An isolated injury to this ligament is uncommon, so finding one is a reason to examine the rest of the knee rather than to plan around it. 4.11.7 covers the combined ligament injury assessment.

The posterolateral corner is the structure that changes the operation when it is injured alongside. 5.10.3 covers PLC injuries.

The posteromedial structures do the same from the other side. 5.9.5 covers posteromedial corner injuries.

Posterior laxity graded clinically, not the ligament's appearance, is what the decision rests on, and the two can disagree. 4.11.6 covers chronic PCL deficiency.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reporting the meniscofemoral ligaments as a tear or a fragment. 2.6.3 covers the meniscofemoral ligaments.

An abnormal curve is reported as laxity when the knee was positioned in flexion. 4.11.1 covers the posterior sag sign.

The ligament is reported in isolation, so the corner injuries that matter are not addressed. 5.10.4 covers the popliteofibular ligament.

A continuous ligament is reported as normal when the knee is lax. 5.8.4 covers chronic PCL injury.

Internal signal in a thick ligament is graded as a partial injury. 5.8.2 covers partial PCL tears.

Key Learning Points

  • The meniscofemoral ligaments are reported as pathology.
  • An abnormal curve in a flexed knee is reported as laxity.
  • The ligament is reported in isolation.
  • A continuous ligament is called normal in a lax knee.
  • Internal signal in a thick ligament is graded as partial injury.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this page exists to set a boundary that the rest of the chapter works inside.

The ligament's visibility creates a false sense of what the study settles, because the thing a referrer wants to know is whether the knee is stable and the thing the study shows is whether the ligament is continuous. 3.8.2 covers the posterior stability.

Those two answers coincide acutely and separate with time, which is why this chapter's reliability runs in the opposite direction to the cruciate chapter's. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

The ligament's own appearance is therefore the least interesting content of a posterior cruciate report, and the secondary signs and the associated structures are the most. 5.20.1 covers structured reporting.

An examination under anaesthesia answers the question the scan raises, and no appearance on an unloaded study replaces it. 4.14.6 covers the examination under anaesthesia.

Key Learning Points

  • Visibility creates a false sense of what the study settles.
  • The referrer wants stability and the study shows continuity.
  • The two answers coincide acutely and separate with time.
  • Reliability runs opposite to the cruciate chapter's.
  • The ligament's appearance is the least interesting part of its report.
