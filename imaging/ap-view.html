AP View

Page 5.3.1 · Plain Radiography · imaging page

The first radiograph of a knee, and the one whose value depends most on how the limb was positioned.

AP View

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

An anteroposterior view projects the knee from front to back, showing both compartments, the joint line and the cortical outlines. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

Taken standing it loads the joint, so the space between femur and tibia reflects what the compartment does under weight. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

It matters because the compartment's joint space is the measurement most osteoarthritis assessment rests on, and that measurement is a projection. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

How It Is Acquired

Centre the beam on the joint line with the patella facing forwards, which is what controls rotation. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

Take it standing where the question is about the compartment, because a supine film unloads the joint. 5.18.5 covers weight bearing imaging.

Keep the knee extended, since flexion changes which part of the femoral condyle faces the tibia. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

Confirm the position from the image: the patella should sit central over the femur and the fibular head overlap should look the same between studies. 5.3.2 covers the lateral view.

Pair it with a lateral, because a single projection superimposes everything along the beam. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

What Normal Looks Like

Two compartments of similar height with parallel subchondral surfaces and a clear cortical margin.

A patella projected centrally over the distal femur, which indicates neutral rotation.

Smooth cortical outlines without a step, a break or a periosteal reaction. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

Trabecular detail visible through the metaphysis, which depends on the exposure being right. 5.2.1 covers the generation of X-rays.

What Abnormal Looks Like

Asymmetric joint space, with one compartment narrower than the other. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Osteophyte at the compartment margins. 5.3.18 covers osteophytes.

A cortical break, a depressed articular surface or a widened metaphysis. 5.3.11 covers tibial plateau fractures.

Subchondral sclerosis or cyst formation in the loaded compartment. 5.3.19 covers subchondral sclerosis.

Key Learning Points

  • The view shows both compartments, the joint line and the cortical outlines.
  • Standing loads the joint and a supine film does not.
  • Rotation is controlled by the patella facing forwards.
  • The position can be checked from the image itself.
  • A single projection superimposes everything along the beam.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer gives the positioning before the findings, because the positioning decides what the findings mean.

The expected points are the centring, the standing position, the extension, the rotation check, and the paired lateral.

Say that a supine film understates joint space loss. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Say that rotation changes the apparent joint space. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

Say that you would check the position on the image before measuring anything. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

What Abnormal Looks Like

The reasoning expected is that joint space on a radiograph is the gap between two bones, and cartilage is not imaged, so the space is an inference about cartilage from the distance between the surfaces it separates. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

That inference holds only if the surfaces are presented the same way each time, which is why rotation and flexion dominate the reproducibility. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Loading matters because cartilage and meniscus deform, so an unloaded space overstates what is left. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

Asymmetry between compartments is more informative than either compartment's absolute height, because it needs no calibration. 5.2.5 covers magnification.

How Good It Is

The standing anteroposterior view is the most informative single image of an osteoarthritic knee and the most position dependent.

Its best contribution is the loaded compartment comparison, which no supine cross sectional study provides. 5.18.5 covers weight bearing imaging.

It is limited by projection, by rotation and by flexion, and by cartilage being inferred rather than seen. 5.11.1 covers normal cartilage.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no joint space measurements or rotation tolerances appear either: those are published quantities tied to specific protocols and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • Joint space is an inference about cartilage, which is not imaged.
  • The inference holds only if the surfaces are presented the same way.
  • An unloaded space overstates what remains.
  • Asymmetry needs no calibration and absolute height does.
  • Most informative single image of an osteoarthritic knee, and most position dependent.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level this view is where the compartment question is answered and where a wrong answer sends a patient to the wrong operation.

Isolated medial compartment loss with preserved lateral space and a correctable deformity is the picture a unicompartmental replacement or an osteotomy is chosen on. 5.16.5 covers osteotomy planning.

Loss in both compartments changes the operation, so the view's reliability is the decision's reliability. 5.3.16 covers Kellgren-Lawrence grading.

A supine film in this role understates the loss, which is the commonest way the wrong operation gets considered. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

The alignment question needs a long film, since a short view cannot show the mechanical axis. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is assessing joint space on a supine film. 5.3.5 covers the Rosenberg view.

Rotation changes the apparent space and is read as a change in disease. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

A flexed knee projects a different part of the condyle and the space looks preserved. 5.3.4 covers the tunnel view.

Alignment is estimated from a short view. 5.3.6 covers long leg alignment films.

Serial studies from different departments are compared as though positioned identically. 5.2.2 covers the image formation.

Key Learning Points

  • Joint space is assessed on a supine film.
  • Rotation is read as a change in disease.
  • A flexed knee can make the space look preserved.
  • Alignment is estimated from a short view.
  • Serial studies are compared across uncontrolled positioning.

Fellowship Level

What Normal Looks Like

At fellowship level the reproducibility of this view is the limiting factor in every longitudinal knee measurement made from it.

Protocols exist that fix the flexion angle and the beam angle and use a positioning frame, and they are used in trials rather than in clinics. 5.21.5 covers the osteoarthritis imaging literature.

Without them, the same knee photographed twice gives joint space heights that differ by an amount comparable to a year of disease progression. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

That is why radiographic progression as an endpoint needs a standardised acquisition, and why clinic films cannot substitute for one. 5.1.7 covers evidence based imaging.

The position is not recorded on the image in any usable form, so a reader cannot correct for it or weight it. 5.20.6 covers reporting osteoarthritis.

Where It Misleads

The commonest fellowship level error is treating a sequence of clinic films as a progression measurement. 5.3.17 covers joint space narrowing.

A standardised protocol's thresholds are applied to a non-standardised film. 5.21.5 covers the osteoarthritis imaging literature.

The compartment is judged from the film and the symptoms are attributed to it, when the two correlate loosely. 5.1.2 covers clinical examination versus imaging.

Automated grading is applied across departments with different positioning. 5.18.8 covers artificial intelligence in imaging.

Weight bearing computed tomography measures the same thing without the projection and is not yet in the pathway. 5.18.5 covers weight bearing imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • Standardised acquisition protocols exist and are used in trials, not clinics.
  • Repeat films differ by about as much as a year of progression.
  • Clinic films cannot substitute for a standardised acquisition.
  • The position is not recorded in any usable form.
  • Compartment appearance and symptoms correlate loosely.
