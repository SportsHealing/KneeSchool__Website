Paediatric ACL Injury

Page 5.15.5 · Paediatric MRI · imaging page

In a child the bone often fails before the ligament, so the first question is which of the two gave way.

Paediatric ACL Injury

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

What It Shows

The same force that tears an adult's cruciate can avulse the bone at its attachment in a child, because the growing bone is the weaker link. 2.2.3 covers the tibial spine.

So a paediatric knee with a cruciate mechanism is read for a bony injury first and a ligamentous one second. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

It matters because the two injuries look different, are treated differently, and one of them is visible on a radiograph while the other is not. 4.19.2 covers the paediatric cruciate examination.

How It Is Acquired

Read the tibial attachment before the ligament, since that is where the bone fails. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

Assess the fragment's position and whether it is rotated, because a displaced fragment is a mechanical problem. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

Read the physis in both bones, because a cruciate mechanism in a child can injure the plate as well. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Look for the secondary signs that accompany a cruciate injury, since they apply whichever structure failed. 5.7.4 covers acute ACL injury.

Read the menisci, as the association that holds in adults holds here too. 5.6.4 covers the vertical tears.

What Normal Looks Like

A continuous cruciate running from its femoral to its tibial attachment, with the paediatric ligament's expected appearance. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

An intact tibial spine with its attachment undisturbed. 2.2.3 covers the tibial spine.

An open physis with no widening and no line crossing it. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Marrow that is uniformly red in the metaphyses, which is a developmental finding rather than injury. 5.2.13 covers T1 imaging.

What Abnormal Looks Like

An avulsed fragment at the tibial attachment, lifted with the ligament still attached to it. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

A midsubstance tear of the ligament, which becomes the usual pattern as maturity approaches. 5.7.3 covers the complete tears.

Physeal widening or a line through the plate alongside either injury. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

A bone bruise at the lateral femoral condyle or the posterolateral tibia, which accompanies the mechanism. 5.13.1 covers bone marrow oedema.

A meniscal tear, especially a peripheral one, which changes the operation. 5.6.10 covers the ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • The growing bone is the weaker link, so the attachment may fail first.
  • The tibial attachment is read before the ligament.
  • An avulsion is visible on a radiograph and a midsubstance tear is not.
  • The physis is read in both bones.
  • Secondary signs apply whichever structure failed.

MRCS Level

How It Is Acquired

In a viva the expected answer gives the avulsion before the tear and explains why.

The expected points are the bone as the weaker link, the tibial attachment, the fragment's displacement, the physis, and the associated meniscal injury.

Say that the pattern shifts towards the adult pattern as the skeleton matures. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

Say that a displaced fragment can block extension, which is a mechanical finding rather than an imaging one. 4.19.2 covers the paediatric cruciate examination.

Say that the state of the physis constrains any reconstruction. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

How Good It Is

Magnetic resonance imaging answers the question the radiograph raises, which is what is attached to the fragment and what else was injured.

Its best contribution is the soft tissue around a bony injury already seen on the film, including whether the ligament itself is intact. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

Its second contribution is the physis, which the film cannot show and which decides the approach. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

It is limited because the paediatric ligament's appearance is less established than the adult's, so a partial injury in a child is reported with less confidence than the same words imply. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

It is limited again because instability is a movement and this is a static study of an unloaded knee, so the examination carries the finding that decides treatment. 4.19.2 covers the paediatric cruciate examination.

No figures appear for accuracy on this page, under decision 028, and no age, fragment size, displacement distance, physeal width or laxity value appears either: those are published quantities measured differently between studies and this environment cannot check them.

Key Learning Points

  • The modality answers the question the radiograph raises.
  • It shows whether the ligament is intact behind a bony injury.
  • The physis decides the approach and the film cannot show it.
  • A partial injury in a child is reported with less confidence than it sounds.
  • Instability is a movement and this study is static and unloaded.

FRCS Level

How Good It Is

At decision level the physis governs the operation, so the report describes the plate alongside the injury rather than after it.

An avulsion with its fragment reduced and the ligament intact is a different operation from a midsubstance tear in the same knee. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

A reconstruction in a knee with an open plate is planned around the plate, so the report gives its state and its relationship to the attachment sites. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

An associated peripheral meniscal tear changes the operation and is the finding most often added late. 5.6.10 covers the ramp lesions.

The decision to reconstruct now or to wait is made on instability and on skeletal stage together, and imaging contributes the second of those. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

Where It Misleads

The commonest error is reading the ligament and not the attachment. 5.7.1 covers the normal ACL.

A fragment is seen and the ligament is assumed torn, or assumed intact. 5.3.14 covers avulsion injuries.

The physis is not described, so the surgeon plans from the operation note of a different patient. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

Normal paediatric ligament signal is reported as a partial tear. 5.7.2 covers the partial tears.

A peripheral meniscal tear is missed in a study read for the cruciate. 5.6.10 covers the ramp lesions.

Key Learning Points

  • The ligament is read and the attachment is not.
  • A fragment is seen and the ligament's state is assumed.
  • The physis is not described.
  • Normal paediatric ligament signal is called a partial tear.
  • A peripheral meniscal tear is missed in a cruciate study.

Fellowship Level

Where It Misleads

At fellowship level this injury is where the chapter's organising point is most expensive, because the structure that failed and the structure at risk are different structures.

A knee can have an intact ligament, a reduced fragment and a plate that will arrest, and a report organised around the ligament will mention the first and not the third. 5.15.2 covers the growth plates.

The pattern shifts with maturity rather than at a point, so the same mechanism in two children of similar age produces different injuries and neither is atypical. 5.15.1 covers the normal paediatric anatomy.

The association with meniscal injury is the same as in the adult knee and the threshold for looking is lower here, because the meniscus is irreplaceable and the knee has decades to use it. 5.6.13 covers meniscal repair imaging.

The useful report names the attachment, the ligament, the physis in both bones, the menisci and the secondary signs, and says which of them it is confident about. 5.20.3 covers reporting ACL injuries.

Key Learning Points

  • The structure that failed and the structure at risk can differ.
  • A report organised around the ligament can omit the plate.
  • The pattern shifts with maturity rather than at a point.
  • Two children of similar age can sustain different injuries.
  • The meniscus is irreplaceable and the knee has decades to use it.
