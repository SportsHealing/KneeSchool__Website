Surgical Anatomy

Page 2.6.6 · Lateral Meniscus · anatomy page

The lateral compartment through the arthroscope: easier to enter than the medial, harder to see at the back, and with a tendon in the way.

Surgical Anatomy

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

Structure and Location

The lateral compartment is reached through the same two anterior portals as the medial one, with the camera and instruments swapped between them.

It opens more readily than the medial compartment, because the lateral collateral ligament is longer and the structures are less tethered. Putting the knee into the figure of four position opens it further.

From the front, the anterior horn, the body and the popliteus hiatus are seen. The posterior horn and the region behind the hiatus are the hardest parts to see and reach.

Relations

The popliteus tendon crosses the posterolateral periphery and is both a landmark and an obstacle.

Outside the posterolateral capsule lie the posterolateral corner structures, and beyond the posterior capsule the popliteal vessels. The common peroneal nerve is further distal, around the fibular neck.

The lateral inferior genicular artery runs just outside the peripheral border.

Function

Easier access is not the same as easier surgery. The compartment opens, but the posterolateral region is deep, the tendon is in the way and the meniscus is mobile.

The figure of four position opens the compartment by applying varus with the knee flexed, which is the standard manoeuvre.

Clinical Relevance

The structure at risk from instruments directed posterolaterally is the popliteal artery behind the posterior capsule, and the common peroneal nerve further out.

A tear at the hiatus may be unrepairable because there is no capsule to fix to, which is an anatomical limit rather than a technical one.

Technique belongs to 7.8 onwards.

Key Learning Points

  • The lateral compartment opens more readily than the medial, and the figure of four opens it further.
  • The popliteus tendon is both a landmark and an obstacle at the posterolateral periphery.
  • Easier access is not easier surgery; the posterolateral region is deep and the meniscus mobile.
  • The popliteal artery lies behind the posterior capsule and the peroneal nerve further out.

MRCS Level

Structure and Location

The figure of four position flexes the knee and applies varus, opening the lateral compartment and bringing the posterolateral periphery into view. It is the single most useful manoeuvre on this side.

The diagnostic sequence runs anterior horn, body, hiatus, posterior horn, and the posterior horn often needs the camera in the anteromedial portal.

An accessory posterolateral portal can be made for posterior horn work. It sits behind the lateral collateral ligament and in front of the biceps femoris tendon, above the joint line, and the interval matters because the common peroneal nerve lies behind the biceps.

A transnotch view, passing the arthroscope through the notch, gives a view of the posterior compartment and is how posterior root and ramp pathology is seen on both sides.

Relations

The popliteus tendon separates the meniscus from the capsule at the hiatus. Instruments passed there arrive inside the tendon sheath, and sutures can capture the tendon.

Behind the posterolateral capsule: the posterolateral corner structures, the lateral inferior genicular artery, and more posteriorly the popliteal vessels.

The common peroneal nerve is a relation of the fibular neck rather than of the compartment, which is why a posterolateral portal placed in front of the biceps tendon and above the joint line is safe and one placed lower is not.

The anterior horn lies close to the anterior cruciate footprint, so instruments working anteriorly are near both.

Blood Supply and Innervation

The perimeniscal plexus is interrupted at the hiatus, so the tissue a repair suture needs is absent there. That is the anatomical reason hiatal tears behave badly.

The lateral inferior genicular artery runs circumferentially just peripheral to the meniscus and is at risk from sutures passed outside the capsule.

The sensory structure at risk anteriorly is the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve at the anteromedial portal rather than anything lateral.

Function

Access is good and visualisation of the posterolateral corner of the compartment is poor. Those two facts together are why pathology there is missed rather than mistreated.

Mobility of the meniscus means probing is part of the examination, not an optional extra. A meniscus that looks normal and translates excessively has a fascicle lesion.

The transnotch view exists because the posterior horns and roots cannot be assessed properly from the front.

Clinical Relevance

Use the figure of four position routinely. A lateral compartment examined in extension has not been examined.

Probe the lateral meniscus for mobility in every diagnostic arthroscopy, because the hypermobility lesion is otherwise invisible.

Place an accessory posterolateral portal in front of the biceps tendon and above the joint line, under needle localisation, because the common peroneal nerve is behind the biceps.

Avoid capturing the popliteus tendon with sutures at the hiatus, and expect a tear there to be difficult or impossible to repair.

Key Learning Points

  • The figure of four is the single most useful manoeuvre on this side.
  • An accessory posterolateral portal goes in front of the biceps tendon and above the joint line.
  • The plexus is interrupted at the hiatus, which is why hiatal tears behave badly.
  • Probing for mobility is part of the examination, because hypermobility is otherwise invisible.
  • The transnotch view exists because posterior horns and roots cannot be assessed from the front.

FRCS Level

Relations

The posterolateral periphery is the least accessible and least repairable part of either meniscus. No capsule at the hiatus, interrupted vascularity, a tendon in the way, and the popliteal vessels not far behind.

Planning surgery there means deciding in advance which of those four is the binding constraint in that knee.

Function

Because the lateral compartment depends on its meniscus for congruence, the threshold for difficult preservation surgery should be lower here than medially. The access is harder and the stakes are higher, which is an uncomfortable combination.

Where preservation is impossible, the consequence is worse than on the medial side, and that should be part of the consent rather than a later discovery.

Clinical Relevance

In a young patient facing lateral meniscectomy, discuss the long term consequence explicitly and consider whether a preservation attempt, including allograft later, is part of the plan.

In discoid meniscus surgery, plan for both reshaping and stabilisation, and assess stability after reshaping rather than before.

For posterolateral access, needle localise and keep in front of the biceps tendon. The nerve is the reason and estimating its position is not acceptable.

Published portal positions and safe distances are all quantitative and none appears here. On a surgical anatomy page that is the most visible cost of the figure policy, and the register is where a reviewer should add them.

Key Learning Points

  • Four constraints at the posterolateral periphery: no capsule, interrupted vascularity, the tendon, the vessels.
  • The threshold for difficult preservation surgery should be lower laterally, where the stakes are higher.
  • Assess stability after reshaping a discoid meniscus, not before.
  • Needle localise a posterolateral portal and keep in front of the biceps tendon.

Fellowship Level

Clinical Relevance

Three situations where lateral compartment access decides the outcome.

The posterolateral tear at the hiatus. There is no capsule to repair to and the vascular tissue is interrupted, so the realistic options are a limited resection, a repair to the fascicles and sheath, or acceptance. Choosing between them is a judgement about tissue made with the probe, and it should be made before any tissue is removed.

The discoid meniscus in a child or adolescent. Saucerisation reshapes and does not stabilise, and the peripheral attachment is abnormal in a substantial proportion. The access problem is that assessing stability needs the figure of four position, a probe and often a transnotch view, in a small knee. Section 10 owns the management.

Lateral allograft transplantation. Both horns are fixed in the intercondylar area, which the cruciates share, and the peripheral attachment has to be made in a compartment whose posterolateral quadrant is the hardest to reach. The operation is an access problem before it is a graft problem.

In all three, nothing about the compartment has changed from the medical student page. What changes is that the limiting factor is what can be reached.

Key Learning Points

  • At the hiatus the options are limited resection, repair to fascicles and sheath, or acceptance.
  • Make that judgement with the probe before any tissue is removed.
  • Saucerisation reshapes and does not stabilise; peripheral attachment is often abnormal.
  • Lateral allograft transplantation is an access problem before it is a graft problem.
