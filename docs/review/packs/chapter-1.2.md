**1.2.1 Bones of the Knee**

- The femur ends in two condyles, with the trochlear groove in front and the notch behind.
- The tibial plateau is broad and gently dished, so it does not grip the femur.
- The patella bears the thickest articular cartilage in the body, on two facets.
- The patella acts as a pulley, increasing the extensor moment arm, most in extension.
- Trochlear shape governs patellar tracking; tibial slope influences translation.

**1.2.2 Articular Cartilage**

- Cartilage is roughly seventy to eighty per cent water, with type two collagen dominating dry weight.
- It is zonally organised, with collagen parallel at the surface and perpendicular at depth.
- Behaviour is biphasic: interstitial fluid pressurisation bears most of the applied load.
- It is avascular, aneural and alymphatic, so repair is minimal and damage is painless.
- Nutrition depends on cyclical loading, which argues against prolonged immobilisation.

**1.2.3 Menisci**

- Medial meniscus is larger and more open in shape than the lateral, and covers a smaller proportion of its tibial plateau
- Lateral meniscus is more nearly circular and covers a greater proportion of its plateau
- Medial meniscus is attached to the deep fibres of the medial collateral ligament and to the capsule, limiting excursion
- Lateral meniscus is separated from the capsule posterolaterally by the popliteal hiatus and is more mobile
- Transverse ligament connects the two anterior horns
- Meniscofemoral ligaments run from the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus to the medial femoral condyle
- Vascular supply arises from the medial and lateral geniculate arteries via a perimeniscal capillary plexus
- Vascular penetration is limited to the peripheral portion, producing red, red-white and white zones
- Avascular inner zone is nourished by diffusion from synovial fluid
- Mechanoreceptors concentrated at the horns contribute to joint proprioception
- Collagen is arranged predominantly in circumferential bundles with radial tie fibres
- Circumferential bundles resist extrusion in tension, termed hoop stress, anchored by the roots
- Meniscal loss reduces contact area and raises peak contact stress on articular cartilage
- Both menisci translate posteriorly in flexion, the lateral further than the medial
- Composition is largely water with type one collagen dominating the dry weight
- Proteoglycan content is low relative to articular cartilage
- A small number of people have a discoid lateral meniscus
- Degenerate tears may occur without a single causative event
- Meniscal tears are found on imaging in asymptomatic knees
- MRI is the standard investigation for suspected meniscal injury

**1.2.4 Ligaments**

- The cruciates are intracapsular but extrasynovial; the lateral collateral is extracapsular.
- Cruciates are primary restraints to anteroposterior translation, collaterals to angulation.
- Fibres within each ligament tighten at different flexion angles.
- Mechanoreceptors provide proprioception, and losing it causes deficit beyond laxity.
- Intra-articular position and synovial fluid exposure explain poor cruciate healing.

**1.2.5 Tendons**

- The quadriceps tendon inserts on the patella; the patellar tendon runs to the tibial tubercle.
- The retinacula can maintain limited active extension despite central tendon disruption.
- The patella increases the extensor moment arm, most in terminal extension.
- Failure anywhere in the chain produces extensor lag; the site varies with age.
- Tendon adapts over months while muscle adapts over weeks, which explains overload pain.

**1.2.6 Muscles Around the Knee**

- Rectus femoris is the only quadriceps head that also crosses the hip.
- The hamstrings act as agonists to the ACL by resisting anterior tibial translation.
- Popliteus unlocks the extended knee by internally rotating the tibia.
- Arthrogenic muscle inhibition causes rapid, reversible quadriceps weakness.
- Isolated quadriceps weakness points away from the knee and towards the nerve supply.

**1.2.7 Nerves and Blood Supply**

- The popliteal artery supplies the whole lower leg and is relatively fixed behind the knee.
- The periarticular network barely penetrates cartilage or inner meniscus, limiting healing.
- Articular innervation follows Hilton's law and carries proprioceptive afferents.
- Knee dislocation risks arterial injury; palpable pulses do not exclude it.
- The common fibular nerve at the fibular neck is the most commonly injured nerve here.

**1.2.8 Synovium**

- The synovium is one to three cells thick, with type A and type B synoviocytes.
- It reflects around the cruciates, making them intracapsular but extrasynovial.
- Type B cells secrete hyaluronan and lubricin; normal volume is one to four millilitres.
- Effusion within two hours implies haemarthrosis; over twenty four hours implies reaction.
- Aspiration distinguishes septic, crystal and inflammatory causes, and fat globules suggest fracture.

**1.2.9 Bursa**

- Bursae are synovium lined sacs at sites of friction, responding to irritation with fluid.
- The suprapatellar bursa communicates with the joint in most adults.
- Bursal swelling is circumscribed with preserved movement; an effusion is diffuse and restricts flexion.
- Septic bursitis is distinct from septic arthritis and differs in urgency and treatment.
- A popliteal cyst usually communicates with the joint and reflects intra-articular pathology.

**1.2.10 Growth Plates**

- The physis has reserve, proliferative, hypertrophic and calcification zones.
- Separation occurs through the mechanically weak hypertrophic zone.
- The distal femoral and proximal tibial physes give about sixty five per cent of limb length.
- Open physes are weaker than ligaments, so mechanism produces different injuries by age.
- Physeal cartilage is radiolucent, so a normal radiograph does not exclude injury.
