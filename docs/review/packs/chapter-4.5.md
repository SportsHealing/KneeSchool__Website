## Claims to confirm

The deepest tier's key learning points on each page, which are the chapter's substantive claims. Every other statement in the body needs the same check; these are where to start.

**4.5.1 Mechanical Axis Assessment**

- The alignment is the commonest reason a soft tissue question has a bony answer.
- Two competent reconstructions with a persisting complaint usually means alignment or slope.
- A stretched lateral reconstruction in a varus limb had the operations in the wrong order.
- After an osteotomy the question is whether the correction achieved the plan.
- Correcting a constitutional varus to a neutral axis is a judgement, not an obvious target.

**4.5.2 Varus Alignment**

- Varus alignment most often explains a failed lateral reconstruction and is usually missing from the first assessment.
- A stretched lateral graft with a varus thrust needs a correction, not another graft.
- With a stretched posterior cruciate graft the tibial slope is the finding most often missed.
- A thrust after an osteotomy is under correction or a ligament problem the correction exposed.
- Correcting a constitutional varus in an athlete has costs to set against the ligament gain.

**4.5.3 Valgus Alignment**

- A medial reconstruction that becomes lax over two years in a valgus limb is a creep problem.
- The same mechanism acts on a cruciate graft, and the correction is part of the revision.
- After a lateral arthroplasty or osteotomy the pre-operative films are needed.
- In an uncontrolled inflammatory arthritis a correction recurs.
- A valgus deformity is almost never attributable to a single injury.

**4.5.4 Flexion Deformity**

- A persisting deformity after reconstruction is a common cause of dissatisfaction and is rarely followed.
- A firm block with a reconstruction has a straightforward treatment and is often referred late.
- A soft contracture after reconstruction is arthrofibrosis, with a less certain result.
- A deformity with posterior cruciate deficiency makes every laxity measurement unreliable.
- The absence of any recorded extension figure is the commonest reason a stiffness claim cannot be assessed.

**4.5.5 Hyperextension**

- It is one of the few findings easy to reproduce exactly, which makes it good to follow.
- Increasing hyperextension after a corner reconstruction is clearer evidence of stretch than rotation is.
- A recurvatum preceding the injury is constitutional, and attribution matters.
- Hyperextension with posterior cruciate deficiency progresses, since each permits the other.
- In hypermobility, tensioning to the other side reproduces the hyperextension.

**4.5.6 Rotational Alignment**

- Malrotation is the diagnosis that was not considered, after several operations that could not have helped.
- Persisting pain after a tibial osteotomy may be a rotational malunion.
- An asymmetrical baseline torsion can produce a false positive dial test with real consequences.
- A reconstruction in an abnormal rotational position is tensioned wrongly.
- Pre-injury rotational alignment is almost never recorded, which complicates attribution.

**4.5.7 Dynamic Alignment**

- The thrust explains the failed reconstruction and is usually the finding nobody looked for.
- A stretched lateral reconstruction with a varus thrust needs a correction, not a graft.
- A thrust after an osteotomy is under correction or a ligament problem the correction exposed.
- An unrecovered nerve palsy resembles a thrust and is not ligamentous.
- A new thrust in a deficient knee is the clearest sign the compartment has changed.

**4.5.8 Limb Length Assessment**

- Limb length is most often relevant as something a previous operation changed and nobody recorded.
- A length complaint after arthroplasty is easier to manage with a raise than to revise.
- A fixed flexion deformity gains length from correction, which is the accurate way to frame it.
- In a multiply operated knee the length may limit what further surgery can achieve.
- A length difference attributed to an injury needs a pre-injury measurement that rarely exists.
