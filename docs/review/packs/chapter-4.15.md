## Claims to confirm

The deepest tier's key learning points on each page, which are the chapter's substantive claims. Every other statement in the body needs the same check; these are where to start.

**4.15.1 Patellar Tracking**

- A persisting abrupt deviation after reconstruction is a failed graft or an unaddressed bony abnormality.
- A central track with unchanged pain means two problems and one treatment.
- An over constrained reconstruction tracks medially and causes medial facet pain.
- After a tubercle osteotomy the comparison is with the intended result.
- The word maltracking carries no information about what was seen.

**4.15.2 J Sign**

- A persisting J sign after ligament reconstruction means a soft tissue operation for a bony problem.
- A resolved J sign with persisting instability suggests the instability was not patellar.
- Generalised hypermobility adds a contribution no bony correction fully addresses.
- After a tubercle osteotomy, persistence may be an incomplete correction.
- It is one of the few patellofemoral findings specific enough to carry medicolegal weight.

**4.15.3 Patellar Apprehension Test**

- The test separates two groups with patellar complaints who need opposite treatments.
- Anterior pain with no apprehension and a normal glide is a load problem.
- Apprehension after a successful reconstruction with a stable patella is a confidence problem.
- Apprehension on medial displacement means over constraint.
- Being a behaviour, it is weak medicolegal evidence; the dislocation history is not.

**4.15.4 Patellar Glide**

- After a reconstruction an increased lateral glide is a clearer finding than any imaging.
- A reduced glide in both directions is over constraint, presenting as pain and stiffness.
- An increased glide with no instability and no complaint needs nothing.
- After a lateral release the medial glide is increased for a surgical reason.
- In a hypermobile patient the tensioning target is a deliberate decision.

**4.15.5 Patellar Tilt**

- A previous lateral release with medial instability is an iatrogenic problem.
- A release with unchanged pain treated a problem that was not lateral tightness.
- Tight tilt, lateral facet change and a valgus limb are one mechanical story.
- An unaddressed tight lateral side loads a medial reconstruction and stretches it.
- The dislocation history and imaging carry a patellofemoral opinion, not the tilt.

**4.15.6 Dynamic Tracking**

- It explains the patellofemoral patient who has had everything else done.
- Normal imaging and a normal static examination with loaded pain is a control or load problem.
- Several procedures with persisting dynamic valgus means the proximal problem was never treated.
- An athlete needs the assessment at the sport's intensity.
- A correction adequate statically can be inadequate under load.

**4.15.7 MPFL Assessment**

- An increased glide with recurrent instability after reconstruction is a stretched or failed graft.
- A reduced glide with medial facet pain and stiffness is over constraint.
- A stable patella with persisting apprehension is a confidence problem.
- In a hypermobile patient the tensioning target has to be chosen deliberately and recorded.
- Recorded medial tenderness at the time of a dislocation is useful evidence one occurred.

**4.15.8 Trochlear Dysplasia Assessment**

- Unrecognised dysplasia most often explains a failed patellofemoral reconstruction.
- A corrected tubercle and a reconstructed ligament with persisting instability leaves the trochlea.
- Bilateral dysplasia with unilateral symptoms puts the other side's prognosis in the conversation.
- In a skeletally immature patient the trochlea is still developing.
- Dysplasia most often establishes a constitutional predisposition.

**4.15.9 Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome**

- The diagnosis arrives as a label on a patient who has had investigations and an operation.
- A normal arthroscopy with unchanged pain treated the wrong compartment.
- A lateral release with unchanged pain treated a problem that was not there.
- Persisting pain with no proximal contribution needs load and pain management, not another hypothesis.
- With no structural correlate, attribution to an injury is difficult and should be said plainly.

**4.15.10 Patellofemoral Arthritis**

- It is most often the later consequence of something treated earlier.
- Recurrent dislocations treated decades ago produce secondary change, which belongs in today's conversation.
- A correction can unload one facet and load another.
- Arthritis after a patellofemoral replacement is progression or malposition, separated by the tenderness.
- Attribution to a single injury is usually unsupportable without a documented dislocation or osteochondral injury.
