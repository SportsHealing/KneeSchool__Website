For Young Learners

Structure and Location

Put your hand on your knee and bend it. Feel the hard bump that moves under your fingers? That is your kneecap, and it is sliding in a groove as you bend.

Your knee is where two long bones meet. Coming down from your hip is the femur, the thigh bone, which is the longest bone in your body. Coming up from your ankle is the tibia, the shin bone. The kneecap sits at the front like a small shield.

There is a fourth bone too, the fibula. It is the thin one on the outside of your shin. You can feel the top of it as a small lump just below and to the outside of your knee. It does not carry much weight.

Everything else lives in the gaps. Tough cords called ligaments hold the bones together. Rubbery pads called menisci sit between them. A smooth white coating covers the bone ends. Muscles pull from above and below, and a thin lining makes the fluid that keeps it all slippery.

Function

Your knee does three things at the same time, and that combination is unusual.

It moves. From almost straight to bent right back, which is a bigger swing than most joints manage.

It carries weight. Your whole body, every step, thousands of times a day.

It stays steady. It does not fold sideways when you turn, even though nothing about the bone shapes stops it.

That last one is the clever bit. The bottom of the thigh bone is rounded, like two knuckles. The top of the shin bone is nearly flat. A round thing balanced on a flat thing should fall off, and yours does not, because everything around the bones is holding it in place.

That is the idea to take away from this page. In your knee, the bones set the shape and the soft parts do the work.

Clinical Relevance

Knowing the parts helps when something goes wrong, because people will use their names.

If a doctor or a physiotherapist mentions a ligament, a meniscus or cartilage, you now know roughly what they mean and where it sits. That makes the conversation easier, and it makes you a better person to have in the room.

The rest of this chapter takes each part in turn.

Key Learning Points

  • The knee is where the thigh bone and the shin bone meet, with the kneecap at the front.
  • The fibula sits on the outside and carries very little weight.
  • Ligaments, menisci, cartilage, muscles and a slippery lining fill the spaces.
  • The bones alone would slide apart, so the soft parts provide the stability.
  • The knee moves a long way, carries a lot, and stays steady, all at once.

If any of this sounds like something happening to you, speak to a parent, teacher, coach or doctor.
