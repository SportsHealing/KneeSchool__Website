For Patients

Curriculum Links

Lessons one to three map onto GCSE biology on cells, tissues, the skeleton and movement, and onto GCSE physical education on joints, movement and injury prevention.

Lesson two also covers A level physical education biomechanics on levers and mechanical advantage.

Lesson four fits scientific method and working scientifically at either level, and works equally well in a critical thinking or extended project context.

The page by page mapping is on the Curriculum Links page.

What This Covers

Four lessons, each designed to run in a single period without equipment beyond what a classroom or a sports hall already has.

Each plan names the pages it draws on, so the subject content is checkable before you teach it rather than taken on trust.

Lesson one. What the knee is made of. Tissue types and their jobs, built on 0.1.2, with a sorting activity on which tissue does what.

Lesson two. How a hinge that also rotates works. Joint movement built on 0.1.3 and 0.1.4, with the screw home observation and the chair standing task from 0.5.4.

Lesson three. Why young knees are different. Growth plates built on 0.1.5, with a discussion on training load in youth sport.

Lesson four. How we know what we know. Evidence built on 0.5.1, using the question of whether warming up prevents injury as the worked example.

How to Use It

Set the relevant site page as reading before the lesson. Each is around five hundred words.

Use the key learning points at the end of that page as the recall questions at the start of the lesson.

Run the practical, which in every case needs only a chair, a tape measure, a protractor or a phone.

Close with the question the lesson was built around, asked as an exit question.

The plans are described here rather than attached. No downloadable files exist yet, because the classroom materials in 0.7.2 have not been produced. Everything needed to teach these four lessons is nonetheless on the pages named above.

What to Watch For

Lesson three raises training load in youth sport, and in most classes somebody is carrying an injury or has recently had one. Keep the discussion general.

The practical tasks are observation and measurement only. Nobody should be loading a painful knee, stretching to a limit, or jumping from anything.

Do not let a measurement be read as a finding about a particular pupil. A knee that bends less far than the one next to it has not been diagnosed with anything.

If a pupil raises a knee problem of their own during a lesson, the answer is to have it looked at by a doctor or physiotherapist rather than discussed in class.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Do I need a science background to teach these? No. Each lesson draws on one or two pages written for a reading age of twelve to fourteen, and the subject content is all on those pages.

Q. Can I adapt them? Yes. They are described rather than scripted, and the site pages they draw on are free to read and link to.

Q. Is there an answer key? The key learning points at the end of each source page function as one. A separate answer sheet is part of the classroom materials that have not yet been produced.

Q. Can I use these with a club rather than a class? Lessons two and three work well with a sports team. Coaching Safely covers the coaching context specifically.

If a pupil describes knee pain that is not settling, a knee that locks or gives way, or any swelling after an injury, the right step is to have it assessed by a doctor or physiotherapist rather than managed in the lesson.
