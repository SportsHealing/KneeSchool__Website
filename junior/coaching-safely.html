Coaching Safely

Page 0.7.3 · For Teachers, Coaches and Parents · teacher_resource page

What a youth coach can do about knee injury risk, how training load works, and where the line sits between coaching and treating.

For Patients

What This Covers

Two things a coach has real influence over. How much load a young athlete carries, and what happens in the first few minutes after something goes wrong.

Neither of those is treatment. A coach is not expected to diagnose anything, and the most useful thing this page can do is make the boundary clear.

Load is the one variable a coach controls directly. Most knee complaints in youth sport are not sudden injuries. They build up over weeks in which the body was asked to do more than it had adapted to.

Curriculum Links

This material sits alongside GCSE and A level physical education on injury prevention, training principles and periodisation.

Coaches working towards a national governing body qualification will find it overlaps with the safeguarding and injury awareness units those awards require.

How to Use It

Four practices that are within a coach's control.

Watch total load, not your own sessions. A young athlete may be in a school team, a club team, a county squad and a physical education lesson in the same week. No single coach sees the total, and the total is what matters. Ask.

Change one thing at a time. Increases in volume, intensity and frequency all count as load. Raising two at once is where trouble tends to appear.

Protect rest. A week with no hard session in it is a training practice rather than a concession, and growing athletes need it more than adults do.

Warm up properly and the same way every time. Page 0.2.3 covers what a warm up is actually for.

When something happens in a session, three steps. Stop the athlete. Do not test the joint to see how bad it is. Tell a parent or guardian the same day, in writing if you can, describing what you saw rather than what you think it was.

What to Watch For

Five things mean a session ends and an assessment is arranged rather than a decision being made on the touchline.

A knee that gives way, or that the athlete says they cannot trust.

A knee that locks, or will not fully straighten.

Swelling that appears within a few hours of an injury.

Pain that keeps returning at the same point in a session, week after week.

An athlete who stops being able to do something they could do a month ago.

Two further points matter in youth sport specifically. A young athlete who is growing quickly often reports knee pain, and growth is never a reason to dismiss it without assessment. And a young athlete will routinely say they are fine because they want to play, which is not information a coach can act on.

Where the line sits. A coach manages load, technique and whether a session continues. Deciding what is wrong, how long it takes, and when an athlete returns to play is a clinical decision belonging to the clinician treating them.

Key Learning Points

  • Load across every team an athlete plays for is the variable a coach most influences.
  • Raise volume, intensity or frequency one at a time, and protect rest weeks.
  • After an incident: stop the athlete, do not test the joint, tell a parent the same day.
  • Giving way, locking, early swelling, repeating pain and lost ability all need assessment.
  • Return to play is a clinical decision, not a coaching one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Is knee pain in a growing athlete just growing pains? Growth does make knee pain more common at certain ages. That is a reason to have it assessed rather than a reason to dismiss it, because several conditions specific to the growing knee present exactly that way.

Q. Can I let an athlete play if they say the pain has gone? Not on that basis alone after an injury that needed assessment. Return to play is set by the clinician looking after them, and a young athlete will usually say they are ready.

Q. Should I stretch an athlete who has just hurt a knee? No. Do not move or test the joint to find out how bad it is. Stop the session and arrange an assessment.

Q. How do I find out what load an athlete is already carrying? Ask them and ask a parent, and ask at the start of a season rather than after a problem. Most young athletes have no idea their weekly total is unusual.

Any knee that gives way, locks, swells within hours of an injury, or hurts in the same place week after week needs assessing by a doctor or physiotherapist. If an athlete cannot bear weight at all, that is the same day rather than next week.
