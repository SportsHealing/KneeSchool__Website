Deep MCL Function

Page 3.9.4 · Medial Knee Biomechanics · biomechanics page

The short one, attached to the meniscus on its way past. It resists rotation and tethers the medial meniscus, which are two jobs the long ligament cannot do.

Deep MCL Function

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

A short structure close to a joint line is loaded by the bones turning on each other, where a long one is not.

A structure attached to something in the middle of its course tethers that thing as well as connecting its two ends.

So a short capsular ligament attached to a meniscus does three things: resists rotation, resists opening a little, and holds the meniscus in place.

At the Knee

The deep medial collateral ligament is a capsular layer running from the femur to the tibia, deep to the superficial ligament. 2.9.2 covers the anatomy.

It attaches to the medial meniscus in passing, which divides it into meniscofemoral and meniscotibial portions.

It contributes to rotational restraint rather than being a major valgus restraint. 3.9.2 covers rotational stability.

It tethers the medial meniscus, which is part of why the medial meniscus translates less than the lateral. 3.6.5 covers meniscal load sharing.

What Changes It

Flexion angle, which changes its tension.

Which portion is injured, meniscofemoral or meniscotibial.

Meniscal integrity, since the two are attached.

Rotation, which is what loads it most.

Clinical Relevance

An injury here produces rotational laxity rather than large valgus opening, which is why it can be missed. Section 4 covers the tests.

A meniscotibial injury can allow the medial meniscus to move abnormally, which links medial ligament injury to meniscal pathology. 3.6.10 covers extrusion.

The attachment to the meniscus is why the medial meniscus is less mobile and more often torn. 2.5.1 covers the anatomy.

Reconstruction of the medial side has to consider this layer as well as the superficial one. 3.9.8 covers the construct.

Key Learning Points

  • A short structure near the joint line is loaded by rotation.
  • The deep ligament attaches to the medial meniscus in passing.
  • That divides it into meniscofemoral and meniscotibial portions.
  • It contributes to rotational restraint rather than being a major valgus restraint.
  • It tethers the medial meniscus, which is why that meniscus translates less.

MRCS Level

The Principle

The layer's mechanical identity comes from its length and its meniscal attachment. Short fibres close to the joint line are loaded by rotation, and the meniscal attachment makes it a meniscal restraint as well as a joint one.

That dual role means an injury here has two consequences: rotational laxity and altered meniscal behaviour.

It also means the layer cannot be assessed separately from the meniscus on imaging or at arthroscopy.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching divides it into meniscofemoral and meniscotibial portions, with the meniscotibial portion shorter and more often implicated in meniscal extrusion. 3.6.10 covers extrusion.

Its contribution to valgus restraint is modest compared with the superficial ligament's. 3.9.3 covers that ligament.

Its contribution to external rotation restraint is larger, which is why isolated deep layer injury presents as rotatory rather than coronal laxity. 3.9.7 covers the pattern.

The meniscotibial attachment is the structure disrupted in a ramp lesion's medial equivalent, and its loss allows meniscal extrusion. 6.35 covers ramp lesions.

What Changes It

Which portion is injured.

Meniscal integrity and position.

Flexion angle and rotation.

Concurrent superficial ligament injury, which is common.

How It Is Measured

Sectioning studies apportion its rotational and coronal contributions.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrates the layer and its meniscal attachment. 5.9 covers imaging.

Arthroscopic assessment of the medial compartment identifies meniscotibial disruption.

No clinical test isolates this layer from the superficial one.

Clinical Relevance

Expect isolated injury here to present as rotatory rather than coronal laxity.

Look for meniscal extrusion where the meniscotibial portion is disrupted.

Assess the layer and the meniscus together rather than separately.

Accept that no clinical test isolates this layer.

Key Learning Points

  • Its identity comes from its length and its meniscal attachment.
  • Injury has two consequences: rotational laxity and altered meniscal behaviour.
  • The meniscotibial portion is more often implicated in extrusion.
  • Its valgus contribution is modest and its rotational contribution larger.
  • No clinical test isolates this layer from the superficial one.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat the meniscotibial attachment as the structure that links medial ligament injury to meniscal extrusion, and treat extrusion as the imaging sign that it has failed. 3.6.10 covers extrusion.

That link matters because a medial injury treated as a ligament problem can leave a meniscal one, and the meniscal consequence is the one with a degenerative endpoint. 3.5.5 covers meniscal loss.

Conventional teaching includes the deep layer in anatomic medial reconstruction, which is a rotational and a meniscal argument rather than a coronal one. 3.9.8 covers the construct.

What Changes It

Whether the meniscotibial attachment is repairable.

Meniscal position and whether extrusion is present.

Concurrent cruciate and corner injury.

Alignment, which loads the repaired structures. 3.13 covers alignment.

How It Is Measured

Coronal magnetic resonance imaging measures extrusion, which is the functional proxy.

Arthroscopy assesses the meniscotibial attachment directly.

Sectioning studies give the rotational apportionment the reconstruction targets.

No measurement confirms the deep layer's function has been restored.

Clinical Relevance

Measure meniscal extrusion in a medial ligament injury.

Repair the meniscotibial attachment where it is disrupted and repairable.

Include the deep layer in anatomic reconstruction, on rotational and meniscal grounds.

Treat the meniscal consequence as the one with the degenerative endpoint.

Key Learning Points

  • The meniscotibial attachment links medial ligament injury to meniscal extrusion.
  • Extrusion is the imaging sign that it has failed.
  • A medial injury treated as a ligament problem can leave a meniscal one.
  • The meniscal consequence has the degenerative endpoint.
  • No measurement confirms the deep layer's function has been restored.
