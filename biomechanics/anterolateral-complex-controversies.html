Contemporary Controversies

Page 3.11.9 · Anterolateral Complex Biomechanics · biomechanics page

What is actually disputed about the anterolateral complex, separated from what is not. Three arguments, each with a different kind of answer.

Contemporary Controversies

Choose a depth

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Three arguments are usually run together and have different kinds of answer. What the structure is, is an anatomical question. What the region restrains, is a mechanical question. Who should have an augmentation, is a clinical question.

The anatomical argument is the loudest and the least consequential. Whether a discrete anterolateral ligament exists in every knee does not change what a femorally anchored construct restrains. 3.11.3 covers the structure.

The mechanical argument is largely settled. Sectioning the anterolateral region increases internal rotation and the simulated pivot shift, consistently across laboratories. 3.11.5 covers internal rotation control.

The clinical argument is the live one: which patients benefit enough to justify the addition. 3.11.8 covers the combined reconstruction.

What Changes It

New dissection and imaging work, which refines the anatomical account without settling the clinical one.

Trial and registry data, which is what moves the clinical question.

Slope correction as a competing intervention for the same problem. 3.13 covers alignment.

Graft and construct developments, which change the cost side of the trade.

How It Is Measured

Dissection prevalence studies answer the anatomical question and disagree with each other.

Robotic sectioning studies answer the mechanical question and agree with each other.

Randomised trials and registries answer the clinical question and are still accumulating.

The three literatures are often cited as if they bore on the same question, which is the commonest error in this area.

Clinical Relevance

Separate the anatomical, mechanical and clinical arguments when discussing this.

Rely on the mechanical consensus rather than on the anatomical dispute.

Apply augmentation in the groups the trial evidence covers.

Avoid citing a dissection study to settle a clinical question.

Key Learning Points

  • Three arguments with three kinds of answer: anatomical, mechanical, clinical.
  • The anatomical argument is the loudest and the least consequential.
  • The mechanical argument is largely settled and consistent across laboratories.
  • The clinical argument is the live one.
  • Citing the three literatures as if they bore on one question is the commonest error.

Fellowship Level

At the Knee

The fellowship level dispute is between augmentation and bony correction for the same problem. A knee with high rotatory laxity and an increased slope can be addressed by adding a lateral construct or by correcting the slope. 3.13 covers alignment.

They are not equivalent. A tenodesis adds restraint and a slope correction reduces demand, which is the difference between a stronger rope and a lighter load.

Conventional teaching has not established which to prefer, or whether both are needed in the knee that has failed once. 3.11.8 covers the combined reconstruction.

What Changes It

Slope magnitude, which decides whether correction is indicated at all.

Previous failure, which raises the threshold for doing less.

Patient demand and willingness to accept an osteotomy.

Graft and tunnel constraints.

How It Is Measured

Lateral radiography and magnetic resonance imaging measure slope.

Robotic testing compares the mechanical effect of augmentation and of slope correction.

Revision cohorts report outcomes after each, in small numbers and without direct comparison.

No trial compares augmentation against slope correction for the same indication.

Clinical Relevance

Measure slope before choosing between augmentation and correction.

Describe the two as adding restraint and reducing demand respectively.

Consider both in the knee that has failed a conventional revision.

State that no trial compares them directly.

Key Learning Points

  • Augmentation adds restraint; slope correction reduces demand.
  • That is the difference between a stronger rope and a lighter load.
  • They are not equivalent and no trial compares them directly.
  • Previous failure raises the threshold for doing less.
  • Both may be needed in the knee that failed once.

Consultant Perspective

At the Knee

The consultant level question is what to do while the evidence accumulates, which is the position most of this debate has occupied for a decade.

Two defensible positions exist. One adds augmentation to the high risk groups the trials identify and waits for the subgroup data. The other treats rotational laxity and bony morphology as the indication and augments by measurement rather than by group.

Both are consistent with the mechanics. They differ in how much weight they place on trial subgroups against individual assessment, which is a question about evidence rather than about the knee. 3.11.8 covers the construct.

What is not defensible is citing the anatomical dispute as a reason not to augment, because the mechanical case does not depend on it. 3.11.3 covers the structure.

What Changes It

Accumulating trial and registry data, particularly subgroup analyses.

Developing slope correction practice, which competes for the same indication.

Local case mix, which decides how often the high risk groups present.

Surgeon experience with each construct, which affects the cost side.

How It Is Measured

Registry reinjury rates by construct and by age group are the most informative accumulating data.

Randomised trial subgroup analyses are limited by sample size and should be read as such.

Instrumented rotatory measurement remains without a clinical threshold, which limits individualised assessment.

No measurement resolves the choice between the two defensible positions.

Clinical Relevance

Hold a defensible position and state which it is, rather than treating the question as settled.

Follow registry and subgroup data as it accumulates.

Do not cite the anatomical dispute as a reason against augmentation.

Expect slope correction to continue competing for the same indication.

Key Learning Points

  • The question is what to do while the evidence accumulates.
  • Two defensible positions: augment by group, or augment by measurement.
  • Both are consistent with the mechanics and differ on how to weigh evidence.
  • Citing the anatomical dispute against augmentation is not defensible.
  • No measurement resolves the choice between the two positions.
