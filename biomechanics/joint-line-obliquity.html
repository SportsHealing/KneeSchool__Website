Joint Line Obliquity

Page 3.13.6 · Alignment and Biomechanics · biomechanics page

Where the joint surface points, as distinct from where the axis runs. A correct overall axis with an oblique joint line still shears.

Joint Line Obliquity

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

Two things describe how a joint is loaded: the direction the load comes from, and the direction the surface faces.

If a surface is tilted relative to the load, part of the load pushes along the surface rather than into it.

A push along a surface is a shear, and a joint resists shear less well than it resists compression.

At the Knee

The joint line is the plane of the knee surface, and in a normal leg it sits close to horizontal when standing. 2.2.2 covers the plateau.

The overall axis can be corrected to neutral while leaving the joint line tilted, if the correction was made in the wrong place. 3.13.1 covers the axis.

A tilted joint line under a vertical load means part of that load pushes sideways across the joint.

That sideways push loads the ligaments and the edges of the joint rather than the middle of the surfaces.

What Changes It

Where a deformity sits, whether in the femur, the tibia or inside the joint. 3.13.2 covers the frames.

Where a correction is made, since correcting far from the deformity tilts the line. 3.13.9 covers osteotomy.

Wear on one side, which tilts the line as the compartment collapses.

A fracture through the joint surface that healed out of line.

Clinical Relevance

It is the reason a correction is planned at the site of the deformity rather than wherever is convenient. 3.13.10 covers the principles.

An axis that looks right on a film can still be loading the knee badly.

It matters more at the extremes of correction than in small ones.

5.16.5 covers how corrections are planned on imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • Load direction and surface direction are two separate things.
  • A surface tilted relative to the load converts some compression into shear.
  • A joint resists shear less well than compression.
  • A neutral overall axis can still have a tilted joint line.
  • Corrections are planned at the site of the deformity to avoid tilting the line.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Overall alignment and joint line orientation are independent descriptions, and a plan needs both. One says how much, the other says where.

Joint orientation angles localise a deformity to the distal femur, the proximal tibia or the joint itself.

Correcting the right amount in the wrong place reproduces the overall angle and distorts the local geometry.

At the Knee

The mechanical lateral distal femoral angle and the medial proximal tibial angle describe the two joint orientations, each with a narrow normal range near 87 to 90 degrees.

A varus knee with a normal tibial angle and an abnormal femoral angle is a femoral deformity, and correcting it at the tibia would tilt the joint line.

Joint line obliquity under load generates a shear component across the joint, which the collateral ligaments and the menisci resist. 3.6 covers the meniscus.

Beyond a few degrees of obliquity the shear is thought to accelerate wear and to load the soft tissue in a way the joint did not develop for.

That is the mechanical limit on how much correction can be taken at a single site. 3.13.10 covers the targets.

What Changes It

The anatomical site of the deformity, which the joint orientation angles identify.

The site chosen for correction, which is the surgeon's decision.

Intra-articular bone loss, which tilts the line without any extra-articular deformity.

Soft tissue laxity, which lets the line tilt further under load than the film shows.

Previous surgery, which may have corrected the overall angle at a convenient rather than the correct site.

How It Is Measured

Mechanical lateral distal femoral angle and medial proximal tibial angle on a standing long leg film. 5.3.2 covers the technique.

Joint line convergence angle, which captures the intra-articular contribution from cartilage loss and laxity.

Weight bearing views, which show the obliquity under load rather than at rest.

Planning software, which computes the predicted obliquity for a proposed correction. 5.16.5 covers it.

No measurement captures obliquity during walking, when the load direction itself is moving. 3.13.7 covers that.

Clinical Relevance

Measure both joint orientation angles before planning, since they say where the deformity sits.

Correct at the site of the deformity rather than at the convenient site, because obliquity is the cost of doing otherwise.

Account for the joint line convergence angle, which is the part no osteotomy will change.

Expect obliquity to matter more as the size of the correction rises. 3.13.10 covers the limits.

Key Learning Points

  • Overall alignment says how much; joint orientation angles say where.
  • Normal joint orientation angles sit near 87 to 90 degrees with a narrow range.
  • Correcting the right amount in the wrong place tilts the joint line.
  • Obliquity under load generates shear that soft tissue and menisci must resist.
  • The joint line convergence angle is the part no osteotomy changes.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Joint line obliquity is the constraint that turns a one variable correction into a two variable one, and it is why large single site corrections are avoided.

A correction taken entirely at the tibia for a femoral deformity produces a neutral mechanical axis with an oblique joint line, and the knee is then loaded in shear during stance.

The accepted working limit is a few degrees of obliquity, and the figure is a convention drawn from deformity correction practice rather than a measured threshold.

In arthroplasty the same argument appears as the debate about how much joint line obliquity a restricted kinematic target may accept. 7.83 covers it.

Conventional teaching plans a double level correction where a single level one would leave unacceptable obliquity, accepting a second osteotomy to preserve the joint line. 7.72 covers combined procedures.

What Changes It

The site and the magnitude of the deformity, which together decide whether one level will do.

The intra-articular component, which sets a floor on achievable obliquity. 3.13.8 covers it.

The correction target chosen, since a larger target produces more obliquity at a single site. 3.13.10 covers the targets.

Soft tissue laxity, which adds obliquity under load that planning on a static film misses.

Whether a second level correction is acceptable to the patient, which is a clinical rather than a geometric variable.

How It Is Measured

Joint orientation angles and the joint line convergence angle on a standardised standing film, which is the planning basis.

Digital planning with predicted postoperative obliquity, which is the step that makes the double level decision explicit. 5.16.5 covers it.

Weight bearing computed tomography, which measures obliquity under load without projection error and is not widely available.

Gait analysis, which would give the obliquity relative to the moving load vector and does not report it. 3.16.7 covers motion capture.

The accepted limit has no outcome study behind it, which should be said when it is quoted as a threshold.

Clinical Relevance

Plan the correction level from the joint orientation angles rather than from the overall angle alone.

Consider a double level correction where a single level would leave obliquity beyond the accepted few degrees. 7.72 covers the procedures.

State that the accepted obliquity limit is a convention rather than a measured threshold.

Expect the intra-articular component to limit what any osteotomy achieves, and quantify it beforehand.

Weigh the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations before proposing a second osteotomy to preserve a joint line.

Key Learning Points

  • Obliquity turns a one variable correction into a two variable one.
  • A tibial correction for a femoral deformity gives a neutral axis loaded in shear.
  • The accepted limit of a few degrees is a convention, not a measured threshold.
  • Double level correction buys joint line preservation at the cost of a second osteotomy.
  • The intra-articular component sets a floor on achievable obliquity.
