Anterior Stability

Page 3.7.8 · ACL Biomechanics · biomechanics page

The function the cruciate performs best and instruments measure best. It is also the one that correlates least well with how the knee feels.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Anterior Stability

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

Stability in one direction means the joint does not travel further than it should when pushed that way.

Measuring it means applying a known push and recording how far the joint moves. The answer is a distance.

The useful comparison is with the other side rather than with a published figure, because people differ.

At the Knee

Anterior stability is resistance to the tibia translating forward relative to the femur. 3.2.7 covers the motion.

Conventional teaching gives normal anteroposterior translation as roughly 5 to 10 millimetres, with side to side difference being the quantity that matters.

The anterior cruciate is the primary restraint, so losing it produces the largest increase of any single injury. 3.7.2 covers it.

The secondary restraints limit how large that increase is, which is why the figure varies between patients with the same injury. 3.7.3 covers them.

What Changes It

Flexion angle, since the structures tighten differently through the range.

Muscle activity and guarding, which reduce the measurement.

Secondary restraint integrity, which sets the size of the increase.

Generalised laxity, which raises the figure on both sides.

Clinical Relevance

This is the function a reconstruction restores most reliably, which is why a reconstructed knee usually measures well. 3.7.20 covers the construct.

A knee that measures well and still gives way has lost something the measurement does not capture, usually rotation. 3.7.7 covers it.

Comparing sides is the method, because individual variation exceeds the injury effect in some people.

Guarding reduces the figure, which is why examination under anaesthetic finds more. Section 4 covers the tests.

Key Learning Points

  • Anterior stability is resistance to the tibia translating forward.
  • Conventional teaching gives normal translation as roughly 5 to 10 millimetres.
  • Side to side difference matters more than the absolute figure.
  • Losing the cruciate produces the largest increase of any single injury.
  • A knee that measures well and gives way has lost rotation, not translation.

MRCS Level

The Principle

An instrumented measurement needs a stated load, a stated flexion angle and a stated limit of agreement. Without the third, a small side to side difference cannot be interpreted.

Translation is the degree of freedom with the only widely used clinical instrument, which is why it dominates the literature out of proportion to its clinical importance. 3.2.1 covers the degrees of freedom.

That dominance is a measurement artefact rather than a clinical judgement, and it is worth naming as one.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching treats a side to side difference of more than 3 millimetres on instrumented testing as abnormal, which is a convention applied to a continuous measurement.

The measurement is made at a flexion angle where the anteromedial bundle is the restraint, so it samples that bundle preferentially. 3.7.5 covers it.

Compliance index and the shape of the load displacement curve carry information beyond the endpoint, including whether there is a firm endpoint at all.

Examination under anaesthetic removes the muscular contribution and gives the larger and more structural figure. 3.2.10 covers the muscular share.

What Changes It

Applied load and flexion angle, both of which must be stated.

Guarding, which the instrument cannot distinguish from a restraint.

Secondary restraint and meniscal status, which change the size of the increase.

Graft maturation after reconstruction, which changes the figure over months.

How It Is Measured

Instrumented arthrometry applies a measured anterior load and records displacement, with a reported limit of agreement that matters at small differences.

Stress radiography measures it with bony landmarks and a known load, and is underused.

Examination under anaesthetic gives the structural figure without muscular interference.

Robotic testing in cadavers gives the apportionment the clinical figure is interpreted against.

Clinical Relevance

State load, angle and the instrument's limit of agreement with any figure.

Treat the 3 millimetre threshold as a convention rather than a boundary.

Read the curve shape and the endpoint rather than the number alone.

Recognise that translation dominates the literature because it is measurable, not because it matters most.

Key Learning Points

  • A measurement needs a load, an angle and a limit of agreement.
  • Translation dominates the literature because it is the measurable degree of freedom.
  • Conventional teaching treats more than 3 millimetres side to side as abnormal.
  • The measurement samples the anteromedial bundle preferentially.
  • Curve shape and endpoint carry information beyond the number.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat restored anterior translation as a necessary and insufficient result. It is the threshold a reconstruction has to clear and not the outcome that satisfies a patient.

The gap between a normal arthrometer reading and a knee that feels unstable is the clinical form of the apportionment argument: the graft restored the share it was best placed to restore. 3.7.7 covers rotation.

Conventional teaching holds that instrumented laxity correlates weakly with patient reported outcome after reconstruction, which is an awkward finding and a consistent one.

What Changes It

Tunnel position and graft tension, which set the restraint through range.

Graft maturation and remodelling, which change the figure over the first year.

Secondary restraint and meniscal repair, which contribute to the result.

Slope, which loads the construct and can produce progressive laxity. 3.13 covers alignment.

How It Is Measured

Serial arthrometry measures the construct's behaviour over time, which is more informative than a single reading.

Stress radiography gives a bony measurement that is reproducible between observers.

Robotic cadaveric testing compares constructs for restored translation under identical loads.

Patient reported outcome measures the result the laxity figure correlates weakly with.

Clinical Relevance

Treat restored translation as the threshold rather than the goal.

Measure serially where progressive laxity is suspected, rather than once.

Investigate slope when laxity increases without a new injury.

Counsel using the weak correlation between laxity and outcome rather than against it.

Key Learning Points

  • Restored anterior translation is necessary and insufficient.
  • A normal reading with an unstable knee is the apportionment argument in clinical form.
  • Instrumented laxity correlates weakly with patient reported outcome.
  • Serial measurement is more informative than a single reading.
  • Progressive laxity without a new injury prompts a slope measurement.
