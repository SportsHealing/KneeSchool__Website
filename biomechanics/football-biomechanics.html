Football

Page 3.15.1 · Sports-Specific Knee Biomechanics · biomechanics page

A sport built on changing direction at speed with one foot fixed in the turf, which is the position the knee tolerates least.

Football

Choose a depth

For Patients

The Principle

A joint is safest when the force going through it points the way the joint bends.

Football repeatedly asks the knee to take force from the side while the foot is stuck in the ground.

That is the one direction the knee has very little of its own strength in.

At the Knee

The studs are designed to grip, which is useful for turning and means the foot does not slip when the body twists.

When the body turns and the foot does not, the difference is taken up by the knee.

Most serious football knee injuries happen without anybody touching the player, during a turn, a landing or a sudden stop.

Contact injuries also happen, usually from a blow to the outside of the knee, which opens the inner side.

Clinical Relevance

A knee that swells within an hour of a twisting injury needs assessing, because that pattern suggests bleeding inside the joint.

Giving way when turning is different from giving way from pain, and it is worth describing which happened.

Training that includes landing and turning technique reduces injuries, which is unusual and worth doing. Section 11 covers it.

This page describes mechanism rather than injury rates. No figures are given because no source was available to check them against.

Key Learning Points

  • A joint is safest when force points the way the joint bends.
  • Football loads the knee sideways with the foot fixed in the turf.
  • Most serious football knee injuries happen without contact.
  • Contact injuries usually come from a blow to the outside of the knee.
  • Swelling within an hour of a twisting injury needs assessing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Are moulded studs safer than long studs? Studs that grip harder resist the foot turning, so the turn is taken by the knee instead. Softer ground usually needs more grip, so the choice is a trade rather than a simple safety question.

Q. Why do knees give way when nobody touched the player? Because the dangerous load is the player's own. Stopping and turning produce enough force through a nearly straight knee to tear a ligament without any outside help.

Q. Does a knee brace prevent injury in football? Braces can help a knee that has already been injured feel more secure. Evidence that they prevent a first injury in football is limited, and they do not replace training.

Q. When can I go back after a knee injury? That depends on the injury and on what your knee can do, not on the calendar. A clinician should test strength and control, including turning and landing, before you return.

See a clinician if your knee swells within an hour of an injury, gives way, locks, or will not take your weight.

For Medical Students

The Principle

A sport's injury pattern follows from what it asks the body to do repeatedly, and from what equipment it uses.

Football combines high speed deceleration, direction change and a boot designed to stop the foot rotating.

Each of those alone is manageable. Their combination at low knee flexion is the problem. 3.14.6 covers cutting.

At the Knee

The classical non contact mechanism is a planted foot, a nearly extended knee, valgus collapse and tibial internal rotation during a cut or landing. 3.7.14 covers it.

Stud design increases rotational traction, which raises the torque transmitted to the knee when the body turns.

The commonest contact mechanism is a valgus force applied to the lateral side, which loads the medial structures. 3.9.1 covers valgus stability.

Repeated deceleration also loads the extensor mechanism, which is why anterior knee pain and patellar tendon problems appear in footballers. 6.64 covers tendinopathy.

Meniscal injury commonly accompanies ligament injury rather than occurring alone. 6.4 covers associated injuries.

What Changes It

Playing surface and stud configuration, which set rotational traction.

Fatigue, which degrades landing and cutting control within a match. 3.14.8 covers landing.

Previous injury, which is the strongest individual risk factor.

Position and playing level, which change how often the risky movements occur.

Neuromuscular training exposure. Section 11 covers it.

Clinical Relevance

Ask whether the injury involved contact, because the mechanism predicts the structures involved. 6.1 covers the cruciate.

Ask about immediate swelling, which suggests haemarthrosis and a significant intra articular injury.

Expect associated meniscal and chondral injury with a cruciate rupture rather than an isolated one. 6.4 covers them.

Describe mechanism rather than quoting injury rates, since no source for rates was available here.

Key Learning Points

  • A sport's injury pattern follows from what it repeatedly asks and what equipment it uses.
  • The non contact mechanism is a planted foot, extended knee, valgus and internal rotation.
  • Stud traction raises the torque transmitted to the knee when the body turns.
  • The contact mechanism is a valgus force applied laterally.
  • Meniscal injury usually accompanies ligament injury rather than occurring alone.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Footballing knee injury is best understood as a conjunction: a movement, a surface interaction and a neuromuscular state that coincide.

Removing any one of the three prevents the injury, which is why prevention has several viable targets and screening has few.

That also explains why the same player performs the same movement thousands of times safely and once unsafely.

At the Knee

Non contact cruciate injury occurs within the first 50 milliseconds of foot contact, before voluntary correction is possible. 3.14.6 covers the window.

Rotational traction at the shoe surface interface determines how much of the body's angular momentum reaches the knee.

Valgus contact injury classically produces medial collateral injury, and the cruciate is involved when the force is larger or rotation is added. 3.9.10 covers the combination.

Posterior cruciate injury in football is usually a direct anterior blow to the flexed tibia, which is less common than the valgus mechanisms. 3.8 covers the ligament.

Anterolateral structures resist the rotational component and are implicated in residual rotatory instability after reconstruction. 3.11.4 covers the pivot shift.

What Changes It

Surface and footwear, which set the traction and are modifiable at a club level.

Neuromuscular training, which changes preparatory activation rather than reaction speed.

Match congestion and fatigue, which concentrate injuries late in matches and seasons.

Previous cruciate injury or reconstruction, which raises the risk on both limbs. 3.7.20 covers reconstruction.

Bony morphology including tibial slope, which is fixed and sets a baseline. 3.13.9 covers slope.

How It Is Measured

Video analysis of actual injuries, which is the only record of the real event and is uncontrolled. 3.7.14 covers the mechanism.

Laboratory cutting and landing protocols, which measure a task rather than a match. 3.14.6 covers them.

Mechanical testing of shoe surface traction, which is reproducible and does not include a player.

Wearable load monitoring in training and matches, which measures exposure. 3.16.8 covers it.

Injury surveillance gives rates, and this page does not quote any, because no source was retrieved to support them.

Clinical Relevance

Take a mechanism history in detail, since contact and non contact mechanisms predict different structures.

Expect late match and late season presentations, which reflect fatigue rather than chance.

Recommend neuromuscular training as prevention with the understanding that adherence decides the effect. Section 11 covers it.

Consider traction at the shoe surface interface as a modifiable club level factor.

State that no injury rates are quoted here and why.

Key Learning Points

  • Injury is a conjunction of movement, surface interaction and neuromuscular state.
  • Removing any one prevents it, which is why prevention has targets and screening does not.
  • Non contact injury occurs before voluntary correction is possible.
  • Rotational traction decides how much angular momentum reaches the knee.
  • Late match and late season presentations reflect fatigue rather than chance.
