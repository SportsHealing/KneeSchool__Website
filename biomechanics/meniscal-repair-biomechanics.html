Meniscal Repair Biomechanics

Page 3.6.13 · Meniscal Biomechanics · biomechanics page

What a repair construct has to do. Hold the tissue together under thousands of loading cycles, while the hoop tension tries to pull it apart.

Meniscal Repair Biomechanics

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

A repair has to hold until the tissue heals. The holding is mechanical and the healing is biological, and both have to succeed.

What the repair holds against is the load the structure normally carries. A meniscal repair holds against hoop tension. 3.6.2 covers it.

A single strong pull is not the test. The test is thousands of smaller pulls, because that is what walking does.

At the Knee

A suture passed across a tear holds the two sides together. Where it is placed and which way it runs decide what it grips.

A suture running vertically catches the circumferential fibre bundles, which is what gives it something strong to hold. 3.6.3 covers the fibres.

A suture running horizontally lies between the bundles and relies on weaker tissue, which is why it holds less well in testing.

The repair fails in one of two ways. Either the suture pulls through the tissue, or the device or knot gives way.

What Changes It

Suture orientation, with vertical catching the bundles.

The number of sutures and their spacing along the tear.

Tissue quality, since a suture needs something to hold onto.

The load applied afterwards, which is what the construct has to survive.

Clinical Relevance

Repair is preferred to resection because it preserves the load path, and the construct is what makes that possible. 3.6.1 covers the functions.

Protecting load after a repair gives the construct less to do while healing happens. Section 6 covers management.

A repair that heals in the wrong position has held the tissue without restoring the fibre path, which is a mechanical failure inside a biological success.

Blood supply decides healing, and the periphery is where the blood is. 2.5.3 covers it.

Key Learning Points

  • A repair holds mechanically while the tissue heals biologically.
  • A meniscal repair holds against hoop tension.
  • The test is thousands of small loading cycles, not one strong pull.
  • A vertical suture catches the circumferential bundles; a horizontal one lies between them.
  • Failure is either suture pull through or device failure.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Three construct properties matter and they are measured separately. Ultimate failure load is the single pull; stiffness is the resistance to displacement; cyclic gap formation is the displacement that accumulates under repeated load.

Cyclic gap formation is the one that predicts clinical failure, because a gap means the tissue edges have separated and healing across them cannot occur.

The failure mode identifies what to change. Suture pull through means the tissue was the limit; device or knot failure means the construct was.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching ranks vertical mattress configurations above horizontal in cadaveric testing, on the ground that vertical sutures capture the circumferential bundles.

Inside out, outside in and all inside techniques are compared on these properties, and conventional teaching has reported the differences narrowing as all inside devices have developed.

Radial and root repairs make different demands, because the construct carries hoop tension rather than approximating a longitudinal split. 3.6.12 covers radial tears.

Biological augmentation, including rasping, trephination and fibrin clot, addresses healing rather than the construct's mechanics.

What Changes It

Suture configuration and orientation relative to the fibre bundles.

Number and spacing of fixation points.

Tissue quality and vascularity, which decide pull through and healing. 2.5.3 covers blood supply.

Postoperative loading, which determines the cyclic demand.

How It Is Measured

Cadaveric or porcine meniscal repair constructs are tested in tension to failure and under cyclic loading, which gives all three properties.

Gap formation is measured optically during cycling and is the most clinically relevant output.

Animal models assess healing with the construct in place, which bench testing cannot.

Bench strength and clinical healing correlate imperfectly, which is a standing caution when reading a construct comparison.

Clinical Relevance

Judge a construct study by cyclic gap formation rather than ultimate failure load.

Read the failure mode to know whether the tissue or the construct was the limit.

Orientate sutures vertically to capture the fibre bundles.

Treat biological augmentation as addressing healing rather than fixation.

Key Learning Points

  • Three properties: ultimate failure load, stiffness and cyclic gap formation.
  • Cyclic gap formation predicts clinical failure, because a gap prevents healing.
  • The failure mode identifies whether the tissue or the construct was the limit.
  • Conventional teaching ranks vertical mattress above horizontal configurations.
  • Bench strength and clinical healing correlate imperfectly.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat the construct as one of three requirements, alongside tissue capable of healing and a mechanical environment that lets it. A perfect construct in a degenerate avascular tear in a malaligned limb fails.

That framing explains the spread of reported healing rates better than construct choice does, and it is why patient and tear selection carries more weight than configuration.

The construct still matters where the demand is highest. Radial and root repairs are where configuration choice has a measurable mechanical consequence. 3.6.11 covers root tears.

What Changes It

Tear pattern and location, which set the demand and the healing capacity.

Alignment, which sets the tension the construct carries. 3.13 covers it.

Concurrent cruciate reconstruction, which changes the mechanical environment favourably and the rehabilitation.

Patient age and tissue quality, which decide whether the tissue can hold a suture and heal.

How It Is Measured

Cyclic construct testing compares configurations and devices under repeatable load.

Clinical healing rates by pattern, location and concurrent procedure are the outcome data that matters.

Second look arthroscopy and imaging assess healing, imperfectly.

Failure analysis in revision identifies which of the three requirements was not met, after the event.

Clinical Relevance

Select the tear and the patient before selecting the construct.

Correct alignment where it would load the repair excessively.

Choose configuration deliberately in radial and root repairs, where it measurably matters.

Read reported healing rates with pattern, location and concurrent procedure attached.

Key Learning Points

  • A construct is one of three requirements, with healing capacity and mechanical environment.
  • Patient and tear selection carries more weight than configuration.
  • Configuration matters most in radial and root repairs.
  • Concurrent cruciate reconstruction improves the mechanical environment.
  • Revision failure analysis identifies which requirement was not met.

Fellowship Level

At the Knee

The fellowship level question is what to do when the three requirements cannot all be met, which is most of the difficult practice. A repairable pattern in a malaligned limb, a root tear with early cartilage change, a radial tear in a forty five year old.

Each is a judgement about whether preservation is still the aim. The mechanics say what would be restored; they do not say whether restoring it is worth the operation.

Combining procedures is the usual answer, and the combination has its own mechanics. A root repair with a correcting osteotomy restores the anchor and reduces the load it carries, which is why the pairing is more than additive.

What Changes It

Which of the three requirements is failing, and whether it can be changed.

Cartilage state, which sets the ceiling on what preservation achieves.

Patient demand and expectation, which decide whether a long rehabilitation is proportionate.

The availability of a second procedure to fix the mechanical environment.

How It Is Measured

Combined procedure cohorts report the outcome of the pairing and are smaller and more heterogeneous than single procedure series.

Cadaveric work demonstrates the mechanical effect of each component and rarely of the combination.

Cartilage status at surgery is recorded inconsistently, which limits comparison between series.

No measurement predicts which individual knee will preserve, which is the honest basis for shared decision making.

Clinical Relevance

Identify which requirement is failing before deciding whether to repair at all.

Pair a repair with a correcting procedure where the mechanical environment is the failing requirement.

Set the aim by cartilage state rather than by tear repairability alone.

Share the uncertainty, since no measurement predicts the individual result.

Key Learning Points

  • The difficult practice is where the three requirements cannot all be met.
  • Mechanics say what would be restored, not whether restoring it is worth the operation.
  • A root repair with a correcting osteotomy is more than additive.
  • Cartilage state sets the ceiling on what preservation achieves.
  • No measurement predicts which individual knee will preserve.
