PLC Landmark Studies

Page 3.17.5 · Landmark Biomechanics Papers · landmark_papers page

The corner that was missed for decades, the sectioning studies that made it visible, and the test that came out of them.

PLC Landmark Studies

Choose a depth

FRCS Level

What the Question Was

The posterolateral corner is the clearest case in the knee of a region that was poorly managed because it was poorly described.

What structures sit there and in what layers. Which of them restrains varus and which restrains external rotation. How does a corner injury behave when the posterior cruciate is also torn. And can the corner be reconstructed in a way that holds.

The second question produced the chapter's most useful finding, because it generated a clinical test rather than only a description. 3.10.6 covers corner function.

What Was Done

Seebacher and colleagues described the lateral side as layers in the 1980s, in the same spirit as the medial layer work and a decade later.

Gollehon, Torzilli and Warren sectioned the posterolateral structures and the posterior cruciate in sequence and in combination, measuring varus and external rotation across flexion.

Grood and colleagues ran parallel sectioning work that separated the contributions of the lateral collateral, the popliteus complex and the posterior cruciate.

Terry and LaPrade examined the lateral structures including the anterolateral tissue and related the anatomy to injury patterns seen at surgery.

LaPrade and colleagues quantified the posterolateral anatomy against bony landmarks in the 2000s and built reconstruction techniques from those measurements.

Claes and colleagues reported the anterolateral ligament as a discrete structure in the 2010s, which reopened an argument about the lateral side that had seemed closed.

What It Showed

The lateral side is layered, and the popliteus complex is a functional unit rather than a muscle with a tendon passing through.

Isolated posterolateral sectioning increases external rotation most at low flexion angles. Adding the posterior cruciate increases it at high flexion as well.

That angle dependence is the dial test. Comparing thirty degrees with ninety separates an isolated corner injury from a combined one, and the test exists because the sectioning work showed the pattern. 3.10.7 covers it.

The lateral collateral is the primary varus restraint and the popliteofibular ligament contributes substantially to rotational control. 3.10.5 covers it.

Whether the anterolateral ligament is a distinct structure or a thickening of the capsule remains argued, and the anatomical disagreement is genuine rather than semantic.

What It Changed

The corner stopped being missed. A cruciate reconstruction failing for no apparent reason became a recognised presentation of an unaddressed corner injury.

The dial test entered examination as a two position test with a mechanical rationale.

Anatomic reconstruction replaced non-anatomic lateral procedures, driven by the quantified landmark work.

The anterolateral argument changed practice before it was settled, which is unusual and worth noticing. Lateral extra-articular procedures became common again on a contested anatomical basis.

Key Learning Points

  • The posterolateral corner was mismanaged because it was undescribed, and description came from layer studies and sectioning.
  • External rotation increases most at low flexion with an isolated corner injury, and across the range when the posterior cruciate goes too.
  • That pattern is the reason the dial test is done at two angles.
  • The lateral collateral restrains varus and the popliteofibular ligament contributes to rotational control.
  • Whether the anterolateral ligament is a discrete structure is still argued.

Fellowship Level

What Was Done

Combined sectioning is harder to interpret here than anywhere else in the knee, because three structures share rotational control and cutting order changes the attribution.

External rotation measurement is sensitive to the applied torque and to how the tibia is held, and studies differ on both, so absolute rotation figures are not comparable.

The anterolateral ligament dispute is partly a dissection method dispute. Whether a structure appears depends on how the capsule is approached and on how a structure is defined.

Reconstruction studies are short term and laboratory based. A construct that restores rotation on a rig has not been shown to hold in a patient for years.

What It Showed

Where the literature is strong: the angle dependence of rotational laxity, reproduced across laboratories, and the layered anatomy.

Where it is weak: the natural history of an untreated corner injury, which rests on small series of patients who presented late.

Where it is contested: the anterolateral ligament's existence as a structure and the indication for a lateral extra-articular procedure. These are separate arguments that are frequently merged.

The practice change ran ahead of the anatomy here, which is a pattern worth recognising in itself. 3.16.10 covers the general problem of evidence arriving after adoption.

What to Read Now

Read a posterolateral sectioning study for its cutting order and its applied torque before reading its numbers.

Keep the anterolateral anatomy argument and the lateral augmentation indication argument separate when reading either.

Read corner reconstruction results with the follow up duration in view, since the mechanical case is strong and the durability case is thin.

Treat external rotation figures as laboratory specific and the angle dependence as the transferable finding.

Key Learning Points

  • Three structures share rotational control, so cutting order governs what a study can attribute.
  • Applied torque and tibial fixation differ between studies, which makes absolute rotation figures incomparable.
  • The anterolateral dispute is partly about dissection method and partly about definition.
  • The mechanical case for corner reconstruction is strong and the durability evidence is thin.

Consultant Perspective

What It Changed

The decision this literature informs is whether to add a lateral procedure, and it is currently the least settled balance in the chapter.

Arguing for adding one: the sectioning work shows the lateral structures contribute rotational control a cruciate graft does not supply, and residual rotational laxity is associated with graft failure.

Arguing against: the anatomy is disputed, the procedure adds lateral constraint whose long term effect on the lateral compartment is not known, and the trials supporting it recruit selected high risk patients rather than everybody.

Weigh the patient's age and physiology, because adding constraint to a young knee commits it for decades. Weigh comorbidities that affect wound healing and stiffness on the lateral side. Weigh the demands on the knee, since rotational demand is the entire reason to consider the addition. Weigh their psychological state, because a patient whose previous graft failed may want the most that can be done rather than the most that is supported. And draw out their ideas, concerns and expectations, since the honest answer here involves telling a patient that the anatomy is argued.

Weigh those and record the balance, because this is a decision that will look different in five years and the record is what makes that reviewable.

What to Read Now

What a trainee should take from this chapter is that practice can move faster than anatomy, and that the lateral side is where it happened most recently.

Read the sectioning work first and the augmentation trials second, because the first is durable and the second is current.

When teaching this, say that a procedure in widespread use can rest on a contested structure, and that noticing this is part of appraisal rather than scepticism.

Key Learning Points

  • Whether to add a lateral procedure is the least settled balance in this chapter.
  • Rotational contribution argues for it and disputed anatomy with unknown long term constraint effects argues against.
  • The five patient factors, and rotational demand in particular, do most of the deciding.
  • Record the balance, because the evidence is moving and the record is what makes a decision reviewable.
  • Practice moved ahead of anatomy here, which is a pattern to recognise rather than condemn.
