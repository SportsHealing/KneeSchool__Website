Double Bundle Reconstruction

Page 3.8.9 · PCL Biomechanics · biomechanics page

Reproducing both bundles instead of one. The rig shows a gain, the clinic has not, and the gap between them is the whole argument.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Double Bundle Reconstruction

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

If a structure has two parts working at different angles, replacing both should control the joint across more of its range than replacing one.

Whether the extra control is worth the extra operation is a separate question, and it is answered by outcome rather than by mechanics.

A gain that is measurable in a laboratory and invisible in patients is a real result, not a contradiction.

At the Knee

The posterior cruciate has an anterolateral bundle tight in flexion and a posteromedial bundle tight near extension. 3.8.3 covers the pair.

A single bundle reconstruction reproduces the anterolateral bundle, which is the dominant one. 3.8.4 covers it.

A double bundle reconstruction adds a second graft for the posteromedial bundle, which controls the knee nearer extension. 3.8.5 covers that bundle.

Conventional teaching reports better restoration of posterior translation in cadaveric testing with two bundles, and comparable clinical outcomes.

What Changes It

Whether the capsule and corner are intact, since they cover the same range near extension.

Graft availability, since two bundles need more tissue.

Femoral tunnel space, which constrains two tunnels.

Surgical time and complexity.

Clinical Relevance

The mechanical case is strongest where the corner and capsule are also deficient, because then nothing else covers the near extension range. 2.10.4 covers the corner.

Conventional teaching reserves it for selected cases rather than using it as a default. Section 6 covers management.

The same shape of argument occurs in anterior cruciate reconstruction, and the resolution has been similar. 3.7.4 covers that chapter's bundles.

Describing the gain honestly, as measurable and unproven clinically, is better than arguing it either way.

Key Learning Points

  • Replacing both bundles should control more of the range.
  • Whether it is worth it is answered by outcome, not mechanics.
  • A single bundle reconstruction reproduces the dominant anterolateral bundle.
  • Conventional teaching: better cadaveric restoration, comparable clinical outcomes.
  • The case is strongest where the corner and capsule are also deficient.

MRCS Level

The Principle

The argument has three parts and they are usually run together: whether the second bundle does something, whether a graft can reproduce it, and whether reproducing it changes the patient's result. The answers are yes, yes, and not demonstrated.

Keeping them apart is what makes the discussion tractable. A cadaveric study answers the second and a trial answers the third.

The marginal gain is small because the capsule covers much of the same range in an otherwise intact knee. 3.8.5 covers that point.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching reports that double bundle reconstruction restores posterior translation closer to the intact state across the flexion range in robotic testing, with the difference largest near extension.

Clinical series and the available trials report comparable laxity and outcome scores between single and double bundle, in small numbers.

Two femoral tunnels in the medial femoral condyle wall risk convergence and weaken the bone between them, which is a specific technical cost. 3.8.8 covers the construct.

Graft quantity is a real constraint, particularly where a corner reconstruction is also needed.

What Changes It

Capsular and corner integrity, which determine the marginal gain.

Femoral condyle size and tunnel spacing.

Graft availability and diameter.

Whether other reconstructions are being performed at the same sitting.

How It Is Measured

Robotic testing compares constructs against the intact knee at several flexion angles.

Stress radiography measures the clinical laxity result reproducibly.

Randomised and comparative clinical studies are small, which limits what can be concluded.

No study has isolated the combined injury subgroup where the mechanical case is strongest.

Clinical Relevance

Separate the three questions when discussing this with a colleague or a patient.

Expect the cadaveric difference to be largest near extension.

Account for femoral tunnel convergence and bone bridge when planning two tunnels.

Note that the strongest mechanical subgroup has no trial evidence.

Key Learning Points

  • Three questions: does the bundle do something, can a graft reproduce it, does it change outcome.
  • The answers are yes, yes, and not demonstrated.
  • The cadaveric difference is largest near extension.
  • Two femoral tunnels risk convergence and weaken the bone bridge.
  • No study has isolated the combined injury subgroup.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat this as a decision about marginal mechanical gain against definite added complexity, in an operation whose results are already less reliable than its anterior counterpart. 3.8.8 covers the construct.

That framing argues for selectivity rather than for either default. A knee with an intact capsule and corner gains least; a multiligament knee gains most and can least afford the graft and the tunnel space.

Conventional teaching has not settled it, and the honest account to a trainee is that the mechanics favour two bundles and the evidence does not require them.

What Changes It

Corner and capsular status.

Graft quantity available, especially in a multiligament reconstruction.

Femoral tunnel space and bone quality.

Surgeon familiarity, which affects the added complexity's cost.

How It Is Measured

Robotic testing gives the mechanical difference.

Comparative clinical studies give the outcome equivalence, in small numbers.

Stress radiography is the reproducible clinical measure of the result.

Registry data for this ligament is far thinner than for the anterior cruciate.

Clinical Relevance

Decide selectively rather than adopting either technique as a default.

Prefer two bundles where the capsule and corner are deficient and graft allows.

Account for graft quantity in a multiligament knee before committing.

Tell a trainee that the mechanics favour two bundles and the evidence does not require them.

Key Learning Points

  • A marginal mechanical gain against definite added complexity.
  • An intact capsule and corner gains least; a multiligament knee gains most.
  • The multiligament knee can least afford the graft and tunnel space.
  • The mechanics favour two bundles and the evidence does not require them.
  • Registry data for this ligament is far thinner than for the anterior cruciate.

Fellowship Level

At the Knee

At fellowship level the question is usually moot, because the multiligament knee that would benefit most is the one where graft and tunnel space are least available.

That is a resource argument rather than a mechanical one, and recognising it as such stops the discussion going round in circles.

Where two bundles are used, the femoral bone bridge and tunnel convergence are the technical constraints that decide feasibility. 3.8.8 covers tunnel planning.

What Changes It

Number of reconstructions in one sitting and total graft needed.

Femoral condyle dimensions and tunnel trajectories.

Previous tunnels in a revision.

Whether staging is an option.

How It Is Measured

Preoperative computed tomography planning assesses tunnel feasibility and convergence.

Postoperative imaging confirms what was achieved.

Multiligament series report outcomes without separating bundle technique.

No measurement establishes the point at which the added complexity stops being worth it.

Clinical Relevance

Recognise the constraint as a resource one rather than a mechanical one.

Plan tunnel trajectories and bone bridge before committing to two bundles.

Consider staging where graft and time are both short.

Accept that the trade off point is not established.

Key Learning Points

  • The knee that would benefit most has the least graft and tunnel space.
  • That is a resource argument rather than a mechanical one.
  • Bone bridge and tunnel convergence decide feasibility.
  • Multiligament series do not separate bundle technique.
  • The point at which added complexity stops being worth it is not established.
