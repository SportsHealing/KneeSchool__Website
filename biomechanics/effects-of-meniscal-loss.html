Effects of Meniscal Loss

Page 3.5.5 · Tibiofemoral Contact Mechanics · biomechanics page

Taking out a meniscus does not reduce the load. It reduces the area carrying it, and the pressure rises by the same factor.

Effects of Meniscal Loss

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

Removing a structure from a load path does not remove the load. The remaining structures carry it, over whatever area they have.

The menisci enlarge the contact area. Remove one and the area in that compartment falls, so the pressure rises.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Halving the area doubles the pressure for the same force, and no biological process compensates for that.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching holds that total meniscectomy reduces contact area in that compartment by roughly half to two thirds and raises peak contact pressure by a comparable factor.

Partial meniscectomy has a smaller effect, and the effect is roughly proportional to the amount removed. The relationship is a dose response rather than a threshold.

The lateral compartment suffers more from losing its meniscus, because the lateral plateau is convex and has no concavity of its own. 2.6.1 covers the anatomy.

A meniscus can stop working without being removed. A root tear unanchors the ring and an extruded meniscus is outside the contact. 3.6 covers the mechanism.

What Changes It

How much was removed, and from where. The periphery carries the hoop tension.

Which compartment, since the lateral depends on its meniscus more.

Whether the ring is still anchored, which decides whether remaining tissue is working tissue.

Alignment, which concentrates load in the compartment that has lost its meniscus. 3.5.6 covers it.

Clinical Relevance

Meniscectomy leads to degeneration through a mechanical route, and the pressure rise is that route. 6.37 covers meniscal deficiency.

Preserving or repairing a meniscus preserves contact area, which is why repair is preferred where the tear allows it. Section 6 covers management.

Losing a lateral meniscus is mechanically worse than losing a medial one, which is counter to how often each is injured.

A patient who feels better after meniscectomy has had a symptom treated, not a mechanical problem solved.

Key Learning Points

  • Removing a structure from a load path does not remove the load.
  • Conventional teaching: total meniscectomy halves the area and doubles the peak pressure.
  • Partial meniscectomy is a dose response, roughly proportional to the amount removed.
  • The lateral compartment suffers more from meniscal loss than the medial.
  • A root tear or extrusion stops a meniscus working without removing it.

MRCS Level

The Principle

The mechanism is hoop tension. Compression of the meniscal wedge is converted into circumferential tension, which the roots carry into bone. Anything that interrupts that loop removes the function. 3.6 covers it.

That is why the tear pattern matters more than the tear size. A radial tear cuts the circumferential fibres; a longitudinal one does not.

It is also why a root tear is mechanically equivalent to a meniscectomy: an unanchored ring carries no tension however complete it looks.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives contact area reduction after total meniscectomy as roughly 50 to 70 per cent and peak contact stress increase as roughly two to threefold, from cadaveric contact studies.

Partial meniscectomy figures scale with the resection. Conventional teaching holds that even a modest resection measurably raises peak stress, which is the argument against resecting more than necessary.

The posterior horn is the part loaded in flexion, so its loss changes the deep flexion contact most. 3.5.4 covers flexion.

Extrusion is a functional loss on the same logic, and it can be present with a structurally continuous meniscus. 6.36 covers it.

What Changes It

Resection volume and location, with the peripheral rim and the roots as the critical parts.

Tear pattern, since radial and root tears interrupt hoop tension and longitudinal ones may not.

Compartment, with the lateral more dependent.

Alignment and body mass, which set the load the smaller area has to carry.

How It Is Measured

Cadaveric contact studies with pressure film or sensors, comparing intact, torn, repaired and resected states in the same specimen, are where all these figures come from.

The paired design is a strength and the absent muscle and single load condition are the limitations.

Finite element modelling extends the comparison to conditions a rig cannot produce, with its material assumptions.

Long term clinical cohorts measure the consequence, and they measure it over a decade rather than a year.

Clinical Relevance

Resect as little as the tear requires, since the stress rise scales with the resection.

Preserve the peripheral rim and the roots, which carry the hoop tension.

Repair a root tear on the same grounds as a meniscal tear, because mechanically it is one.

Read extrusion as a functional loss even where the meniscus is continuous.

Key Learning Points

  • Hoop tension is the mechanism; interrupting the loop removes the function.
  • Tear pattern matters more than tear size.
  • A root tear is mechanically equivalent to a meniscectomy.
  • Conventional teaching: 50 to 70 per cent area loss and a two to threefold stress rise.
  • Even a modest resection measurably raises peak stress.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat the contact studies as the mechanical case for preservation and the cohorts as the clinical one, and note that they agree. That agreement is unusual and worth relying on.

Meniscal transplantation restores part of the lost area, and conventional teaching holds that it restores less than a native meniscus and that sizing determines how much. 6.43 covers failed transplantation.

Repair of a root restores the hoop mechanism if it restores the attachment at the right position and tension, which is the mechanical reason anatomic repair is specified rather than preferred.

What Changes It

Whether the hoop mechanism can be restored, which is the question a repair answers.

Graft size and position in transplantation, which set the area restored.

Alignment, which decides whether a preserved or restored meniscus is protected or overloaded. 3.13 covers it.

Cartilage state at the time, since a restored area over damaged cartilage is a different proposition.

How It Is Measured

Paired cadaveric contact studies compare intact, deficient, repaired and transplanted states and are the standard for a mechanical claim.

Weight bearing imaging and extrusion measurement assess whether the restored meniscus is in the load path.

Second look arthroscopy and magnetic resonance imaging assess healing rather than function.

Long term cohorts measure joint preservation, and no clinical measurement confirms the contact mechanics were restored in a given patient.

Clinical Relevance

Repair rather than resect wherever the hoop mechanism can be restored.

Size a transplant accurately, since the area restored depends on it.

Correct alignment alongside preservation or transplantation, or the restored area is overloaded.

Explain that healing on imaging is not the same as restored contact mechanics.

Key Learning Points

  • The contact studies and the clinical cohorts agree, which is unusual and useful.
  • Transplantation restores less area than a native meniscus, and sizing decides how much.
  • Anatomic root repair is specified because position and tension restore the hoop mechanism.
  • A restored meniscus in a malaligned limb is overloaded.
  • Healing on imaging is not restored contact mechanics.
