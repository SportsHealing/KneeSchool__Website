Anterior Translation

Page 3.2.7 · Functional Anatomy and Motion · biomechanics page

The tibia sliding forwards under the femur. The one degree of freedom with a clinical instrument, and the one most often mistaken for the whole problem.

Anterior Translation

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

Anterior translation is the tibia moving forwards relative to the femur along the joint's anteroposterior axis.

It is a translation rather than a rotation, so it is measured in millimetres.

It is the knee's best studied motion, because it is the one that can be measured in a clinic with an instrument.

At the Knee

The anterior cruciate ligament is the primary restraint. Conventional teaching gives its share as about 85 per cent of the resistance to anterior translation.

Conventional teaching gives normal anterior translation as about 5 to 10 mm at 30 degrees of flexion, with the side to side difference being the clinically useful quantity.

The secondary restraints are the menisci, particularly the medial posterior horn, the collateral ligaments and the joint surfaces themselves.

The hamstrings resist it dynamically, which is why a tense patient measures tighter than a relaxed one.

What Changes It

Flexion angle. Translation is largest around 30 degrees, which is why the Lachman test is done there rather than at 90.

Cruciate injury increases it, and a meniscal injury alongside increases it further because the secondary restraint has gone too.

Muscle tone reduces it, so examination conditions are part of the measurement.

Tibial slope changes the resting position and the translation under load, which is why it appears in the revision conversation.

Clinical Relevance

The Lachman test at 30 degrees is the most sensitive clinical test for the anterior cruciate ligament, and the anterior drawer at 90 is less so because the secondary restraints contribute more there. 4.10 covers the examination.

The endpoint matters as much as the distance. A soft endpoint with a normal excursion is an abnormal finding.

Because this is the measurable motion, it is the one trials report, which gives it more weight in the literature than its clinical importance alone would justify. 3.2.3 covers the motion that patients describe instead.

Key Learning Points

  • Anterior translation is measured in millimetres, not degrees.
  • Conventional teaching gives the anterior cruciate about 85 per cent of the restraint.
  • Normal translation is about 5 to 10 mm at 30 degrees; side to side difference is what matters.
  • The Lachman test at 30 degrees is more sensitive than the anterior drawer at 90.
  • The endpoint matters as much as the distance.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Translation and rotation are coupled here. Anterior translation of the tibia is greater laterally than medially in a cruciate deficient knee, which means the tibia rotates internally as it subluxes. 3.2.9 covers the coupling.

A test that measures only the translation therefore measures part of the event.

That is the mechanical reason a knee with a normal arthrometer reading can still have a positive pivot shift.

At the Knee

The anteromedial bundle of the anterior cruciate carries more of the restraint in flexion and the posterolateral bundle more near extension, which 2.7.8 and 2.7.9 cover.

The medial meniscus posterior horn is the most important secondary restraint, which is the mechanical basis for the association between chronic cruciate deficiency and medial meniscal tears.

Conventional teaching gives a side to side difference of more than about 3 mm on instrumented testing as the usual threshold for abnormality.

Posterior tibial slope increases anterior translation under axial load, with conventional teaching putting normal slope at about 7 to 10 degrees.

What Changes It

Flexion angle, muscle tone, secondary restraint integrity and slope.

Chronicity, because a deficient knee loses its secondary restraints over time and measures worse later than early.

Graft choice and tension after reconstruction, which set the restored restraint.

How It Is Measured

Instrumented arthrometry applies a known force and measures displacement, usually at about 134 newtons, and reports side to side difference.

Its reliability depends on muscle relaxation and on examiner technique, and it under-detects rotational instability entirely.

Stress radiography is an alternative quantitative method, and examination under anaesthetic removes the muscle variable.

The fact that this motion has an instrument and rotation does not has shaped what the literature measures. 3.2.3 covers that.

Clinical Relevance

Perform the Lachman at 30 degrees and record the endpoint as well as the excursion.

Examine the menisci in a chronic cruciate deficient knee, because the secondary restraint is under load and fails over time.

Measure side to side rather than absolute, since baseline laxity varies widely.

Avoid concluding that a knee is stable from a normal arthrometer reading alone.

Key Learning Points

  • The tibia rotates internally as it subluxes, so a translation measurement captures part of the event.
  • The medial meniscus posterior horn is the most important secondary restraint.
  • Conventional teaching gives a side to side difference above about 3 mm as abnormal.
  • Instrumented testing usually applies about 134 newtons.
  • A normal arthrometer reading does not exclude rotational instability.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat anterior translation as a partial description of anterior instability. The full event is a coupled translation and rotation, and the clinical instrument measures one component of it.

Secondary restraint failure is progressive, which is why the same knee measures worse with time and why early meniscal preservation matters mechanically.

Slope is a bony contribution to a problem usually described as ligamentous, and it is not modifiable without an osteotomy.

What Changes It

Graft position and tension, which set the restored restraint and its behaviour through range.

Slope, which loads the graft every step in the same way alignment loads a collateral reconstruction.

Meniscal state, because a graft in a meniscus deficient knee carries load the meniscus should have taken.

How It Is Measured

Arthrometry is reproducible enough for group comparison and weak for an individual decision, which is the reverse of how it is often used.

Published outcome studies report translation because it is measurable, and report rotation as a grade because it is not, so the two halves of the same problem are reported with different precision.

That asymmetry of measurement is worth naming when reading a trial, because it shapes the conclusion.

Clinical Relevance

Assess rotation alongside translation before judging a reconstruction, since only one of the two has an instrument.

Measure slope in a revision, because it is a modifiable bony cause of a repeated ligamentous failure.

Preserve meniscal tissue in a cruciate deficient knee, since the secondary restraint protects the primary one and the graft that replaces it.

Avoid treating a measured translation as the outcome of interest when the patient's complaint is rotational.

Key Learning Points

  • The clinical instrument measures one component of a coupled event.
  • Secondary restraint failure is progressive, which is the mechanical case for early meniscal preservation.
  • Slope is a bony contribution to a problem usually described as ligamentous.
  • Arthrometry is adequate for groups and weak for individuals, which is the reverse of its common use.
  • Translation and rotation are reported with different precision, which shapes trial conclusions.
