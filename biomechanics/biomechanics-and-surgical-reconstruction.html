Biomechanics and Surgical Reconstruction

Page 3.1.6 · Introduction to Knee Biomechanics · biomechanics page

Why surgeons study mechanics: because an operation is a mechanical intervention and is judged by a mechanical outcome.

Biomechanics and Surgical Reconstruction

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

Every knee operation changes a mechanical property. A reconstruction restores a restraint, an osteotomy moves a load line, a meniscal repair restores contact area, an arthroplasty replaces a bearing surface.

So the question an operation answers is mechanical: which quantity is being restored, and to what value.

That is why mechanics appears in surgical examinations at all. It is the language in which operations are compared.

At the Knee

A ligament reconstruction aims to reproduce a restraint, not a ligament. The graft is a different material in a different place with different properties, and what is being matched is the function.

Graft position decides length behaviour through flexion, which is why footprint accuracy matters more than graft strength in most constructs. 2.7.6 and 2.12.2 cover examples.

Tension at fixation sets the resting length, so a graft fixed at the wrong flexion angle is the wrong length through the whole range even if its position is correct.

Osteotomy works by moving the load line rather than by changing any joint surface, and it is the clearest example of a purely mechanical operation.

What Changes It

Graft choice changes stiffness and failure load. A stiffer graft restrains more and shares load less.

Fixation changes where the construct's weakest point sits and how much it moves before loading.

Alignment changes the load a construct carries, which is why an uncorrected deformity shortens the life of a reconstruction.

Rehabilitation changes the loading history of the graft while it remodels, which is why protocols are mechanical documents.

How It Is Measured

Laboratory testing measures construct stiffness and failure load in cadavers, usually at time zero and without healing.

Robotic testing measures restraint restored under simulated loads, which is closer to the clinical question and still without muscle.

Clinical outcome measures laxity instruments, pivot shift grade and patient reported scores, which are three different kinds of evidence.

Time zero mechanics is not healed mechanics. A construct is weakest some weeks after surgery, not on the day of it, which is the single most important limit of laboratory data.

Clinical Relevance

Judge a reconstruction by whether it restores the restraint, not by whether it looks like the original structure.

Expect graft position to matter more than graft choice in most published comparisons.

Correct alignment where it is abnormal, because the construct carries the consequence at every step.

Read a rehabilitation protocol as a loading schedule rather than as a list of exercises.

Key Learning Points

  • Every knee operation changes a mechanical property, so the question it answers is mechanical.
  • A reconstruction reproduces a restraint, not a ligament.
  • Graft position sets length behaviour; tension at fixation sets resting length.
  • Time zero laboratory mechanics is not healed mechanics.
  • A rehabilitation protocol is a loading schedule.

MRCS Level

The Principle

State the design problem: restore a restraint using a different material, fixed at two points, in a joint that moves through a hundred and thirty degrees. Each of those constraints produces a failure mode.

Isometry is the usual organising idea. A graft whose length changes little through the range is loaded predictably; one whose length changes is either slack somewhere or overloaded somewhere.

Over-constraint and under-constraint are both failures, and only one of them presents as instability.

At the Knee

Conventional teaching gives hamstring and patellar tendon graft ultimate loads in the same range as or above the native anterior cruciate, so failure is rarely about graft strength at time zero.

Tunnel position error is the commonest technical cause of failure, which follows from the length behaviour argument rather than from any one study.

Osteotomy and ligament reconstruction interact. An uncorrected varus knee loads a lateral reconstruction at every step, which Section 7 covers.

Meniscal preservation is a mechanical decision: contact area falls with tissue removed and pressure rises for the same load. 3.6 covers it.

What Changes It

Graft remodelling changes material properties over months, passing through a weaker phase. The protocol is written around that curve.

Fixation distance from the joint line changes construct compliance, the so called bungee and windscreen wiper effects.

Patient alignment, activity and mass change the load the construct sees for the rest of its life.

How It Is Measured

Time zero cadaveric testing, robotic kinematic testing, animal healing models and clinical outcome studies each answer part of the question and none answers all of it.

Instrumented laxity measurement quantifies translation and correlates imperfectly with function, which is why pivot shift grade is reported alongside it.

Graft failure in registries is defined by revision, which undercounts graft failure that is not revised.

Clinical Relevance

Plan tunnel position from landmarks and confirm it, because position is the variable most associated with failure.

Assess and correct alignment before or with a soft tissue reconstruction in a deformed limb.

Preserve meniscal tissue wherever repair is possible, because contact pressure is the mechanical consequence of removing it.

Avoid judging a construct by its time zero strength, since the weakest point comes later.

Key Learning Points

  • Restore a restraint with a different material, two fixation points, through 130 degrees.
  • Isometry decides whether a graft is loaded predictably.
  • Over-constraint and under-constraint are both failures; only one presents as instability.
  • Tunnel position is the commonest technical cause of failure.
  • Registry failure is defined by revision, which undercounts failure.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat the knee as a system of restraints whose contributions change with flexion angle, and a reconstruction as an alteration to one term in that system.

A construct restores restraint in the plane and the range its geometry allows. Nothing restores the native pattern, and claiming otherwise misdescribes what was done.

Combined injuries change the target. Restoring one restraint in a knee that has lost three leaves the construct carrying load it was not tensioned for, which 2.10.4 covers.

What Changes It

Alignment is the variable with the largest effect per step and the one most often left uncorrected.

Slope, in the sagittal plane, changes anterior translation and graft load, and is now part of the revision conversation.

Activity level and expectation set the loading history, so two identical constructs in two patients are not under identical demand.

How It Is Measured

The gap between laboratory restraint restoration and clinical outcome is where most disagreement in this field lives. A construct can restore laxity measurements and leave a patient unsatisfied.

Patient reported outcome measures and return to sport rates answer a question that mechanics does not, which is worth stating before quoting either as a mechanical result.

Registry data gives revision rates at scale with little mechanical detail; cadaveric work gives mechanical detail with no patients.

Clinical Relevance

Assess the whole restraint system before reconstructing one part of it.

Measure and consider alignment and slope in any revision, since both are now recognised contributors to failure.

Set the expectation that a construct restores a restraint rather than a knee, and document that conversation.

Avoid attributing a clinical failure to a graft when the mechanical environment it was placed in was never corrected.

Key Learning Points

  • A reconstruction alters one term in a system of restraints that varies with flexion.
  • Nothing restores the native pattern; claiming otherwise misdescribes the operation.
  • Alignment has the largest effect per step and is most often left uncorrected.
  • Tibial slope is now part of the revision conversation.
  • A construct can restore laxity measurements and leave a patient unsatisfied.
