Osteotomy Landmark Studies

Page 3.17.7 · Landmark Biomechanics Papers · landmark_papers page

An operation justified by a mechanical argument that is almost certainly right, with a literature that has never been able to prove it.

Osteotomy Landmark Studies

Choose a depth

FRCS Level

What the Question Was

Osteotomy rests on a single mechanical claim, and the literature is the story of trying to establish it.

Does load distribution across the knee follow alignment. Does changing alignment change the load. Does changing the load change the disease. And how much correction is right.

The first two are answerable in a laboratory and are answered. The third is the one that matters and the one no study has settled. The fourth has an answer that is widely used and weakly supported. 3.13.9 covers osteotomy biomechanics.

What Was Done

Coventry described proximal tibial valgus osteotomy as a procedure for medial compartment disease in the 1960s and reported his series through the following decades.

Maquet argued the mechanical case for osteotomy from a load distribution analysis, which gave the operation a rationale rather than only a result.

Fujisawa and colleagues examined cartilage at arthroscopy before and after osteotomy and described the correction point now named after that work.

Hernigou and colleagues related the amount of correction achieved to survival of the procedure over long follow up, from a clinical series.

Dugdale and Noyes examined correction planning where ligament laxity is present, and showed that measured alignment and functional alignment differ when the lateral structures are lax.

Paley set out general deformity correction principles, including the centre of rotation of angulation, which brought osteotomy planning into line with the rest of limb reconstruction.

What It Showed

Load distribution across the tibiofemoral joint tracks alignment closely, and a small angular change moves a large proportion of load. 3.13.8 covers alignment and osteoarthritis.

Correcting alignment redistributes load in cadaveric and modelled knees in the direction and roughly the magnitude the mechanical argument predicts.

Clinical survival relates to the correction achieved, with undercorrection associated with earlier failure, which is the strongest clinical support the mechanical claim has.

Cartilage can show improvement after correction, which is the most direct evidence the literature offers that load change affects the disease.

Where the lateral structures are lax, the alignment measured on a radiograph overstates the correction the knee will experience under load.

No randomised comparison establishes that osteotomy changes the course of osteoarthritis against a non-operative control.

What It Changed

Alignment became a planned quantity rather than an impression, and preoperative planning became standard.

A target correction entered practice, and most practice still uses a target derived from that early arthroscopic work.

Ligament laxity entered osteotomy planning, which it had not been part of before.

Osteotomy survived the arrival of arthroplasty as an operation for the young patient, largely on the strength of the mechanical argument rather than on trial evidence.

Key Learning Points

  • Load distribution tracks alignment closely, and small angular changes move large load fractions.
  • Clinical survival relates to correction achieved, which is the strongest clinical support for the mechanical case.
  • Lateral laxity makes radiographic alignment overstate the correction the knee will feel.
  • The correction target in common use comes from early arthroscopic observation.
  • No randomised comparison against non-operative care establishes disease modification.

Fellowship Level

What Was Done

The mechanical studies are cadaveric or modelled, and both assume a knee whose soft tissues behave normally, which an arthritic knee's do not.

The clinical studies are series rather than trials, and a series of a procedure done for a selected indication cannot separate the operation from the selection.

Correction is measured on radiographs taken supine or standing, and the two give different answers in a knee with laxity or with a thrust.

Follow up in the survival studies is long, which is a genuine strength, and the cohorts are from eras with different indications and techniques, which limits what the survival figures transfer to.

What It Showed

Where the literature is strong: the relationship between alignment and load, and the association between undercorrection and failure.

Where it is weak: disease modification. The mechanical chain is plausible at every link and the end to end claim has not been tested against a control.

Where it is contested: the correct target. Overcorrection into valgus is justified by load redistribution and penalised by lateral compartment loading and patient dissatisfaction with the resulting appearance and gait.

Dynamic alignment adds something static radiographs cannot. A varus thrust during walking indicates a loading pattern that the standing film does not show. 3.13.7 covers it.

What to Read Now

Read correction targets with the radiographic protocol and the laxity assessment attached.

Treat survival series as evidence about selected patients and techniques of their era.

Read the dynamic alignment work before planning in a knee with a thrust, since the static film will mislead.

Do not quote the mechanical argument as though it were the clinical evidence. They are different strengths of claim.

Key Learning Points

  • Mechanical studies assume normal soft tissues, which an arthritic knee does not have.
  • A series cannot separate the operation from the selection that produced it.
  • Standing and supine radiographs disagree in a lax or thrusting knee.
  • The mechanical argument and the clinical evidence are different strengths of claim and are often quoted as one.

Consultant Perspective

What It Changed

The decision this literature informs is osteotomy against arthroplasty against waiting in the younger patient with unicompartmental disease, and the balance is genuinely close.

Arguing for osteotomy: it preserves the joint, the mechanical rationale is sound, the survival data over decades are reasonable, and it leaves arthroplasty available later.

Arguing against: recovery is long and uncomfortable, the evidence for disease modification is absent rather than negative, arthroplasty results in this group are better than they once were, and a failed osteotomy complicates the arthroplasty that follows.

Weigh age and physiology, since this operation's whole case is that it buys time a younger patient needs. Weigh comorbidities, because union and rehabilitation both depend on them and smoking in particular changes the arithmetic. Weigh the demands on the knee, since osteotomy suits a patient who intends to keep loading the joint hard and arthroplasty does not. Weigh their psychological state and their tolerance of a long recovery, because this is the operation patients most often abandon partway through rehabilitation. And elicit their ideas, concerns and expectations, since many arrive wanting the definitive operation and osteotomy is explicitly not that.

State the balance, and record what the alternative was and why it was not chosen.

What to Read Now

What a trainee should take from this chapter is that a mechanical argument can be correct and still not constitute evidence that the operation works.

Read the long survival series for what they say about correction and the short trials for what they say about symptoms, and do not merge them.

Teach that the absence of disease modification evidence here is an absence rather than a negative finding, because those are different and trainees routinely hear the first as the second.

Key Learning Points

  • Osteotomy against arthroplasty against waiting is a close balance in the younger patient.
  • Joint preservation and a sound rationale weigh against a long recovery and absent disease modification evidence.
  • The five patient factors, and tolerance of recovery in particular, decide most of these consultations.
  • A correct mechanical argument is not evidence that an operation works.
  • Absent evidence and negative evidence are different, and this chapter is where the difference matters.
