Q Angle

Page 3.12.6 · Patellofemoral Biomechanics · biomechanics page

A single angle that tried to describe the whole line of pull on the patella. It is easy to measure, it survives in teaching, and it predicts poorly.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Q Angle

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

If a rope passes over a pulley and the two ends are not in line, the pulley is pushed sideways towards the outside of the bend.

The more the two ends diverge, the harder the sideways push.

Measuring that divergence with one angle is attractive, because it turns a complicated three dimensional problem into a number.

At the Knee

The quadriceps pulls the patella upwards and slightly outwards. The patellar tendon holds it down towards the tibial tubercle. 2.2.5 covers the tubercle.

The Q angle is the angle between a line from the pelvis to the patella and a line from the patella to the tubercle.

A larger angle means a larger sideways push on the patella, pointing outwards.

The angle is larger in women on average, mostly because the pelvis is wider relative to the knee.

What Changes It

Pelvic width relative to the length and the alignment of the leg.

The position of the tibial tubercle. 3.12.12 covers it.

Knee alignment in the coronal plane, meaning how knock kneed or bow legged the knee is. 3.13.4 covers valgus.

The position of the hip and foot at the moment of measurement, which is why the number moves.

Clinical Relevance

A large Q angle used to be treated as an explanation for patellofemoral pain, and that link turned out to be weak. 6.60 covers the condition.

Most people with a large angle have no symptoms, and many with symptoms have a normal angle.

It remains useful as a way of explaining why the push on the patella is outwards rather than straight.

4.15 covers how the patellofemoral joint is actually examined.

Key Learning Points

  • A rope over a pulley with ends out of line pushes the pulley sideways.
  • The Q angle measures the divergence between the quadriceps and patellar tendon lines.
  • A larger angle means a larger outward push on the patella.
  • It is larger in women on average, mostly because of pelvic width.
  • Its link with patellofemoral pain turned out to be weak.

MRCS Level

The Principle

The Q angle is a two dimensional projection of a three dimensional vector, measured on a surface rather than on bone.

Every step of that sentence loses information, and the losses are not small.

A measurement that is reproducible but not valid is worse than one that is neither, because it is believed.

At the Knee

It is measured in the coronal plane from the anterior superior iliac spine to the centre of the patella, and from the patella to the tibial tubercle.

Quoted normal values are around 14 degrees in men and 17 degrees in women, with a wide spread and no agreed threshold.

The angle changes with hip rotation, knee flexion, foot position and whether the quadriceps is contracted, none of which is standardised between studies.

It ignores the transverse plane entirely, which is where femoral anteversion and tibial torsion act. 3.12.11 covers them.

It uses the patella as a landmark, so a patella that is already displaced reduces the measured angle in the knee that most needs describing.

What Changes It

Limb position at measurement, which is the largest source of variation.

Quadriceps contraction, which moves the patella and therefore the middle point.

Tubercle position in the transverse plane, which the angle only partly captures. 3.12.12 covers it.

Coronal alignment, which it captures reasonably. 3.13.3 covers varus.

Rotational alignment, which it does not capture at all.

How It Is Measured

Goniometer measurement supine or standing, with the published protocols differing in position, flexion angle and quadriceps state.

Interobserver agreement is moderate at best, and the same observer on the same knee can differ by several degrees.

Radiographic and tomographic alternatives measure bone rather than skin, which is the reason the tubercle to groove distance replaced it. 3.12.12 covers that.

No study has established a threshold that separates symptomatic from asymptomatic knees.

The measurement therefore has reasonable face validity and poor construct validity.

Clinical Relevance

Measure the tubercle to groove distance rather than the Q angle where a decision depends on it. 3.12.12 covers the index.

Do not use a Q angle to explain anterior knee pain, because the association does not support it. 6.60 covers the condition.

Record the limb position if the angle is measured at all, since the number is uninterpretable without it.

Use the angle as a teaching device for the direction of the vector, which is what it is good for.

Key Learning Points

  • The Q angle is a two dimensional projection of a three dimensional vector, measured on skin.
  • Quoted normals are around 14 degrees in men and 17 in women, with no agreed threshold.
  • The number changes with hip rotation, flexion, foot position and quadriceps state.
  • It ignores the transverse plane, where anteversion and torsion act.
  • A displaced patella reduces the measured angle in the knee that most needs describing.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

The Q angle is the clearest example in this chapter of a measurement that survived because it was easy, not because it worked.

Its failure is structural rather than technical. Improving the goniometry does not fix a projection that omits a plane and uses a mobile landmark.

The tubercle to groove distance replaced it by measuring bone to bone in the transverse plane, which is where the lateral vector is actually generated. 3.12.12 covers it.

That replacement has its own limits: it varies with knee flexion and rotation during acquisition and has a soft threshold. 5.12.4 covers the imaging.

The useful lesson is that a vector with components in three planes cannot be reduced to one number, and the chapter's other pages exist because of that.

What Changes It

Nothing about the patient that the angle measures reliably, which is the point.

Measurement position, which changes the number more than most patient factors do.

Prior realignment surgery, which changes the tubercle and therefore the angle. 7.53 covers the operation.

Patellar position, since a subluxed patella falsifies the middle landmark.

Coronal alignment, which it does capture, and which 3.13 covers properly.

How It Is Measured

Surface goniometry, with protocols that differ enough to make published normals incomparable.

Tubercle to groove distance on computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, which is the current standard and is not interchangeable with the Q angle. 3.12.12 covers it.

Patellar tendon to trochlear groove distance, which removes the influence of tubercle rotation and is less widely reported.

Three dimensional reconstruction, which measures the vector properly and is not routine. 3.16.5 covers computational methods.

No surface measurement has been shown to predict symptoms or outcome in this joint.

Clinical Relevance

Abandon the Q angle for decision making and keep it for explanation.

Quote the tubercle to groove distance with the imaging modality and the knee position attached. 3.12.12 covers the index.

Treat a normal index in a symptomatic knee as a reason to look at morphology and rotation, not as reassurance. 3.12.4 covers morphology.

Resist a single number for a three plane problem, which is the chapter's general lesson.

Read the historical literature built on the Q angle with its measurement limits in mind. 3.17.6 covers the landmark studies.

Key Learning Points

  • The Q angle survived because it was easy, not because it worked.
  • Its failure is structural: it omits a plane and uses a mobile landmark.
  • The tubercle to groove distance replaced it by measuring bone to bone.
  • That replacement has its own limits and a soft threshold.
  • A vector with components in three planes cannot be reduced to one number.
