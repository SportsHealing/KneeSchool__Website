Medial Knee Landmark Studies

Page 3.17.4 · Landmark Biomechanics Papers · landmark_papers page

A literature that spent thirty years turning a vague medial side into named layers with measured jobs, and still has not settled what to do when they fail.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Medial Knee Landmark Studies

Choose a depth

FRCS Level

What the Question Was

The medial side was described as a mass of tissue long after the cruciates had been separated into bundles, and the literature reflects that late start.

What structures are there and how are they arranged in depth. Which one resists valgus and at what flexion angle. What resists anteromedial rotation, which is a different question. And does a medial injury need reconstruction or does it heal.

The last question is the one the chapter still cannot answer, and the reason is that the medial side heals well enough often enough to make a trial difficult. 3.9.1 covers valgus stability.

What Was Done

Warren and Marshall described the medial side as three layers in the 1970s, from cadaveric dissection, which gave the region a vocabulary it had lacked.

Hughston and Eilers examined the posteromedial structures and argued that the posterior oblique fibres were a functional unit rather than part of a sheet.

Grood and colleagues ran sequential sectioning on the medial structures in the 1980s, measuring valgus laxity at a range of flexion angles after cutting each layer in turn.

Robinson and Amis tested the medial structures in the 2000s with the posteromedial capsule and the deep fibres separated, and looked at rotation as well as valgus.

LaPrade and colleagues quantified the medial anatomy against bony landmarks in the 2000s, producing the measurements reconstruction techniques are now built from.

Griffith and LaPrade separated the individual medial structures' contributions experimentally, including the deep and superficial parts of the medial collateral.

What It Showed

The layered description is correct and useful, and most medial confusion in teaching comes from collapsing the layers into one structure.

The superficial medial collateral is the primary restraint to valgus, and its contribution depends on flexion angle rather than being constant. 3.9.3 covers its function.

The posteromedial structures restrain anteromedial rotation, which the superficial band does much less well, so a knee can be valgus stable and rotationally unstable. 3.9.7 covers that pattern.

The deep fibres and the posterior oblique fibres have distinct jobs, and sectioning them separately produces different laxity patterns.

Quantified anatomy against bony landmarks made anatomic reconstruction reproducible, which the layered description alone did not.

What It Changed

Examination teaching separated valgus testing at full extension from valgus testing in flexion, which the sectioning work gave a reason for.

Anteromedial rotatory instability became a recognised entity with a mechanical basis rather than a clinical impression.

Reconstruction technique moved from reefing available tissue to reconstructing named structures at measured positions.

Combined ACL and medial injury management changed, because the medial contribution to rotational control affects what the cruciate reconstruction is asked to do. 3.9.10 covers the combination.

Key Learning Points

  • The medial side is three layers, and most teaching confusion comes from treating it as one.
  • The superficial medial collateral is the primary valgus restraint and its contribution varies with flexion angle.
  • The posteromedial structures restrain rotation, so valgus stability and rotational stability are separate findings.
  • Quantified anatomy against bony landmarks is what made anatomic reconstruction reproducible.
  • Medial rotational contribution changes what a combined cruciate reconstruction must achieve.

Fellowship Level

What Was Done

Sectioning order is the methodological weak point again, and it bites harder here than on the cruciate side because the medial structures are continuous with each other.

Separating the deep fibres from the superficial band is a dissection judgement, and two laboratories can draw that boundary differently and report different contributions.

Rotation measurement requires a defined coordinate system, and medial studies predating a standard one are hard to compare with those after it.

The quantified anatomy work used a modest number of specimens and reports means with ranges, and the ranges are wide enough that a measured position is a target rather than a constant.

What It Showed

Where the literature is strong: the layer description, the valgus hierarchy, and the separation of rotation from valgus.

Where it is weak: healing. The claim that medial injuries heal is supported mostly by clinical series with no imaging endpoint, and what heals mechanically rather than radiologically is largely unmeasured.

Where it is contested: whether a grade three medial injury alongside an ACL tear should be reconstructed, braced, or left. All three positions cite the same mechanical work.

The flexion angle dependence is the detail most often lost in summary, and it is the detail that makes examination findings interpretable.

What to Read Now

Read a medial sectioning study's dissection boundary before its attribution, since the boundary is a judgement rather than a given.

Treat quantified anatomical positions as targets with ranges rather than as fixed points.

Read the healing literature separately from the mechanical literature and do not let the strength of the second lend credibility to the first.

Read rotation results only where the coordinate system is stated.

Key Learning Points

  • The deep and superficial boundary is a dissection judgement, which limits comparison between studies.
  • Quantified anatomical positions carry wide ranges and are targets rather than constants.
  • The healing literature is clinical and weak while the mechanical literature is strong, and the two are often quoted together.
  • Rotation findings are uninterpretable without a stated coordinate system.

Consultant Perspective

What It Changed

The decision this literature informs is what to do with a significant medial injury alongside a cruciate tear, and the mechanical evidence genuinely supports more than one answer.

Arguing for reconstructing the medial side: the rotation work shows the medial structures do a job the cruciate graft cannot, and a graft loaded by residual medial laxity is a graft at risk.

Arguing against: the medial side heals more reliably than any other ligament around the knee, a second reconstruction adds stiffness and morbidity, and the published results of bracing are not obviously worse.

Take the patient's age and physiology into account, since healing capacity is the whole argument for waiting and it is not uniform. Take comorbidities into account, because stiffness after medial surgery is more likely in some of them. Take the demands on the knee into account, since rotational demand is what residual medial laxity actually troubles. Take the psychological state into account, because a staged plan requires a patient who can tolerate an interval of uncertainty. And ask for their ideas, concerns and expectations, since a patient who wants one operation and a patient who wants the smallest operation will accept different plans.

Decide on the balance, state it, and record what would change it.

What to Read Now

What a trainee should take from this chapter is that a well established mechanical hierarchy does not settle a management question, and the medial side is the cleanest example.

Read the rotation work before the valgus work, because rotation is where the management argument actually lives.

Teach the layers and the flexion dependence together, since each is hard to use without the other.

Key Learning Points

  • What to do with a combined medial and cruciate injury is a balance the mechanical literature does not settle.
  • Healing capacity is the argument for waiting and it varies between patients more than the mechanics do.
  • The five patient factors are what separates the three defensible plans.
  • Rotation, not valgus, is where the management argument sits.
  • A strong mechanical hierarchy and an unsettled management question can coexist.
