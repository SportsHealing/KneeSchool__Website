Root Biomechanics

Page 3.6.9 · Meniscal Biomechanics · biomechanics page

Where the ring is anchored. The roots take the hoop tension into bone, and a meniscus without them carries nothing.

Root Biomechanics

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

A rope under tension needs something to pull against at both ends. Without an anchor the rope goes slack and carries no load.

The anchor has to be in the right place as well as strong. Moving it changes the direction of the pull and how much tension can be held.

A tent guy rope shows both. It needs a peg, and the peg needs to be in the right spot.

At the Knee

Each meniscus has an anterior and a posterior root attaching it to the tibia. 2.5.7 and 2.6.4 cover the attachments.

The roots carry the hoop tension into bone. That is their mechanical job, and the rest of the meniscus depends on them doing it. 3.6.2 covers hoop stress.

The attachment is not a simple insertion. It has a transitional zone of fibrocartilage into bone, like a tendon insertion, which spreads the stress at the junction.

Root tension rises with flexion, because the load share through the posterior horns rises with flexion. 3.6.5 covers load sharing.

What Changes It

Whether the root is attached. A detached root leaves a complete ring carrying nothing. 3.6.11 covers root tears.

Where it is attached, since position changes the direction of the pull.

Flexion angle, which changes the tension the root carries.

The condition of the bone at the attachment, which is where the tension ends up.

Clinical Relevance

A root tear is mechanically equivalent to removing the meniscus, which is why it matters out of proportion to its size.

Repairing a root restores an anchor, and the position of the repair matters as much as its strength. Section 7 covers technique.

The anterior root of the medial meniscus lies close to the anterior cruciate's tibial attachment, which matters when a tunnel is drilled there. 2.5.7 covers the overlap.

A meniscus can be entirely normal in appearance and functionless if a root has gone. 5.6 covers imaging.

Key Learning Points

  • A rope under tension needs an anchor at both ends.
  • The roots carry the hoop tension into bone.
  • The attachment has a transitional fibrocartilage zone, like a tendon insertion.
  • Root tension rises with flexion, as the posterior horn load share rises.
  • A detached root leaves a complete ring carrying nothing.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Position, orientation and strength are three separate properties of a root attachment, and a repair can restore one without the others.

The footprint has an area and a centre, and conventional teaching holds that restoring the centre matters more than covering the whole footprint.

Root strain is the measurable quantity. It rises with joint load and with flexion, and it is what a repair construct has to hold.

At the Knee

The posterior medial meniscal root attaches just posterior and medial to the tibial attachment of the posterior cruciate, and the posterior lateral root sits adjacent to the lateral intercondylar eminence. 2.6.4 covers the anatomy.

The anterior medial root footprint overlaps the anterior cruciate's tibial footprint, so a tunnel of useful diameter will involve some root fibres. 2.5.7 covers it.

Conventional teaching holds that posterior root tears are commoner medially and that lateral root tears are commonly associated with anterior cruciate injury.

Meniscofemoral ligaments provide a secondary attachment for the posterior lateral meniscus, which is why a lateral root tear behaves less severely than a medial one. 2.6.3 covers them.

What Changes It

Attachment integrity, which is the primary variable.

Repair position relative to the native footprint centre.

Flexion angle and load, which set the strain.

Bone quality at the attachment, which determines fixation.

How It Is Measured

Cadaveric footprint mapping measures the position and area of the attachments.

Root load cells and strain measurement under applied load measure the tension arriving at the root.

Contact pressure studies measure the consequence of a root tear rather than the root's own mechanics.

Imaging localises the attachment in a patient and does not measure its function. 5.6 covers imaging.

Clinical Relevance

Repair to the footprint centre rather than aiming to cover the whole footprint.

Expect a lateral root tear to behave less severely than a medial one, because of the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Look for a lateral root tear in anterior cruciate injury, where the association is recognised.

Account for the anterior medial root when drilling a tibial cruciate tunnel.

Key Learning Points

  • Position, orientation and strength are three separate properties of a root attachment.
  • Restoring the footprint centre matters more than covering the whole footprint.
  • The anterior medial root footprint overlaps the anterior cruciate's tibial footprint.
  • Meniscofemoral ligaments give the posterior lateral meniscus a secondary attachment.
  • Root strain rises with load and with flexion.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

Treat a root as a load bearing attachment with a position specification. A repair that is strong but misplaced restores an anchor in the wrong direction, and the hoop tension it carries is not the native one.

Conventional teaching holds that non anatomic root repair restores contact mechanics less well than anatomic repair, which is a position argument rather than a fixation one.

The anterior medial root's overlap with the cruciate footprint is the one place where two operations compete for the same bone, and it is a reason to know the footprint rather than to avoid the tunnel. 2.5.7 covers it.

What Changes It

Repair position relative to the native centre, which is the dominant variable.

Fixation method and bone quality, which decide whether the repair holds.

Alignment, which sets the load the repaired root must carry. 3.13 covers it.

Concurrent cruciate surgery, where tunnels and roots share bone.

How It Is Measured

Cadaveric contact studies compare anatomic and non anatomic root repair against the intact and torn states.

Cyclic loading measures displacement of the repaired root, which is the construct losing position.

Postoperative imaging assesses position and healing rather than restored tension.

No clinical measurement confirms a repaired root is carrying hoop tension.

Clinical Relevance

Repair a root anatomically, since position decides the mechanics more than fixation strength does.

Plan tunnel and root work together where they share bone.

Correct alignment where a repaired root would be overloaded.

Expect imaging to show position and healing rather than function.

Key Learning Points

  • A strong but misplaced root repair restores an anchor in the wrong direction.
  • Non anatomic repair restores contact mechanics less well, by conventional teaching.
  • Position decides the mechanics more than fixation strength does.
  • The anterior medial root and the cruciate tunnel compete for the same bone.
  • No clinical measurement confirms a repaired root carries hoop tension.
