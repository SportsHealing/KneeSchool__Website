Cricket

Page 3.15.2 · Sports-Specific Knee Biomechanics · biomechanics page

A sport where one delivery stride loads the front knee very heavily, repeated hundreds of times over days.

Cricket

Choose a depth

For Patients

The Principle

A load that is bearable once can be damaging if it is repeated often enough without recovery.

That is a different kind of risk from a single bad landing, and it is managed differently.

Counting how often something is done matters as much as how hard it is done.

At the Knee

A fast bowler's front leg lands almost straight and is used as a block, converting the run up into ball speed.

That landing puts several times body weight through the front knee in a fraction of a second.

It is repeated every delivery, over an over, a spell, a day and a season.

Fielding adds diving, sliding and sudden turns, and wicketkeeping adds hours of deep squatting. 3.12.3 covers kneecap pressures.

Clinical Relevance

Knee pain in a bowler is usually a loading problem rather than a single injury, and the history is about workload.

Keeping a record of overs bowled is genuinely useful information for a clinician.

Pain that settles with rest and returns at the same point in a spell suggests overload rather than damage.

No injury rates are given on this page. No source was available to check them against.

Key Learning Points

  • A bearable load repeated often enough becomes damaging.
  • The front leg lands almost straight and acts as a block.
  • That puts several times body weight through the front knee in a fraction of a second.
  • It is repeated across an over, a spell, a day and a season.
  • Knee pain in a bowler is usually about workload rather than one injury.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Why is the front knee kept straight when bowling? A straighter leg is a more rigid block, and a rigid block converts more of the run up into ball speed. It is faster and it costs more at the knee.

Q. Is spin bowling easier on the knee? The front foot impact is much smaller, so the single large load is not there. Spinners bowl longer spells, so the total count can still be high.

Q. Does wicketkeeping damage the knees? Prolonged deep squatting loads the kneecap joint heavily. It is a sustained load rather than an impact, and discomfort is common while lasting damage is less clear.

Q. How do I know if I am bowling too much? Pain that appears at the same point in a spell, and returns earlier each time, is the usual warning. Recording overs makes this visible rather than remembered.

See a clinician if your knee swells after a delivery, gives way, locks, or if pain stops you completing a spell.

For Medical Students

The Principle

Sporting knee injury divides into acute events and cumulative loading, and the two need different histories.

Cricket is dominated by the second, which is why its knee problems present as pain rather than as instability.

Cumulative load is the product of load per repetition and the number of repetitions, and the second term is usually the modifiable one. 3.14.4 covers the same argument for running.

At the Knee

Front foot contact in fast bowling produces a high ground reaction force with the knee near extension, so the quadriceps and the patellar tendon take it. 3.4.2 covers the forces.

A near extended knee at impact has a small contact area in the patellofemoral joint, which raises pressure. 3.12.2 covers the areas.

Bowling technique varies the knee flexion at landing, and a more flexed landing lowers peak force and ball speed together.

Wicketkeeping involves sustained deep flexion, which loads the patellofemoral joint and the posterior horn of the meniscus. 3.6 covers the meniscus.

Fielding adds the acute mechanisms common to field sports: diving, sliding and turning. 3.15.1 covers those.

What Changes It

Overs bowled and their distribution across days, which is the dominant variable.

Knee flexion angle at front foot contact, which trades load against pace.

Footwear and surface hardness.

Trunk and hip mechanics in the delivery stride, which also drive lumbar stress injury.

Age and skeletal maturity, since adolescent bowlers are the group most affected by workload.

Clinical Relevance

Take a workload history in overs and spells, which is the specific history for this sport.

Expect anterior knee pain and patellar tendon problems rather than instability. 6.64 covers tendinopathy.

Ask wicketkeepers about sustained squatting separately, since the mechanism differs.

Avoid quoting injury rates, which this page does not, because no source was available.

Key Learning Points

  • Sporting injury divides into acute events and cumulative loading.
  • Cricket is dominated by cumulative loading, so it presents as pain not instability.
  • Front foot contact loads a near extended knee through the extensor mechanism.
  • A more flexed landing lowers peak force and ball speed together.
  • Wicketkeeping loads the patellofemoral joint through sustained deep flexion.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Workload management is the only intervention in this chapter with a plausible dose response relationship, and cricket is where it has been studied most.

That work is largely about the lumbar spine in fast bowlers, and the knee has been carried along with it rather than studied in its own right.

So the principle is well founded and the knee specific evidence behind it is thin, which is worth saying.

At the Knee

Front foot contact generates ground reaction forces several times body weight with a short contact time, and the knee is the second joint in that chain after the ankle.

Peak patellofemoral stress is high because flexion at impact is low, which keeps the contact area small. 3.12.3 covers pressure.

Patellar tendinopathy and fat pad irritation are the characteristic presentations, with meniscal and chondral problems appearing later. 6.64 covers tendinopathy.

Wicketkeeping's sustained deep flexion is a different mechanical exposure and plausibly loads the posterior horn and the patellofemoral joint. 3.6 covers the meniscus.

The acute injuries are fielding injuries and follow field sport mechanisms rather than bowling ones. 3.15.1 covers them.

What Changes It

Cumulative overs and acute to chronic workload change, which is the framework used in the sport.

Technique, specifically knee flexion at front foot contact.

Skeletal maturity, since adolescent bowlers carry disproportionate injury burden.

Playing format, since the long format concentrates workload in days.

Surface and footwear, which alter the loading rate.

How It Is Measured

Force plate and motion capture of the delivery stride, which exists in biomechanics laboratories at national level. 3.16.7 covers capture.

Wearable workload monitoring, which counts deliveries and estimates intensity. 3.16.8 covers it.

Over counts and bowling logs, which are what workload guidelines are actually built on.

Imaging for tendon and bone stress change, which follows the clinical presentation. 5.11 covers cartilage imaging.

Knee specific biomechanical data in cricket is sparse compared with the lumbar literature, so claims here rest on mechanism and on neighbouring sports.

Clinical Relevance

Take overs bowled, spell length and recent change in workload as the core history.

Expect an extensor mechanism presentation and ask about the point in a spell at which pain appears.

Treat adolescent bowlers' workload as the specific risk, since that is where the sport's evidence concentrates.

State that the knee specific evidence in cricket is thinner than the lumbar evidence it borrows from.

Weigh the player's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, because selection pressure makes under reporting of pain common in this sport.

Key Learning Points

  • Workload management is the intervention here with a plausible dose response relationship.
  • That evidence is largely lumbar, and the knee has been carried along with it.
  • Low flexion at front foot contact keeps patellofemoral contact area small and pressure high.
  • Patellar tendinopathy and fat pad irritation are characteristic.
  • Adolescent bowlers carry disproportionate injury burden.
