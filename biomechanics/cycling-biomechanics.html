Cycling

Page 3.15.8 · Sports-Specific Knee Biomechanics · biomechanics page

The knee bearing almost no body weight through the same arc eighty times a minute. Low load, enormous repetition, and the setup matters more than the rider.

Cycling

For Patients

The Principle

A movement that is harmless once can cause trouble if it is repeated often enough and always in exactly the same way.

Variety protects joints. A machine that fixes the path removes the variety.

So the thing to adjust is usually the machine rather than the person.

At the Knee

On a bike the body weight is on the saddle, so the knee carries far less than it does when walking.

The knee moves through the same arc roughly eighty times a minute, which is thousands of repetitions an hour.

The pedals and cleats fix where the foot can be, so the knee cannot choose a slightly different path.

Saddle height sets how much the knee bends, and it is the single most important setting. 3.12.3 covers kneecap pressures.

Clinical Relevance

Cycling knee pain is usually a setup problem, and a bike fit is a reasonable first step.

Pain at the front of the knee often means the saddle is too low. Pain at the back often means it is too high.

Cycling is often recommended after knee injury precisely because the loads are low.

No injury rates are given on this page, because no source was available to check them against.

Key Learning Points

  • A harmless movement repeated identically enough can cause trouble.
  • Body weight is on the saddle, so the knee carries far less than in walking.
  • The knee repeats the same arc thousands of times an hour.
  • Cleats fix the foot, so the knee cannot choose a different path.
  • Saddle height is the single most important setting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Is cycling good or bad for knees? The loads are low and the movement is smooth, which is why cycling is often recommended after knee problems. The risk is repetition in a fixed position rather than load.

Q. How do I know if my saddle is the right height? A rough guide is a slight bend in the knee at the bottom of the stroke. Pain at the front suggests too low and pain at the back suggests too high, but a proper fit measures it.

Q. Do cleats cause knee pain? They can. Cleats that hold the foot at an angle the knee does not like give it no way out. Cleats with some rotational float are often more comfortable.

Q. Can I cycle with knee arthritis? Often yes, and it is one of the better options. The low load and controlled movement suit a worn joint, and a clinician can advise on your particular knee.

See a clinician if your knee swells after riding, gives way, locks, or if pain persists after your bike has been properly fitted.

For Medical Students

The Principle

Cycling inverts the usual sporting risk profile: the load per repetition is low and the repetition count is extreme.

That makes the knee's problem one of accumulated cycles in a fixed kinematic path rather than of peak force.

A fixed path is the key feature, because it removes the small variations that spread loading across tissue.

At the Knee

Pedalling cadence of 80 to 90 revolutions per minute gives around five thousand knee flexion cycles an hour per limb.

The knee is largely unloaded by body weight, and the force comes from the quadriceps against pedal resistance.

Patellofemoral load rises as knee flexion increases, so a low saddle raises it throughout the stroke. 3.12.3 covers pressure.

A high saddle extends the knee more and increases reach at the bottom of the stroke, which loads the posterior structures and the hamstring insertion.

Cleat position and rotational float determine whether the tibia can find its own rotation or is held. 3.2.3 covers internal rotation.

What Changes It

Saddle height, which is the dominant variable for knee flexion range.

Saddle fore and aft position, which changes the knee's position over the pedal axle.

Cleat rotation and float, which govern transverse plane freedom.

Gear selection and cadence, since a low cadence in a high gear raises force per repetition.

Training volume and its rate of change.

Clinical Relevance

Take a bike setup history, since the setup is the exposure here.

Anterior knee pain suggests excessive flexion, commonly a low saddle. Posterior pain suggests excessive extension.

Lateral pain suggests iliotibial band friction, often related to cleat position. 3.11.1 covers the band.

Avoid quoting injury rates, which this page does not, because no source was available.

Key Learning Points

  • Cycling inverts the usual profile: low load per repetition, extreme repetition count.
  • Around five thousand flexion cycles an hour per limb at normal cadence.
  • A fixed path removes the small variations that spread loading across tissue.
  • A low saddle raises patellofemoral load throughout the stroke.
  • Cleat float governs whether the tibia can find its own rotation.

MRCS Level

The Principle

Cycling is the clearest case in this chapter where the intervention is engineering rather than biology.

The bike is adjustable in several axes and the rider is not, so the efficient move is to change the machine.

That is a different clinical posture from every other sport here, where the advice is about the athlete.

At the Knee

Patellofemoral joint reaction force in cycling is modest in absolute terms and is applied many thousands of times per session.

Saddle height determines peak knee flexion, and lowering it raises patellofemoral force across the whole stroke. 3.12.2 covers contact areas.

Excessive saddle height produces posterior knee pain and hamstring insertional symptoms, and causes pelvic rocking.

Restricted cleat float can impose a tibial rotation the knee does not choose, which is implicated in medial and lateral pain. 3.2.9 covers coupled motion.

Cycling is used therapeutically after knee injury and arthroplasty precisely because of this profile. 3.14.12 covers that gait.

What Changes It

Saddle height, fore and aft, and cleat position, which are the adjustable axes.

Cadence and gear, which trade force against repetition at a given power.

Crank length, which alters the flexion range at the top of the stroke.

Bilateral asymmetry, which a fixed bilateral machine makes visible.

Volume and its rate of change, as in all repetition dominated sports.

How It Is Measured

Motion capture based bike fitting, which measures joint angles through the stroke and is commercially available. 3.16.7 covers capture.

Instrumented pedals, which give force and its direction through the stroke.

Static fit measurements, which are what most riders receive and are a poorer proxy.

Modelled patellofemoral force from pedal force and kinematics. 3.16.4 covers modelling.

Cycling has better availability of individual biomechanical measurement than any other sport here, because the fitting industry supplies it.

Clinical Relevance

Refer for a dynamic bike fit rather than adjusting by rule of thumb, since the measurement is available in this sport.

Map the site of pain onto the likely setup fault before investigating the knee.

Allow cleat float unless there is a reason to restrict it, since a held rotation has no escape.

Recommend cycling as rehabilitation on the mechanical grounds given here. Section 8 covers the programmes.

Weigh the rider's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations, since a fit changes a position the rider may have held for years.

Key Learning Points

  • The intervention here is engineering rather than biology.
  • Saddle height determines peak flexion and therefore patellofemoral force.
  • Excessive height produces posterior pain and pelvic rocking.
  • Restricted float imposes a rotation the knee did not choose.
  • This sport has better individual biomechanical measurement than any other here.
