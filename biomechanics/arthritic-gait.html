Arthritic Gait

Page 3.14.11 · Gait Analysis · biomechanics page

The adaptation to pain, and the one measurement in this chapter that predicts what the joint will do next.

Pipeline status. Editorial draft. Evidence verification did not run. Not consultant reviewed. Not for clinical use.

Arthritic Gait

Choose a depth

For Medical Students

The Principle

Pain changes movement automatically, before anyone decides to change it.

The change is usually to spend less time loading the sore part and to move it less.

Moving a joint less protects it in the short term and weakens everything around it over time.

At the Knee

A painful knee spends less time in stance on that side, which is what produces a limp. 4.4.2 covers the antalgic pattern.

The knee moves through a smaller range during walking, and the stance bend is reduced or absent. 3.14.2 covers stance.

Walking speed falls, which is one of the earliest and most reliable changes.

Some people with a bow legged arthritic knee show a sideways thrust as weight comes onto it. 3.13.3 covers varus.

6.71 covers osteoarthritis itself.

What Changes It

How much pain there is on the day.

How much the joint has worn and how stiff it has become.

Body weight, which scales the load. 3.5.7 covers obesity.

Muscle strength, which falls as the joint is used less.

Clinical Relevance

Walking speed is a simple measurement that tracks this condition well.

A thrust is visible without equipment and means something. 4.3.2 covers gait observation.

Strengthening and weight management both change the mechanics measurably. Section 8 covers them.

Watching a patient walk into the room is a real part of the assessment.

Key Learning Points

  • Pain changes movement automatically.
  • Stance time shortens on the painful side, which is a limp.
  • Knee excursion falls and the stance bend is reduced or absent.
  • Walking speed falls early and reliably.
  • A varus thrust is visible without equipment and means something.

MRCS Level

The Principle

The arthritic knee's gait contains both a protective response to pain and a mechanical driver of further damage, and the same measurement captures them.

The knee adduction moment is the clearest example: it reflects the deformity and it predicts the progression.

A variable that is simultaneously a consequence and a cause is the hardest kind to interpret and the most useful kind to measure.

At the Knee

Reduced walking speed, reduced stance time on the affected side, reduced sagittal excursion and a flattened stance flexion wave are the consistent findings.

The external knee adduction moment is raised in medial compartment disease and its first peak predicts radiographic progression. 3.13.7 covers the moment.

Varus thrust during loading is an independent predictor of progression and is a clinical observation rather than a laboratory one. 3.13.3 covers varus.

Quadriceps inhibition from pain and effusion flattens the stance flexion wave, and the weakness that follows is both consequence and contributor.

Co-contraction of quadriceps and hamstrings increases, stiffening the joint and raising compressive load. 3.4.3 covers joint reaction forces.

What Changes It

Pain on the day, which changes the pattern more than the radiograph does.

Coronal alignment, which sets the adduction moment arm. 3.13.3 covers it.

Body mass, which scales the moment directly. 3.5.7 covers obesity.

Walking speed, which the patient reduces and which lowers the moment.

Trunk lean towards the affected side, which lowers the moment and is trainable. 3.13.7 covers it.

How It Is Measured

Three dimensional gait analysis with force plates for the adduction moment, which is the predictive measurement and is not clinically available. 3.16.7 covers it.

Walking speed over a fixed distance, which is free, reliable and underused.

Observation for varus thrust, which is free and predicts independently. 4.3.2 covers it.

Instrumented walkway for stance time symmetry, which is deployable.

The gap between the predictive measurement and the available one is wider here than anywhere else in this chapter.

Clinical Relevance

Measure walking speed, which tracks the condition and costs nothing.

Look for varus thrust during walking, since it predicts progression independently of the radiograph.

Expect pain to change the gait more than the radiographic grade does.

Offer trunk lean retraining and weight reduction as mechanical interventions that lower the moment. Section 8 covers them.

Key Learning Points

  • The gait contains both a protective response and a mechanical driver of further damage.
  • The adduction moment reflects the deformity and predicts the progression.
  • Varus thrust predicts independently and is a free clinical observation.
  • Quadriceps inhibition flattens the stance flexion wave; the weakness is consequence and contributor.
  • The gap between the predictive measurement and the available one is widest here.

FRCS Level

At the Knee

This is the page where gait analysis has the strongest clinical claim in the whole chapter, and the claim is still not acted on.

The first peak adduction moment predicts medial compartment progression better than the static alignment angle that surgery is planned from. 3.13.1 covers the angle.

Interventions that lower it, including weight reduction, lateral wedges, gait retraining and trunk lean, have measurable mechanical effects and modest symptomatic ones.

Osteotomy lowers it too, by shortening the moment arm, and the reduction is smaller than the degrees of correction imply. 3.13.9 covers the operation.

Conventional teaching plans from radiographs because that is what exists in clinic, and the honest statement is that the better predictor is in a laboratory nobody has access to. 6.154 covers the controversies.

What Changes It

Alignment, mass and speed, which are the three terms in the moment.

Pain and its treatment, since analgesia alters gait and therefore alters the measurement.

Disease stage, because a late knee has lost the excursion the measurement depends on.

Muscle co-contraction, which raises compressive load independently of the moment.

Footwear and walking aids, which are the cheapest mechanical interventions available.

How It Is Measured

Laboratory gait analysis, which is the reference and is a research resource.

Walking speed and stance symmetry, which are clinically available proxies for overall function rather than for the moment.

Wearable estimation of the adduction moment, which is the development most likely to close the gap. 3.16.8 covers wearables.

Radiographic alignment, which is what decisions actually rest on and predicts less well.

No clinical pathway measures the quantity that best predicts the outcome being prevented, which is this chapter's recurring finding.

Clinical Relevance

Use walking speed and thrust observation as the clinically available signals, and say what they are proxies for.

Offer weight reduction and gait modification as mechanical treatments rather than as lifestyle advice. Section 8 covers them.

State that the predictive variable is not the one being measured when explaining a correction. 3.13.7 covers that argument.

Expect a walking aid in the opposite hand to lower the moment immediately, which is unusual among interventions here.

Take the patient's age, physiology, comorbidities, knee demand, psychological state and their own ideas, concerns and expectations into the decision, since the whole non operative side of this depends on what the patient will actually do.

Key Learning Points

  • Gait analysis has its strongest clinical claim here and the claim is still not acted on.
  • The first peak adduction moment predicts progression better than the static angle.
  • Interventions that lower it have measurable mechanical and modest symptomatic effects.
  • Osteotomy lowers it by less than its degrees of correction imply.
  • No clinical pathway measures the quantity that best predicts the outcome being prevented.
